# Further Reading

Every source this book relies on is listed in the Notes, chapter by chapter. That list is long, and much of it is research papers, court records and technical reports. You don't need to read any of it to use AI well.

This page is shorter. It picks out the sources worth your time if you want to go a little further, mostly free, plain-language pages from Australian government agencies, plus a few reports the book leans on. Each entry says which chapter it connects to.

Web pages move and AI products change quickly. Everything here was checked while the book was being written, in 2026. If a link no longer works, search for the title. The companion website keeps an up-to-date version of this list.

## Understanding What AI Can and Can't Do

- OpenAI Help Center, "Does ChatGPT Tell the Truth?" <https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8313428-does-chatgpt-tell-the-truth>. A short page from a chatbot's own maker explaining why it can sound sure of itself and still be wrong. It goes with Chapter 5.
- Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence, *The 2026 AI Index Report*, <https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report>. A large yearly report on where AI actually stands: what it can do, who is building it, and how it is being used. Dip into the chapter summaries rather than reading it cover to cover. It goes with Chapters 9 and 11.

## Using AI at Home and at Work

- ASIC Moneysmart, "AI and money decisions," <https://moneysmart.gov.au/online-safety/ai-and-money-decisions>. Practical advice on using AI for money questions: share only what the task needs, and check what it tells you before you act. It goes with Chapter 6.
- OpenAI Help Center, "Data controls in ChatGPT," <https://help.openai.com/en/articles/7730893-data-controls-in-chatgpt>, and Google, "Gemini Apps Privacy Hub," <https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961>. Where to find the settings that decide what happens to your conversations. Other AI tools have similar pages. They go with Chapters 9 and 13.
- Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, "Australian Privacy Principles quick reference," <https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-quick-reference>. A one-page summary of the rules Australian organisations follow with your personal information. Useful before you paste anything personal into an AI tool at work. It goes with Chapter 13.

## Jobs, Skills and Hype

- Jobs and Skills Australia, *Australia's AI Transition: Jobs, Skills and the Future of Work*, <https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study>. The Australian Government's study of how generative AI is likely to affect work, looking at tasks rather than whole job titles. It goes with Chapters 10 and 12.
- Joel Becker, Nate Rush, Beth Barnes and David Rein, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity," METR (10 July 2025), <https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/>. A careful study in which experienced programmers felt faster with AI but, on the measured tasks, were slower. It's a narrow, early-2025 result, not a verdict on AI in general, and it's a good reminder to measure rather than assume. It goes with Chapters 3, 7, 10 and 11.
- Center for AI Safety, "Statement on AI Risk" (30 May 2023), <https://safe.ai/work/statement-on-ai-extinction-risk>. The one-sentence statement on extinction risk discussed in Chapter 11, with its list of signatories. Worth reading in full so you know exactly what was claimed, and what wasn't.

## Staying Safe

- Scamwatch, "How scammers use technology and AI," <https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/stop-check-protect/help-to-spot-and-avoid-scams/how-scammers-use-technology-and-ai>, and "Why anyone can be a victim of a scam," <https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/stop-check-protect/help-to-spot-and-avoid-scams/why-anyone-can-be-a-victim-of-a-scam>. The national scam service on voice cloning, deepfakes and fake messages, and why falling for a scam is not a sign of stupidity. It goes with Chapter 9.
- Australian Signals Directorate, "Social engineering," <https://www.cyber.gov.au/threats/types-threats/social-engineering>. How criminals manipulate people rather than computers, and the habits that protect you. It goes with Chapter 9.
- eSafety Commissioner, "Image-based abuse," <https://www.esafety.gov.au/key-topics/image-based-abuse>. What to do if intimate images of you, real or fake, are shared without consent, including how to report them. It goes with Chapter 9.
- eSafety Commissioner, *Talking to machines: Children's experiences with AI assistants and companions* (2026), <https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/talking-to-machines-childrens-experiences-with-ai-assistants-and-companions>. Australian research on how children use AI chatbots and companions. Especially useful for parents and teachers. It goes with Chapters 6, 9, 13 and 14.

## Creativity and Copyright

- Attorney-General's Department, "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence Reference Group (CAIRG)," <https://www.ag.gov.au/rights-and-protections/copyright/copyright-and-artificial-intelligence-reference-group-cairg>. Where the Australian Government's work on AI and copyright is published. The law here is still moving. It goes with Chapters 8 and 9.
- U.S. Copyright Office, *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability* (29 January 2025), <https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf>. A readable official report on when work made with AI can be protected by copyright in the United States. It goes with Chapter 8.

## Where AI Goes Next

- Department of Industry, Science and Resources, *National AI Plan* (2 December 2025), <https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/national-ai-plan>. The Australian Government's plan for AI, including the Australian AI Safety Institute. It goes with Chapters 9, 11 and 14.

## The Companion Website
