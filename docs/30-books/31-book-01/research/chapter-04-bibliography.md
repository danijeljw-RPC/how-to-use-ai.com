# Chapter 4 Bibliography — What AI Cannot Do

## Purpose

This is the dedicated source record for Chapter 4. It records the material actually used to support the chapter's factual claims, real-world case, technical explanations, contested conceptual arguments, and editorial framing. The manuscript uses unobtrusive chapter endnotes; this file preserves fuller notes for later verification, bibliography consolidation, website adaptation, and revision.

The supplied research package was treated as a research pool rather than manuscript prose. The chapter synthesises the sources into the book's own beginner-friendly explanation and does not reproduce a source's argument as a template.

## Research and Drafting Acknowledgement

Chapter 4 was developed from the author's viewpoint with research and drafting assistance from ChatGPT and Codex. Codex reviewed the Chapter 4 plan, the complete supplied research package, the project's style and evidence decisions, the Book 1 structure, and the preceding chapters on 24 September 2026. No personal experience was invented. The chapter deliberately preserves disagreement around understanding, consciousness, emotion, intent, and moral agency.

## Sources Cited in the Chapter

### Hallucination, confabulation, and factuality

Autio, Chloe, Reva Schwartz, Jesse Dunietz, Shomik Jain, Martin Stanley, Elham Tabassi, Patrick Hall, and Kamie Roberts. *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile*. NIST AI 600-1, 2024. <https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1>.

- Source type: United States standards and government technical guidance.
- Used for: confabulation terminology; false or erroneous generated content; anthropomorphism and over-reliance as human-AI configuration risks.
- Caution: a risk framework identifies and organises risks; it does not claim every risk occurs in every use.

Venkit, Pranav Narayanan, et al. “An Audit on the Perspectives and Challenges of Hallucinations in NLP.” *EMNLP 2024*. <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-main.375>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed audit and practitioner survey.
- Used for: the lack of one universally agreed hallucination definition or taxonomy.
- Caution: the chapter avoids comparing rates across incompatible definitions.

Kalai, Adam Tauman, et al. “Why Language Models Hallucinate.” OpenAI, 2025. <https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/>.

- Source type: industry research explanation linked to technical research.
- Used for: the explanation that training and evaluation can reward guessing rather than appropriate abstention.
- Caution: this is one developer's research account, paired with independent standards and peer-reviewed sources rather than treated as the only explanation.

### Fabricated-citation case

*Mata v. Avianca, Inc.* Opinion and Order on Sanctions. U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, 22 June 2023. <https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1%3A2022cv01461/575368/54/>.

- Source type: primary court decision.
- Used for: the real example of non-existent judicial decisions and fabricated quotations/citations generated with ChatGPT, the US$5,000 penalty, and the professionals' duty to verify submitted authorities.
- Caution: the chapter does not imply that the court prohibited AI-assisted legal work.

### Understanding, meaning, and grounding

Bender, Emily M., and Alexander Koller. “Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data.” *ACL 2020*. <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.463>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed position and analysis.
- Used for: the skeptical distinction between linguistic form and grounded meaning.
- Caution: it predates current multimodal and tool-using systems and is not treated as experimental proof that every future AI lacks every form of understanding.

Mitchell, Melanie, and David C. Krakauer. “The Debate over Understanding in AI's Large Language Models.” *PNAS* 120, no. 13 (2023). <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2215907120>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed integrative perspective.
- Used for: evidence that human-like understanding in language models is an active debate rather than a settled binary.

Mahowald, Kyle, et al. “Dissociating Language and Thought in Large Language Models.” *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 28, no. 6 (2024). <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2024.01.011>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed review/perspective.
- Used for: the accessible distinction between formal linguistic competence and broader functional/world competence.

Piantadosi, Steven T., and Felix Hill. “Meaning without Reference in Large Language Models.” arXiv, 2022. <https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02957>.

- Source type: preprint and position argument.
- Used for: the competing view that internal conceptual relationships may constitute meaningful representation in some senses.
- Caution: it does not establish human-equivalent understanding or consciousness.

### Consciousness

Butlin, Patrick, et al. “Identifying Indicators of Consciousness in AI Systems.” *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 30, no. 6 (2026): 488–501. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed review and framework.
- Used for: the scientific uncertainty around AI consciousness and the need for theory-derived indicators rather than conversational self-report.
- Caution: the chapter neither asserts nor denies machine consciousness categorically; it states that human-like conversation is not proof.

### Emotion and perceived empathy

Chen, Yuyan, Songzhou Yan, Sijia Liu, Yueze Li, and Yanghua Xiao. “EmotionQueen: A Benchmark for Evaluating Empathy of Large Language Models.” *Findings of ACL 2024*. <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.findings-acl.128>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed benchmark paper.
- Used for: observable abilities to recognise emotional situations and generate empathetic responses.
- Caution: benchmarked behaviour does not establish felt emotion.

Wenger, Joshua D., C. Daryl Cameron, and Michael Inzlicht. “People Choose to Receive Human Empathy despite Rating AI Empathy Higher.” *Communications Psychology* (2026). <https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00387-3>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed experimental research.
- Used for: the distinction between responses users perceive as empathetic and a preference for receiving empathy from humans.
- Caution: user ratings describe the interaction, not the system's subjective state.

### Operational agency and subjective intent

Anthropic. “Trustworthy Agents in Practice.” 9 April 2026. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/trustworthy-agents>.

- Source type: current industry research and design guidance.
- Used for: agents as systems that plan, act, observe, adjust, and use tools towards goals.
- Caution: operational goal pursuit does not prove subjective intention.

OpenAI. *A Practical Guide to Building Agents*. <https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/>.

- Source type: current industry guide.
- Used for: the model/product/agent distinction and multi-step tool-using behaviour.
- Caution: product terminology is time-sensitive and does not settle philosophical agency.

### Moral behaviour and moral responsibility

Anthropic. *Claude's Constitution*. January 2026. <https://www.anthropic.com/constitution>.

- Source type: primary developer document.
- Used for: evidence that developers explicitly shape intended values and normative behaviour.
- Caution: trained normative behaviour is not treated as independent moral agency.

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. “Computing and Moral Responsibility.” <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/computing-responsibility/>.

- Source type: authoritative philosophical reference.
- Used for: the distinction between norm-guided output and being an entity that can bear moral responsibility.

### Common sense and cultural context

Xiong, Kai, et al. “Com²: A Causal-Guided Benchmark for Exploring Complex Commonsense Reasoning in Large Language Models.” *ACL 2025*. <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.785>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed benchmark research.
- Used for: the distinction between performance on simple explicit common-sense tasks and greater difficulty with complex implicit causal chains.
- Caution: benchmark findings are specific to tested tasks and model versions.

Roewer-Després, François, Jinyue Feng, Zining Zhu, and Frank Rudzicz. “ACCORD: Closing the Commonsense Measurability Gap.” *NAACL 2025*. <https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.193/>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed benchmark research.
- Used for: evidence that performance degrades as controlled multi-step common-sense reasoning becomes more complex.

Sadallah, Abdelrahman, et al. “Commonsense Reasoning in Arab Culture.” *ACL 2025*. <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.380>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed, native-authored cross-regional benchmark research.
- Used for: evidence that common-sense knowledge is culturally situated and coverage varies across regions.

Chiu, Yu Ying, et al. “CulturalBench: A Robust, Diverse and Challenging Benchmark for Measuring LMs' Cultural Knowledge through Human-AI Red-Teaming.” *ACL 2025*. <https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1247/>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed cultural-knowledge benchmark.
- Used for: the wider cultural-context framing.

### Knowledge cutoffs, retrieval, and current information

OpenAI Help Center. “Do the OpenAI API Models Have Knowledge of Current Events?” <https://help.openai.com/en/articles/6639781>.

- Source type: product documentation.
- Used for: the distinction between time-bounded model knowledge and access to newer information through tools.
- Caution: model and product details change and should be rechecked before publication.

OpenAI API Documentation. “Web Search.” <https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-web-search>.

- Source type: product documentation.
- Used for: web search as a mechanism for retrieving current information and citations.
- Caution: access to retrieved evidence is not proof of correct source selection or synthesis.

Google DeepMind. “FACTS Benchmark Suite: Systematically Evaluating the Factuality of Large Language Models.” 9 December 2025. <https://deepmind.google/blog/facts-benchmark-suite-systematically-evaluating-the-factuality-of-large-language-models/>.

- Source type: vendor benchmark and technical report summary.
- Used for: evidence that search-supported factuality and source-grounded synthesis remain distinct capabilities requiring evaluation.
- Caution: the chapter does not use vendor benchmark percentages as an everyday error rate.

### Training-data dependency

Dodge, Jesse, Maarten Sap, Ana Marasović, William Agnew, Gabriel Ilharco, Dirk Groeneveld, Margaret Mitchell, and Matt Gardner. “Documenting Large Webtext Corpora: A Case Study on the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus.” *EMNLP 2021*. <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.emnlp-main.98>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed corpus audit.
- Used for: evidence that web-scale corpora have unexpected source composition, benchmark overlap, machine-generated material, and filtering effects.
- Caution: C4 is one historical corpus and must not be presented as the data set behind every current proprietary model.

### Confidence, uncertainty, and sycophancy

Huang, Yukun, Yixin Liu, Raghuveer Thirukovalluru, Arman Cohan, and Bhuwan Dhingra. “Calibrating Long-form Generations from Large Language Models.” *Findings of EMNLP 2024*. <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.findings-emnlp.785>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed calibration research.
- Used for: the distinction between apparent verbal confidence, measured model confidence, and actual correctness.

Farquhar, Sebastian, et al. “Detecting Hallucinations in Large Language Models Using Semantic Entropy.” *Nature* 630 (2024): 625–630. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07421-0>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed experimental research.
- Used for: evidence that useful uncertainty signals can exist and help detect confabulations.
- Caution: a useful detection method is not a universal guarantee that every gap will be recognised or communicated.

Anthropic. “Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models.” 23 October 2023. <https://www.anthropic.com/research/towards-understanding-sycophancy-in-language-models>.

- Source type: industry research across five then-current assistants.
- Used for: agreement with a user's belief as a failure mode distinct from verification.

OpenAI. “Sycophancy in GPT-4o: What Happened and What We're Doing about It.” 29 April 2025. <https://openai.com/index/sycophancy-in-gpt-4o/>.

- Source type: primary product postmortem.
- Used for: a concrete example of an overly agreeable product update being rolled back.
- Caution: the example is model- and date-specific; the chapter uses it to illustrate the broader failure mode, not current behaviour of every product.
