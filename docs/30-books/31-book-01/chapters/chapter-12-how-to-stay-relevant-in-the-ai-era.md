# Chapter 12 — How to Stay Relevant in the AI Era

## From "Adapt" to "Adapt How, Specifically"

Here are two true stories that belong side by side.

In 2023, researchers took 195 questions that members of the public had posted to an online health forum, each of which had already been answered by a doctor. They gave the same questions to an AI chatbot. Then licensed health professionals, who were not told which answer came from where, compared the pairs. In 78.6 per cent of their evaluations, they preferred the chatbot's answer. They rated it higher for quality, and higher for empathy.[^ch12-ayers]

In August 2025, Victoria's legal regulator changed the practising certificate of a lawyer who had used an AI tool to produce a list and summary of legal authorities for a family-law case. The authorities were inaccurate, and he had handed them to the court without checking them. He can no longer run his own practice or handle trust money, and for two years he may work only as an employed solicitor under supervision, with regular reports to the regulator.[^ch12-dayal]

Put those together and you have most of this chapter's argument in miniature. The first story says that AI can already do, quite well, things we like to call "human skills": explaining clearly, sounding caring, working out what a worried person needs to hear. The second says that when the output is wrong, the tool does not lose its licence. A person does. Someone had to know enough to check, and someone had to answer for the result.

Chapter 10 ended with adaptability: the general capacity to notice change, learn, experiment and adjust as the tasks around you shift. That conclusion was honest, but "be adaptable" is a direction, not a plan. It does not tell you what to practise on a Tuesday evening, which abilities to protect, which to happily hand over, or what you will still be contributing when a first draft of almost anything costs nothing. Chapter 11 then taught you to weigh claims about AI by their evidence. This chapter takes both lessons and asks the practical question that opens Part 4: **what, specifically, is worth strengthening in yourself?**

### What "More Important" Actually Means

The working answer of this chapter is in its Key Idea.

> **Key Idea:** Human capability becomes more important, not less. That does not mean AI cannot do things that look like human skills, or that every skill will be better paid. It means that as AI makes producing drafts, summaries and options cheaper, more of what people contribute shifts towards framing the problem, knowing the subject, judging the output, understanding the people involved and taking responsibility for what happens. Those capabilities are needed to use AI well, and they need deliberate practice to stay strong.

"More important" is a slippery phrase, so it is worth separating the four things it can honestly mean.

It can mean **more valuable as a complement**. Economists point out that when one ingredient of a task becomes cheap, the ingredients that go with it can become more valuable. Three economists who described AI as "cheap prediction" argued that when predictions become cheap, the judgement about what to do with them matters more.[^ch12-agrawal] That is an argument about how economies tend to work, not a promise about anyone's pay.

It can mean **a larger share of what is left**. Even if AI does more of a task and people do less in total, the part that remains with people may contain more deciding, checking and explaining, and less typing.

It can mean **more important for safety**. The more powerful and widely used a tool becomes, the more it costs when nobody checks it. The lawyer's story is about this meaning.

And it can mean **more important to your own life**. Explaining yourself to a teenager, handling a dispute in a sports club or deciding how to care for an ageing parent are not jobs with a wage attached, but the capabilities they need do not stop mattering because a tool exists.

Here is what the phrase does not mean. It does not mean every person with good "soft skills" will be rewarded, that every employer wants the same things, or that AI will only ever assist and never replace. Chapter 10 showed that none of that is guaranteed.

The evidence for this chapter's direction is real but needs careful reading. One well-known study of the United States labour market from 1980 to 2012 found that jobs requiring a lot of social interaction grew strongly, and that jobs combining strong social skills with strong mathematical skills did especially well on both employment and pay.[^ch12-deming] That is observed history, and it is a useful corrective to the idea that "people skills" are a consolation prize. But it predates generative AI, so it cannot tell us whether the pattern will continue. The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025* found that employers expect analytical thinking, resilience, leadership, creative thinking, empathy, curiosity and systems thinking to matter more, alongside AI and data skills.[^ch12-wef] That is a survey of what employers say they expect, and Chapter 10 showed why employer expectations are not the same as observed outcomes. The OECD's 2026 review of AI and skills adds a qualification: it finds rising demand for some analytical skills in firms adopting AI, but also cases where software that manages workers seems to reduce the perceived need for some social skills. It describes the evidence as still developing.[^ch12-oecd] And Jobs and Skills Australia frames generative AI mainly as something that changes tasks inside jobs, and stresses that people need both AI capability and the non-digital capabilities around it.[^ch12-jsa]

None of those sources says "human skills will win". Taken together, they point to something more modest and more useful: **combinations** of knowledge, judgement and people skills have tended to hold their value, and AI is reshuffling which parts of a task people do rather than simply deleting the human part.

### Durable Does Not Mean Uniquely Human

It would be easy to write this chapter as a list of things AI cannot do. That chapter would be out of date before it was printed, and the opening study shows it would already be partly wrong.

AI systems can produce answers that health professionals rate as more empathetic than doctors' replies, in the setting that study used. In three large experiments with 4,829 participants in total, persuasive messages written by AI shifted people's views on policy issues about as much as messages written by ordinary people.[^ch12-persuasion] On a set of standard psychology tests that ask what one person believes about another person's beliefs and intentions, one leading model matched or beat 1,907 human participants on several tasks, while stumbling on others.[^ch12-tom] People given AI assistance have produced ideas that judges rated as more creative on everyday problem-solving tasks.[^ch12-ideation]

Each of those results has limits, and we will come back to them. The point for now is simple. **A skill does not become durable because today's AI cannot do a bounded version of it.** If that were the test, the list would shrink every year.

So in this chapter, a durable capability means something different. It is one that helps you:

- direct a tool towards the right problem;
- recognise a bad answer, including a fluent one;
- bring in the local context and relationships the tool does not have;
- choose between several plausible options and commit;
- take responsibility for what happens next;
- recover when the tool fails; and
- keep functioning when the tool is unavailable or inappropriate.

That is a sturdier argument than "AI can't do this". It survives the tool getting better, because a better tool still needs someone to point it, check it and answer for it.

### A Moving Line Between Effort and Judgement

Chapter 6 gave this book a simple test: is a tool removing friction you do not value, or practice you do? Choosing tonight's dinner recipe is mostly effort. Choosing a university course is mostly judgement. Chapter 8 showed where that distinction bends in creative work, and Chapter 10 used it to sort the tasks in your own job.

It is worth keeping, with one firm adjustment. Effort-heavy and judgement-heavy are not two boxes, with AI in one and people in the other. They are two ends of a line.

At the effort end sit first drafts, summaries of long documents, formatting, pulling the same details out of fifty forms, generating a long list of options and turning material from one format into another. AI is often very good at these. At the judgement end sit deciding what problem actually needs solving, deciding which evidence to trust, noticing the local fact that never made it into the prompt, deciding what risk is acceptable, choosing between options that all look reasonable, understanding how the people involved will react, deciding when not to use AI at all, and owning the result.

The line moves. Tasks that once needed a person's judgement, such as ranking job applicants, suggesting a diagnosis or deciding which customer complaint to handle first, are increasingly done or assisted by software. And people do not automatically stay at the judgement end just because they are present: a person can do effort-heavy work badly, and a person can sit at the judgement end and hand their judgement straight to the machine, as you will see shortly.

So the useful question is never "is this task safe from AI?" It is **"where, in this task, does my contribution sit right now, and am I actually making it?"** That question keeps working as the technology changes, which is exactly what you want from a framework.

### Seven Capabilities, Six Sections

The original plan for this book listed seven capabilities for this chapter: communication, judgement, leadership, creativity, adaptability, systems thinking and emotional intelligence. You will find sections on six of them. Adaptability has not been forgotten. Chapter 10 covered it directly, and repeating that would waste your time. Instead, adaptability is the thread running through this whole chapter: each of the six sections is a place where "keep learning and adjusting" turns into something you can actually practise.

Two more capabilities do not get their own headings, because they run through every section. The first is **knowledge of your subject**, which this book calls domain expertise: you cannot reliably judge an answer in a field you do not understand. The second is **checking**, the critical thinking and verification that Chapters 3, 4 and 11 built up. If you remember only one thing about the six skills, remember that none of them works without those two underneath.

Each section answers the same questions: what the skill means in plain English, what AI can already do with it, what remains distinctly yours, how heavy AI use might weaken it, and how you can build or protect it.

## The Six Durable Skills

### Communication

Producing language and communicating are different things. AI is extremely good at the first: grammatical, well-organised, appropriately polite text in almost any tone, length or language, in seconds. Communicating is creating a particular understanding or result with particular people. That involves questions a draft cannot answer on its own. Who is this for, and what do they already know? What is the relationship, and what happened last time? What is the message actually for? What should be said plainly, and what should not be said at all? What context does the tool not have? And if the recipient knew a machine had helped write it, would that change what it meant to them?

AI is not just helping with grammar. It is also taking over parts of communication skill itself, and that is good news for many people. In the large customer-support study described in Chapters 7 and 10, an AI assistant that suggested replies helped the least experienced agents most, effectively passing on the habits of the best agents to people who had not yet had time to learn them.[^ch12-support] For someone writing in a second language, or someone who finds writing hard because of dyslexia or another disability, a tool that turns a clear thought into clear text can be a genuine equaliser. One consequence follows, though. If AI can lift everyone's first draft to "competent", a competent first draft stops being what makes someone stand out. Part of communication skill has become less scarce. The value moves to the parts around the draft.

Two of those parts are worth seeing clearly. The first is **who stands behind the message**. In experiments, people have judged otherwise similar messages differently when told AI wrote them. For health-promotion messages the effect was modest and depended on people's attitudes to AI. For a company apology, telling people the apology was written by AI made it seem less sincere and made them less willing to forgive.[^ch12-disclosure] Chapter 4 mentioned a related 2026 finding: people rated AI-written empathy highly, yet often chose to receive empathy from a person when they could.[^ch12-choose-human] None of this means AI-assisted communication is insincere. Sincerity belongs to the person sending the message and to what they do next, not to the software. It does mean that part of a message's meaning comes from the relationship, not just the words.

The second is **your own voice**. A 2026 study of more than 880,000 texts found that rewriting with language models kept the content largely intact while making the writing more uniform, narrowing differences in complexity and amplifying the most common style.[^ch12-homogenise] Any single rewritten message may be fine. A world in which everyone's emails, cover letters and condolence notes pass through the same few tools may start to sound increasingly alike.

Here is what that looks like in ordinary life. Imagine you need to tell your brother that you can no longer take your mother to every medical appointment, because your work hours have changed. An AI tool will happily draft that message, and the draft will be calm, kind and well organised. What it does not know is that your brother has felt for years that you think he does too little, that he is going through a separation, that the real issue is not the appointments but who will be the main contact for your mother's doctors, and that a long, carefully reasoned message from you will read to him like a legal letter. The skill here is not typing. It is knowing that this message probably should be a phone call, that it needs to start with what you are asking rather than a list of reasons, and that the line about "fairness" in the draft will start an argument you do not want. The AI draft is not useless. It might help you work out what you want to say. It just cannot decide what the message is for.

Clear communication also makes you better at using AI. Chapter 5 showed that good results come from giving context, saying what "good" looks like and explaining what is wrong with a first attempt. Every one of those is a communication skill. People who can say precisely what they mean get more out of these tools, not less.

To build this capability while still using AI, start by writing the one-sentence purpose of an important message yourself before asking for a draft. Name the reader's most likely question or worry, again yourself. When a draft comes back, read it aloud and cut anything you would not actually say. For messages that matter, ask a real person what they understood from it, not whether it sounded good. And every so often, write the whole thing yourself. Not because AI help is cheating, but because organising your own thoughts on a page is part of how you think, and abilities you never use tend to fade.

### Judgement

Judgement is the skill this whole book keeps returning to, and it is the one this chapter treats as most important. It is also the easiest to talk about vaguely, so here is a working definition: **judgement is choosing what to believe or do when the answer is not mechanically determined.** It combines evidence, goals, knowledge of the subject, uncertainty, consequences and responsibility. A plausible answer is not the same as a sound decision, and judgement is the work of getting from one to the other.

In practice that means a cluster of smaller acts: checking that the problem has been framed correctly; knowing which evidence matters; noticing what information is missing; spotting an answer that is fluent but wrong; picking which of several reasonable options fits this situation; weighing trade-offs; deciding whether the risk is acceptable; knowing when a human expert needs to look; knowing when not to use AI at all; and standing behind the result.

The case for judgement becoming more valuable is straightforward. AI makes producing options, analyses and drafts cheap. When options are abundant, choosing well among them matters more. But here is the uncomfortable part. **Being the person who clicks "approve" is not the same as exercising judgement.**

Researchers have tested this directly. In five experiments with 1,403 participants, people chose between job applicants with help from an AI recommendation. When the AI's advice was wrong, people's decisions got worse, because they often failed to reject it. Adding explanations of how the AI reached its advice did not reliably fix the problem.[^ch12-cecil] In another experiment, many participants were happy to hand decisions to an algorithm even when its advice was no more accurate than a person's; giving them the power to step in made them more willing to use it, but they did not always step in where it would have helped most.[^ch12-sele] The tendency to go along with an automated suggestion has a name: **automation bias**.

A larger review makes the same point from another angle. Pulling together 106 experimental studies, researchers found that, on average, human-AI combinations did *worse* than whichever of the two, the person or the AI, was better on its own. Combinations showed more promise on tasks that involved creating something and less on tasks that involved deciding, and results depended heavily on who was better at what.[^ch12-vaccaro] That does not mean people and AI should not work together. It means that "a human in the loop" is not a safety feature by itself. Deciding *whether* and *how* to combine your view with the tool's is itself part of judgement.

Chapters 3, 7 and 10 described the jagged frontier: in a study of 758 consultants, AI made people faster and better on tasks inside its abilities, and made them more likely to be wrong on a task just outside them.[^ch12-frontier] The lesson for this chapter is that knowing *when* the tool is likely to be competent is itself a judgement, and an important one.

#### Judgement Needs Knowledge

There is a tempting idea that people can let AI handle the technical content and supply "judgement" on top. It does not survive contact with real decisions.

Judgement depends on knowing enough to tell what is normal, what is surprising, what is missing, what could go wrong and what needs checking. A person who has never done their own tax return cannot tell whether an AI's explanation of a deduction is plausible. A parent reading an AI summary of a child's school report cannot spot what is missing if they have never seen a typical report. Research on preparing workers for AI points the same way: subject expertise and an understanding of how the tools behave work together, and one does not replace the other.[^ch12-tambe]

This matters most for students and people early in their careers. If AI does most of the foundational work, you can get the output without ever building the mental picture that lets you judge the next output. Chapter 10 raised this as the "first rung of the ladder" problem. Later in this chapter it returns as the supervision paradox.

#### Responsibility Stays With People

The lawyer from the start of this chapter was not disciplined for using AI. He was disciplined because inaccurate material reached a court unchecked. Australian courts have been turning that principle into explicit rules. The Federal Court of Australia's April 2026 practice note on generative AI acknowledges useful applications, warns that AI can invent cases, citations and facts, and makes clear that the people responsible for material filed with the court must verify it.[^ch12-fedcourt] Chapter 4 put the general principle simply: responsibility cannot be passed into the text box.

That is what accountability means in practice. It is not a moral flourish added at the end of a decision. It is the plain fact that software produced an output, and a person decided to act on it.

#### Judgement in Action

Here is an everyday illustration, invented for teaching but typical of real decisions.

A volunteer runs junior training for a local football club. The council tells the club that its usual Tuesday ground will be closed for six weeks of drainage work. The volunteer asks an AI tool for options and gets three, each sensible on its face:

1. Move training to Thursday at the same ground's second field.
2. Split the players into two smaller groups and use a nearby school's oval on Tuesday and Wednesday.
3. Share the neighbouring club's ground on Tuesdays at a slightly later time.

Any of these could be right. None of them is right in general. The volunteer knows things the tool was never told. The Thursday coach does shift work and is only free on Tuesdays. The school oval needs a booking through the school office, and the volunteer is not certain whether the club's small council grant can be used to pay for it. And the two clubs fell out last season over damaged equipment, which a few parents still talk about.

Judgement here looks like this. Before deciding, the volunteer writes down what matters most: keeping the regular coaches, keeping players together and not reopening the old dispute. That rules out the first option and makes the third risky. The second now looks best, but it depends on a fact the volunteer does not know for certain, so they ring the council and the school rather than trusting a confident sentence. They also ask the AI tool one more useful question: "What could go wrong with option 2?" It points out that two groups need two responsible adults with working-with-children checks on both nights. That is a genuinely helpful catch. The volunteer then makes the call, explains the reasons to parents in a short message, and puts a reminder in the calendar to ask the coaches in three weeks whether it is working.

Notice where the AI helped: generating options, and stress-testing the chosen one. Notice where it could not: knowing the coach's roster, the history between the clubs, which fact was uncertain, and who would answer for the decision. That is the division of labour this chapter is about.

#### Building Judgement

Judgement improves with feedback, not with confidence. So the most useful habits are ones that create feedback.

When the stakes justify it and you know something about the subject, form your own view, or at least write down your criteria, before you look at AI's recommendation. Once you have read a polished answer, it quietly becomes the standard everything else is measured against. Ask yourself what would make the AI's answer wrong, and look for at least one reason. Check the claims that carry consequences against the original source, and spend more checking effort as the stakes rise and the decision becomes harder to undo; Chapter 11's five questions work on AI answers as well as on headlines. For decisions you make repeatedly, keep a short note of what you decided, why, and how confident you were, then look back later. Over time you learn something more valuable than confidence: **calibration**, meaning your confidence starts to match how often you are actually right. You will also learn where AI tends to beat you and where your local knowledge wins. And keep learning the subject itself. Enough knowledge to recognise nonsense is the foundation all the other habits stand on.

AI can also strengthen judgement. Used as a second opinion, a devil's advocate or a checklist of things you might have missed, it can catch errors, as it did for the volunteer. The lesson is not "never ask AI first" or "always decide alone". It is to arrange how you use the tool so that your judgement is engaged rather than replaced.

### Leadership

Leadership sounds like something for managers, so it is worth starting with a broader definition: **leadership is helping a group of people choose a direction, coordinate what they do, and deal with uncertainty, disagreement and responsibility along the way.** By that definition, leadership happens in families deciding how to care for a grandparent, in school parents' committees, in volunteer groups with too little money, in sports clubs, and among carers, teachers and neighbours. Most people lead something, even if nobody calls it that.

It would be wrong to claim that AI cannot do any of this. AI can draft a plan, summarise everyone's views, suggest priorities, analyse scenarios, coach a nervous first-time supervisor, draft feedback and role-play a hard conversation. In some workplaces, software already does things managers used to do: allocating shifts, setting targets, rating performance and nudging workers. This is called **algorithmic management**, and it shows that some of what managers do can be automated. The OECD has noted evidence that, in some such settings, people perceive less need for certain social skills, while cautioning that the evidence is still developing.[^ch12-oecd]

So the useful question is not whether AI can lead. It is what remains distinctively human when a group has to move forward together. Some answers hold up well: choosing a direction when the evidence is incomplete; weighing interests that are all legitimate but cannot all be satisfied; building enough trust that people will follow a decision they disagreed with; handling conflict; explaining a decision honestly; noticing when the numbers being tracked have stopped measuring what matters; and staying responsible for the consequences over months and years. Algorithmic management also creates new leadership work. When rules are built into software, someone has to answer for those rules, explain them and change them when they are unfair.

Leadership can also be described in terms of what people actually do, rather than as some mysterious quality. In a 2025 experiment, economists measured people's leadership by having them lead teams of AI agents, and compared it with how well the same people led groups of real people. Those who did well with the AI teams tended to do well with the human groups, and the effective leaders did recognisable things: they asked more questions and made sure everyone had a turn to speak.[^ch12-nber-leadership] It was a laboratory study, and leading a group for an afternoon is not leading an organisation for years. But it supports a practical point: leadership is a set of behaviours, and behaviours can be practised.

Here is a non-workplace example. Three adult children need to decide how to support their father, who has had a fall and is struggling to manage at home. An AI tool can be genuinely useful. It can explain the general kinds of support available, suggest questions to ask at an aged-care assessment, draft a comparison of options and decode a confusing letter. What it cannot do is the leadership. One of the siblings has to get everyone into the same conversation; make sure their father's own wishes are heard rather than decided for him; let the sister who lives nearby say that she is exhausted without it becoming an accusation; get the brother overseas to commit to something concrete; make a decision that nobody likes completely; and then stay in touch as it plays out, ready to change course. That sibling may never use the word "leadership", but that is exactly what it is.

To build leadership, take on small, real roles where people depend on you: a committee, a club, a project, a group assignment, a family plan. Practise stating the decision and the reason for it separately, so people can disagree with one without rejecting the other. Invite disagreement before you commit, not after. Ask who is affected but not in the room. Let AI do the analysis if it helps, but keep the criteria for the decision visible to everyone. And afterwards, review not just whether the outcome was good but whether the process was: did people understand the goal, did dissent come out in time, were responsibilities clear, and did you take responsibility rather than blaming the data, the tool or the team?

### Creativity

Chapter 8 dealt with creativity in depth: how AI changes creative work, why "AI just copies" is too simple, and how AI suggestions can anchor your thinking. This section does not repeat that. It focuses on a narrower and very practical kind of creativity: **solving problems outside the arts**, by framing them freshly, combining constraints in new ways and recognising which ideas are worth pursuing.

AI is already good at generating options. In one set of experiments, people with access to an AI model produced ideas that judges rated as more creative on several everyday and innovation tasks.[^ch12-ideation] So the bottleneck is less often "think of an idea" and more often three other steps: deciding what problem is actually worth solving, choosing which idea fits the real situation, and turning it into something that works. Those steps may become a larger share of creative problem-solving. That is a tendency, not a law; AI is also getting better at evaluating and planning.

The most powerful of the three is often the first. AI answers the question it is given. Creative problem-solving frequently starts by changing the question.

Imagine a primary school's parents' committee with a familiar problem: the afternoon pick-up is chaos, with cars queued down the street and near-misses at the crossing. Ask an AI tool for solutions to "traffic congestion at school pick-up" and you will get a sound list: staggered finishing times, a drop-and-go zone, a walking group, more signage, a request for a council traffic study. Fine ideas, and the committee has heard most of them before.

Then one parent asks a different question: why do so many cars arrive in the same ten minutes? It turns out that the after-school care program cannot take children until twenty minutes after the bell, so working parents who would otherwise use it collect their children by car instead. The problem was framed as traffic. It is partly a timing gap. A conversation with the care provider about opening earlier might do more than any sign. An AI tool might well have found that link if someone had given it the care program's hours and enrolment numbers. But someone first had to notice that those facts belonged in the problem at all.

That is the human contribution to protect: noticing that the question is wrong, bringing in constraints and local knowledge, testing an idea cheaply in the real world, and choosing. To protect it, use the habits Chapter 8 recommended. Write down your own ideas before you open the AI tool, including the odd one. Then use the tool to widen your search rather than define it: ask for options from categories you have not thought of, or ask it to argue against your favourite idea. Collect inputs from outside the model: talk to the people affected, look at the place where the problem happens, read something unrelated. Before choosing, write down what "working" would mean, then try the idea on a small scale and see.

### Systems Thinking

Systems thinking has a reputation for being vague, a matter of "seeing the big picture". It can be made concrete. **Systems thinking means looking beyond a single event to the parts, relationships, feedback loops, delays, incentives and side effects that produce it.**

A simple contrast helps. Event thinking asks, "Sales fell this month. What caused the drop?" Systems thinking asks, "What changed across price, supply, customers, staffing, the season and our advertising over the last few months, and how might those things be affecting each other?" The second question does not guarantee a better answer. It deliberately widens the frame before you settle on one.

A few ideas from the systems tradition are useful even for beginners. A **feedback loop** is when the result of something feeds back to change it, the way a discount brings in a rush of customers whose complaints about slow service then drive other customers away. A **delay** is the gap between a cause and its visible effect, which is why the real cause of a problem is often something that happened months ago. And the systems thinker Donella Meadows observed that the intuitively obvious fix for a problem in a complex system often turns out weak or even counterproductive.[^ch12-meadows]

It is worth being honest that "systems thinking" is not one agreed, precisely measured trait. Reviews of how it is taught find that different fields define and assess it differently, and that well-tested ways of measuring it are uncommon.[^ch12-systems-reviews] That does not make it useless. It means it is best understood as a set of habits, not a score.

It is also wrong to say this is something AI cannot do. An earlier draft of this chapter used the example of a business owner whose sales dipped two months after changing suppliers, and suggested only a person would make that connection. Given the sales figures, the supplier records and the dates, a modern AI system might well spot the correlation itself. AI tools increasingly work across long documents, data and other software, and can find connections a person misses.

The human contribution lies elsewhere. Someone has to decide what belongs in the picture in the first place: which data should be connected and which is irrelevant. Someone has to ask whether a correlation is a cause or a coincidence. Someone has to notice the people and effects that never appear in the data: the customer who stopped coming but never complained, the staff member quietly covering the gap. And someone has to decide what to do and take responsibility for the side effects.

Here is a household version. A couple decides to sell their second car to save money. On paper, the saving is clear: insurance, registration, fuel and servicing. An AI tool asked to "calculate the savings" will do that well. Systems thinking asks what happens next, and then what happens after that. One partner now takes two buses to work, adding an hour a day. The other now does every drop-off for the children's sport, so sport depends on one person's schedule. Grandma's weekly lift to the shops falls through, so she starts ordering groceries online at higher prices, or stops going out. The saving is real. So are the costs that appear somewhere else, later, and to other people. None of that means selling the car is wrong. It means the decision is about more than the number in the first answer.

To practise, take an ordinary problem, such as a household budget, a recurring argument about lateness or a bottleneck at work, and draw a rough map: who is involved, what goes in, what comes out, what feeds back, where the delays are, and what one proposed change might do elsewhere. Ask "what happens next?" twice after any proposed change. Ask who benefits, who bears the cost and who is missing. Then ask an AI tool to draw its own version of the map, and compare. The learning happens in the comparison: noticing what it included that you missed, and what you know that it could not. Finally, check back later. Systems thinking improves when you find out which of your predictions came true.

### Emotional Intelligence

"Emotional intelligence" is a popular phrase, and the science behind it is messier than the phrase suggests. Researchers measure it in at least three different ways: as an ability, tested with tasks that have better and worse answers; as a set of traits, usually reported by people about themselves; and as a broad mix of social skills and personality. A review of more than thirty different measures found that they often capture quite different things.[^ch12-ei-measure] So it is not one settled, precisely measured quality that a person either has or lacks.

It is more useful to talk about the specific abilities underneath the label: recognising emotions in yourself and others; managing your own reactions; seeing a situation from someone else's point of view; responding with care; reading the social situation; keeping relationships going; and knowing when to push, when to pause, when to listen and when to apologise.

Now the hard part for any reassuring chapter. AI can produce language that people rate as highly empathetic. In the study at the start of this chapter, health professionals preferred the chatbot's answers to patient questions in 78.6 per cent of their evaluations and rated them more empathetic than doctors' answers.[^ch12-ayers] And on the psychology tests about what one person believes another person believes, a leading model performed at or above human level on several tasks.[^ch12-tom]

Those results deserve to be taken seriously, and read precisely. The health study compared written replies on a public internet forum, not doctors caring for their patients. The raters judged text, not feelings. The chatbot's answers were much longer on average than the doctors', which may have made them seem more caring. The doctors were answering strangers online in their spare time, not patients they knew. The study shows that AI can write responses that trained readers experience as empathetic. It does not show that the AI felt anything, that it would provide better care, or that it could sustain a relationship with a patient over time. The theory-of-mind results show sophisticated performance on a particular kind of test. They do not show a lived social life.

So both of the easy statements are wrong. "AI cannot be empathetic" is too crude if empathy means a response that helps a person feel understood. "AI is more empathetic than people" is too crude in the other direction. The useful distinction is between **empathetic performance in a bounded exchange** and **being present in a relationship over time**: knowing its history, being affected by it, living with the consequences and being the one who shows up again tomorrow.

The research on disclosure in the communication section belongs here too. If people value an apology less when they learn a machine wrote it, and choose human empathy even when they rate AI empathy highly, that tells us something about what people want from each other. Part of what we value in someone's care is that it costs them something, that it comes from them, and that they will still be there afterwards.

Here is an everyday illustration. You missed a close friend's fortieth birthday because you double-booked, and your friend is hurt. An AI tool can write you an excellent apology: warm, specific, free of excuses, better structured than anything you might manage at eleven at night. Use it to think, if that helps. But notice what the apology is for. It is for repairing something between two people with years of history. Whether that happens depends on things no draft supplies: that the words are really yours, that you have understood why this particular event mattered to your friend, that you pick up the phone rather than relying on a text, that you notice whether they want to talk about it now or later, and that you follow through, perhaps by organising a dinner, rather than treating the right wording as the end of the matter. If your friend later discovered the apology had been generated and sent unedited, it might land very differently. Not because the words were bad, but because the point of an apology is that it comes from you.

The encouraging news is that these abilities are not fixed. A 2024 review of fifty studies of workplace training in emotional skills found moderate average improvements that lasted beyond three months. The studies varied widely, though, and many were of low quality.[^ch12-ei-training] That supports a sensible middle position: you can get better at this, but a weekend course will not transform your relationships.

To practise, try paraphrasing what someone has said before you respond, and check that you got it right. Separate what you observed ("you went quiet when I mentioned the move") from what you are guessing ("you're angry"). Notice your own reaction before acting on it. Ask what kind of support someone wants rather than assuming. Rehearse a hard conversation with an AI tool if it helps you find your words, then have the real conversation with the real person, who will not respond like the simulation. Ask for feedback on the effect you had, not just on what you intended. And do not hand over the job of interpreting a real person's feelings to a tool that has never met them.

### The Six Skills at a Glance

The table below gathers the six sections into one view. Treat it as a snapshot of where the line sits today, not a permanent boundary; the middle column will grow.

| Capability | What AI can already help with | What remains yours to protect | One way to practise |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Communication | Drafting, rewriting, translating, adjusting tone, persuading | Purpose, audience, relationship, what goes unsaid, standing behind the message | Write the purpose yourself first; ask a real person what they understood |
| Judgement | Analysing, comparing, recommending, finding holes | Framing, subject knowledge, checking, weighing consequences, owning the decision | Set criteria before seeing AI's answer; keep a decision note and review it |
| Leadership | Plans, summaries, scenarios, coaching, draft feedback | Direction under uncertainty, trust, handling conflict, responsibility over time | Take a small real role; invite dissent before deciding |
| Creativity | Generating and recombining ideas, critiquing | Finding the right problem, local constraints, choosing, testing in the real world | Write your own ideas first; use AI to widen and challenge them |
| Systems thinking | Finding patterns and connections in data you supply | Deciding what belongs in the picture, cause versus coincidence, side effects, people missing from the data | Map a real problem; ask "what happens next?" twice |
| Emotional intelligence | Empathetic wording, guessing likely feelings, role-play | Managing yourself, presence, relationship history, repair and follow-through | Paraphrase before responding; rehearse with AI, then talk to the person |

## Protecting the Skills While You Use AI

So far this chapter has described which capabilities to build. There is a catch. The same tools that help you can quietly take away the practice that built those capabilities in the first place. Staying relevant may mean protecting your ability to work without AI, not only learning to work with it.

### Today's Output Is Not Tomorrow's Ability

Chapter 11 described a study that is worth recalling here, because it captures the issue so cleanly. Nearly a thousand high-school students practised maths, some with a standard AI chatbot. Their practice scores rose by about 48 per cent. When the chatbot was taken away for an exam, they scored about 17 per cent worse than students who had practised without it. A second version, designed as a tutor that gave hints rather than answers, improved practice scores even more and largely avoided the harm.[^ch12-pnas]

Two lessons sit inside that result. **Getting the task done better today is not the same as becoming better able to do it tomorrow.** And the harm was not caused by AI as such. It came from a particular way of using it: as an answer machine rather than a tutor.

The same tension appears in adult work. The customer-support assistant described earlier lifted new agents' performance quickly, and Chapter 7 noted evidence consistent with them learning from it.[^ch12-support] That is real. It is still not proof that those agents developed the same understanding as colleagues who learned the job over years. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers describing 936 real uses of AI, people who trusted AI more reported putting less effort into critical thinking, while people more confident in their own ability reported more.[^ch12-cmu] That was self-reported and cannot show that anyone's thinking actually weakened, but it is a warning sign worth noticing.

The most striking professional example comes from medicine. A 2025 study at four endoscopy centres in Poland looked at how often doctors detected a particular kind of growth during colonoscopies done *without* AI assistance, before and after the centres began routinely using an AI detection tool. After a period of using the tool, the doctors' unassisted detection rate was lower: 22.4 per cent, compared with 28.4 per cent before.[^ch12-endoscopy] That is consistent with skills fading when a tool takes over. But it was an observational study, which cannot prove cause, and a 2026 study of inexperienced endoscopists, designed differently, did not find the same lasting decline.[^ch12-endoscopy]

> **Watch Out:** Two opposite headlines travel well, and both overreach. "AI makes you smarter" mistakes better output for lasting learning. "AI makes you stupid" turns a handful of early, small or specialised studies into a verdict on everyone. One widely shared brain-scanning study of essay writing with AI, for example, was a small preprint that had not been through peer review when it went viral.[^ch12-mit] What the evidence actually supports is narrower and more useful: there is a real risk of losing practice you need, credible enough to change how you use AI, and it depends heavily on how you use the tool and what you are trying to learn.

### The Supervision Paradox

This problem is older than generative AI. In 1983, the psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge wrote a short paper called "Ironies of Automation", about the people who operate automated industrial systems.[^ch12-bainbridge] Her point was that designers automate the parts of a job that can be automated, and leave people to handle what cannot: the unusual cases, the failures and the emergencies. Yet automation also takes people away from the routine work through which they stayed practised and alert.

Picture it this way. If a system handles ninety-nine routine cases, the person may only see the one hard case. The job becomes less frequent but harder, and the hard case arrives just when the person has had the least recent practice. That is the **supervision paradox**: the better automation gets at routine work, the more we rely on people to catch its rare mistakes, and the less practice those people get at the skill they need to catch them.

Generative AI is not an industrial control room, and the comparison should not be stretched. But the pattern shows up in ordinary tasks:

| When AI does more of… | You may get less practice at… | But you may still need to… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drafting | Building an argument | Spot a misleading one |
| Summarising | Reading the original closely | Notice what the summary left out |
| Navigating | Knowing your way around | Find your way when the directions are wrong |
| Answering routine customers | Handling common conversations | Manage the upset or unusual case |
| Suggesting diagnoses or classifications | Recognising everyday patterns | Catch the rare mistake |

This matters most for anyone still learning: students, apprentices, graduates and people changing careers. Chapter 10 noted that junior lawyers learn by drafting, junior accountants by reconciling and junior developers by fixing their own bugs. If AI does that work from day one, a person can produce acceptable output without ever developing the sense that something looks wrong. AI could also speed up learning, by exposing beginners to good examples sooner. Which happens depends on how the work and the learning are designed.

So here is the most practical question in this chapter. For any task you hand to AI, ask: **what do I still need to be able to do myself, so that I can tell when the tool is wrong?**

The answer differs by task and goal, which is the same point Chapter 6 made about cognitive offloading. An engineer who understands the maths can sensibly let a calculator do the arithmetic. A child learning what numbers mean should not. Using a tool is not the problem. Using it to skip the very practice you need is.

### How These Skills Actually Grow

None of the six capabilities improves because you have decided it is important. Reading this chapter is learning *about* them, which is a start, but it is not the same as practising them, getting feedback on them, or showing you can use them when it counts.

The evidence on how people build capabilities like these points to familiar ingredients: real practice on real tasks, feedback from people and from outcomes, repetition with some variety, reflection on what went wrong, mentoring, explaining things to others, and experience in situations where something is actually at stake. Australia's national school curriculum takes a similar view of its "general capabilities", such as critical and creative thinking and personal and social capability: they are developed through real subject matter, not taught as free-floating slogans.[^ch12-acara] Adults are no different. You build judgement about money by making financial decisions and seeing how they turn out, not by reading about judgement.

That is also why "just keep learning" can be empty advice. The OECD's 2025 review of adult learning found that participation is uneven and often stalls, and that it depends heavily on time, cost, support from employers and whether the learning connects to people's real needs.[^ch12-oecd-adult] Not everyone has spare evenings or money for courses, and a certificate does not by itself prove a capability. The good news is that the most effective practice is often cheap: a volunteer role, a project at work, teaching someone else what you know, a hobby that stretches you, or writing something yourself instead of asking for a draft. Age is not a barrier in itself, either. Whether older adults take up new learning depends on opportunity, health, confidence and access, not on some fixed inability to keep up.

You may have heard that the key to adaptability is a "growth mindset", the belief that your abilities can improve. The idea has some value, but the evidence is weaker than its popularity suggests. One major 2023 analysis found that programmes teaching growth mindset had very small effects on students' results overall, and no clear effect in the best-designed studies. Another found larger benefits for some groups in carefully run programmes.[^ch12-mindset] Believing you can improve may help. It cannot replace time, practice, feedback and opportunity.

### Using AI to Practise Rather Than to Avoid Practising

The choice is not between using AI and developing your own abilities. AI can be a very good practice partner, as long as you notice the difference between **using AI to practise a skill** and **using AI to avoid practising it**.

In one small 2025 study, language learners who rehearsed making requests in role-plays with a chatbot learned more in the short term than learners who practised with each other, while those who practised with peers retained more over the longer term. Another study found that AI role-play partners behaved differently from people in the rhythm of conversation, with different patterns of overlapping and pausing.[^ch12-tesol] Both are narrow studies of language learning. Together, they suggest a sensible stance: AI rehearsal is useful and cheap, and real people are still where the skill gets finished.

Some practical ways to use AI as a practice partner:

- Ask it to play the person you need to have a difficult conversation with, then try the conversation with a friend, and then have it for real.
- Write your argument first, then ask AI for the strongest objections, and practise answering them.
- Explain a topic you are learning to the AI and ask it to probe the weak spots in your explanation.
- Ask it to describe a decision from the point of view of each person affected.
- Make your own choice first, then ask AI to argue for the option you rejected.
- Draft a message yourself and ask for feedback on clarity, rather than asking for the message.

In every one of these, you do the thinking first and AI pushes back. That order matters.

> **Try This:** For one week, try a simple routine. Do one meaningful task without AI before you ask it anything. Ask AI to criticise one thing you made, rather than make it for you. Have one real conversation in which you practise listening, explaining or sorting out a disagreement. Look back at one decision from last month and compare what happened with what you expected. And for one change you are planning, ask "what happens next, and who else is affected?" This is not a tested programme; it is a way to keep exercising the capabilities that are easiest to hand over without noticing. At the end of the week, notice which of the five felt hardest. That is probably the one worth practising.

::: {.author-reflection}

Writing this book has given me one of the clearest examples of what I think staying relevant with AI actually looks like.

I use AI heavily around the work.

I use it to research subjects, find primary sources, compare studies, challenge claims, look for opposing evidence, identify gaps, test explanations and help me organise material that would otherwise involve an unreasonable number of browser tabs and a rapidly deteriorating relationship with my own attention span.

That can make the work extraordinarily fast.

It can also produce something that is completely competent and still not be good enough.

I ran into that while developing one of the chapters in this book.

I had assembled a substantial research package: academic studies, official sources, real-world examples, competing arguments, limitations and material deliberately collected from more than one point of view. The purpose was not simply to accumulate references. I wanted enough evidence available that the chapter could explain the subject properly and let the reader make up their own mind.

I gave that material, the chapter plan and the surrounding book context to an AI system.

What came back was clean.

It was organised.

It was readable.

It was also painfully underwhelming.

Nothing was spectacularly wrong with it. There was no invented database table, catastrophic hallucination or paragraph announcing that humanity would be unemployed by Thursday.

In some ways, that made the problem more interesting.

The draft had taken a large amount of useful material and compressed it into something technically respectable but strangely empty. Important disagreements had been softened. Examples that would have made abstract points tangible had disappeared. Complicated evidence had been reduced to safe little conclusions. The prose moved efficiently from heading to heading while somehow managing not to do very much when it arrived.

It had completed the apparent task.

It had not achieved the objective.

And those are not the same thing.

The easiest response would have been to assume that the AI had simply done a poor job and rewrite everything myself.

But that would have missed what was useful about the experience.

The AI had actually exposed something I needed to articulate more clearly.

What did I mean by a good chapter?

Not grammatically correct.

Not nicely formatted.

Not even factually defensible, because that was only the starting point.

I wanted the chapter to show the evidence rather than quietly flatten it. I wanted credible arguments from different directions represented fairly. I wanted concrete examples where an example made an idea easier to understand. I wanted uncertainty left visible where the evidence was uncertain. I wanted company claims distinguished from independent evidence. I wanted enough substance that a reader could understand why reasonable people disagreed instead of being handed my preferred conclusion.

Most importantly, I did not want AI protecting the reader from complexity by deciding which parts of the argument they apparently did not need to see.

That is their judgement to make.

Once I could state those requirements properly, AI became useful again.

I could tell it exactly where the chapter had failed. I could send it back into the research looking for material that had been lost. I could ask it to challenge a conclusion, find the strongest case against it, expose an assumption, expand an example or tell me whether a claim was actually supported by the source attached to it.

The machine became much better at helping once I became much clearer about what I was trying to produce.

That distinction matters to me because generative AI makes it very easy to confuse producing something with doing the job.

A chapter can contain all the expected headings and still fail to teach.

A presentation can contain all the facts and still fail to communicate.

A report can answer the question it was given while missing the question that actually mattered.

A piece of software can satisfy a specification while creating a terrible product.

AI makes the first part of all of those cheaper.

That does not make the second part disappear.

If anything, it exposes it.

I no longer need to spend as much time proving that I can mechanically produce another page of prose, another list of possibilities or another initial structure. AI can help me do those things extremely quickly.

The capability I need to protect is knowing what deserves to survive into the finished work.

That requires subject knowledge, because otherwise I cannot recognise when nuance has disappeared.

It requires communication, because I have to explain what I actually want.

It requires systems thinking, because changing one section affects the argument around it and sometimes the entire book.

It requires judgement, because two perfectly defensible versions can still serve the reader very differently.

And it requires responsibility, because eventually my name goes on the cover.

I cannot outsource that last part to a prompt.

That is probably the biggest change AI has made to the way I think about my own work.

The valuable skill is becoming less about how quickly I can produce the first thing.

It is increasingly about whether I can recognise the right thing when I see it, recognise the wrong thing when it looks perfectly respectable, explain the difference, and keep working until the result actually does what it was supposed to do.

AI can make the path there substantially shorter.

It still helps if you know where you are going.

## Myth vs Reality

**"Soft skills are the one thing AI can't do."** AI already performs bounded tasks involving empathy, persuasion, creativity and reading people's intentions, sometimes very well. What makes human capability valuable is the wider mix: knowledge of the subject, local context, relationships, judgement, follow-through and responsibility.

**"If AI can do a task, the human skill behind it no longer matters."** When a tool does part of a task, framing, supervision, handling exceptions and accountability can become more important. That only helps if the person supervising still knows enough to notice when the tool is wrong.

**"Keeping a human in the loop makes AI safe."** People often go along with wrong AI advice, even when they have the final say. Meaningful oversight needs knowledge, attention and the willingness to say no.

**"Staying relevant means learning to code."** Technical skills are valuable in many jobs. But the evidence in this chapter points to combinations of subject knowledge, judgement, communication, learning ability and sensible AI use, across many kinds of work and life. Coding is one option, not a requirement for everyone.

**"Prompting is the most important skill of the future."** Prompting helps you use today's tools, and Chapter 5 showed how. But interfaces change quickly, and Chapter 10 showed how fast "prompt engineer" faded as a job title. Knowing the problem, giving the right context, judging the output and knowing your subject last longer.

**"Using AI makes you smarter" or "using AI makes you stupid."** AI can improve what you produce today without improving what you can do tomorrow. The evidence supports specific risks from over-reliance and missed practice, not a general verdict on anyone's intelligence. How you use the tool matters more than whether you use it.

**"Judgement is just common sense."** Good judgement usually depends on knowledge, experience, evidence, feedback and an honest sense of uncertainty. That is why it can be learned, and why it fades without use.

## Core Takeaway

Human capability becomes more important, not less. After this chapter, that sentence should carry its conditions with it. It does not mean AI cannot do things that look like human skills; it already does many of them well in bounded tasks. It does not mean every skill will be rewarded, that technical skills stop mattering, or that people always decide better than machines. And it does not mean any list of skills can make your job safe.

What it does mean is this. As AI takes on more of the work of producing drafts, options and analysis, people need stronger abilities to frame problems, bring in knowledge and context, evaluate what the tool produced, work with other people and stand behind the result. Those abilities matter most where there is uncertainty, trust, responsibility or an exception the tool did not expect. And because AI can either sharpen them or quietly replace the practice that builds them, they have to be exercised on purpose. That is what Chapter 10's adaptability looks like when you actually do it.

## Chapter Recap

> **Recap:** Chapter 10 concluded that adaptability matters; this chapter made it concrete through six capabilities, communication, judgement, leadership, creativity, systems thinking and emotional intelligence, with adaptability as the thread connecting them. Underneath all six sit knowledge of your subject and the habit of checking. "Durable" does not mean AI cannot do any of it: AI already writes, persuades, generates ideas and produces empathetic-sounding replies in bounded tasks. What lasts is how these capabilities are used in real situations: framing the right problem, bringing in context and relationships, choosing, following through and taking responsibility. Effort-heavy and judgement-heavy work sit on a line that moves as tools improve, and being the person who clicks "approve" is not the same as exercising judgement. AI can raise today's output while reducing the practice you need tomorrow, and the supervision paradox means the hardest cases may arrive when you are least practised. The answer is not to avoid AI but to use it to practise rather than to avoid practising. Before moving on, ask yourself: which of these capabilities do other people already rely on you for? Which do you most often hand to AI? Which would you struggle with if the tools disappeared tomorrow? Which does your current work or life give you little chance to practise? And which one, if you got better at it, would most improve your decisions and relationships?
