# Chapter 13 — Building Your Personal AI Toolkit

## Categories, Not a Catalogue

Sooner or later, almost everyone who gets curious about AI asks the same question: "Which one should I get?"

It is a fair question, and this chapter will not answer it with a product name. That is not coyness. It is because the honest answer is usually another question: *get it for what?*

Picture two people on the same evening. The first scrolls past an advertisement for an "AI Photo Genius" app that promises to remove anything from any picture, free for three days. She taps install. The app asks for her card details to start the trial, and then for access to her contacts. The second person wants to remove a stranger from the background of a holiday photo. She opens the photo app already on her phone, finds an editing option she had never noticed, and is done in a minute without creating an account, paying anything or handing over her address book.

Both of them wanted the same job done. Only one of them started with the job.

Chapter 12 was about what to strengthen in yourself as AI becomes more capable: judgement, communication, the habit of checking and the knowledge that lets you notice when a fluent answer is wrong. This chapter turns outward, to the tools around you. If you want to use more AI yourself, how do you choose sensibly without depending on somebody else's list of "the best AI tools"? The capabilities from Chapter 12 turn out to be exactly what make that choice go well.

### Why This Book Will Not Give You a List

A printed list of recommended AI products would age faster than almost anything else in this book, and not only because new products appear. Existing ones change underneath you. One of the best-known AI assistants changed its name entirely less than a year after launch, while the service carried on. Providers regularly retire the specific models inside their products, sometimes within months, so advice like "use model X for writing" can expire while the product name stays the same. Even privacy instructions move: one major provider's help page on privacy settings now carries a warning that it describes an older version of the app.[^ch13-churn]

Names, models, settings, prices, bundles and terms all change, at different speeds. A book that told you "go to this menu and switch off this toggle" could be accurate on the day it was printed and misleading a year later.

That creates a real problem for a beginner, though, and it would be evasive to ignore it. "Learn the principles" is cold comfort if you want something to try tonight. Chapter 6 deliberately used one familiar assistant as a teaching doorway and gave a dated starting map of a few others, precisely so you would have somewhere to begin. This chapter does something different: it teaches you how to judge any entry on a map like that, including the ones that did not exist when this book was written. The two jobs are split on purpose. The printed book carries the durable part: the categories, the questions, the evaluation method and the safety principles. The companion website carries the volatile part: current products in each category, current prices and tiers, walkthroughs of today's privacy and permission settings, current age requirements and current scam warnings, each marked with the date it was last checked.

### What "Literacy" Means Here

This chapter's central idea is short.

> **Key Idea:** AI literacy matters more than loyalty to any specific tool.

That sentence is easy to misread, so it is worth being precise about both halves.

Researchers who study AI literacy describe it as the set of competencies that let a person critically evaluate AI, use it effectively and understand where it fits, rather than familiarity with one interface.[^ch13-literacy] For this chapter, the useful distinction is between two kinds of knowing. **Tool familiarity** is knowing where the buttons are and how one service behaves today. **AI literacy** is knowing what kind of capability a task needs, what can go wrong, what information you are handing over, how to test what comes back, how to tell a provider's claim from independent evidence, and when not to use AI at all. The first dates quickly. The second transfers to the next tool, and the one after that.

Now the other half: loyalty. It would be easy to hear "literacy over loyalty" as "never get attached" or "switch whenever something newer appears". Neither is the point. Staying with a tool can be entirely rational. Your chat history is there. It may remember your preferences. Your files, projects and saved instructions live inside it. You may have set it up to work with your screen reader, your workplace may have approved it, or it may come bundled with a subscription you already need. Every switch costs time, habits and sometimes data.

So the target is not disloyalty. It is **examined** loyalty: knowing why you use a tool, being able to judge whether it still earns its place, and being capable of leaving when it no longer fits. Constantly chasing the newest release is not literacy. It is just a different way of letting someone else decide for you.

The word "toolkit" in this chapter's title is helpful up to a point: you keep tools for jobs you actually have, and one multipurpose tool can cover several. But a hammer does not read your email, change its terms, remember what you told it or charge your card every month. Many AI tools are closer to a phone plan than a hammer: an ongoing relationship with a company whose features, prices and rules can change, and which you may one day need to leave cleanly.

## Start With the Task, Not the App

The most useful habit in this chapter is also the simplest. Before you think about products, describe the job.

"I want to use more AI" is not a job. "I want a summary and an action list from the hour-long recording of our monthly club committee meeting" is. So is "I want to dictate emails because typing hurts my wrists", or "I want to understand the error message my spreadsheet keeps showing". A clear job gives you something to test against, which makes every later decision easier.

It helps to pin down five things:

- **What goes in?** A recording, a photo, a document, a question, a spreadsheet?
- **What should come out?** A summary, a draft, an edited image, an explanation, a transcript?
- **How often?** Once, occasionally, or every week?
- **What happens if it goes wrong?** Mildly annoying, costly, embarrassing, private, unsafe?
- **What do you already have?** Could something you use now do this?

From there, a sensible sequence follows. Identify the task. Check whether a tool or feature you already have can do it. If not, work out which kind of capability you need. Try it on a low-stakes example you can judge. Look at quality, privacy, permissions and cost. Only then consider paying, or moving more important work into it.

If not, look at one new tool using the first-pass check. Either way, test it with a low-stakes example you can judge, then decide whether it is worth keeping, running a deeper check before paying, sharing sensitive data or connecting accounts. Sometimes the answer at the end of that sequence is "none". The capability you need is already inside your phone, your email or your word processor, or AI turns out not to be the right approach at all. That is a perfectly good outcome. Australia's privacy regulator makes a similar point to organisations: AI should not be adopted simply because it is available, and its use should be justified by a need and checked as it goes.[^ch13-oaic] That guidance is written for businesses rather than households, but the principle travels well.

It is also worth remembering how varied "using AI" already is. In a large 2026 survey of adults in the United States, the most common uses of AI chatbots were searching for information and work tasks, followed by entertainment, making or editing images and videos, and looking up health and fitness information.[^ch13-pew] That is a US snapshot, not a picture of Australia, but the pattern is the point: "AI" covers many different jobs, and the right tool for one may be the wrong tool for another.

## Six Kinds of Help AI Can Give

This book groups AI tools into six categories: chat, image, research, productivity, coding and voice. They are a useful map. They are not six apps you need to install.

Think of them as six **kinds of help**. A single general-purpose assistant today may offer conversation, web search, longer source-based research, image generation and editing, spoken conversation, analysis of files you upload, help with code and connections to other software, all in one place. Meanwhile, a word processor, a phone, a search engine or a photo library may contain AI features without ever calling itself an "AI tool". So one product can cover several categories, and a category can be covered by something you already own.

The categories are still worth learning, because they help you name what you are trying to do and what to check. The table below gives the short version, and the sections that follow fill it in.

| Kind of help | Often good for | The main thing to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chat | Drafting, rewriting, explaining, summarising, brainstorming | Fluent errors, invented details, what you are sharing |
| Image | Generating, editing and describing pictures; mock-ups | Uploaded photos of people, usage rights, credits and limits |
| Research | Finding, comparing and summarising sources | Whether the cited source actually supports the claim |
| Productivity | AI inside email, documents, spreadsheets, meetings and phones | What it can see and do in your accounts |
| Coding | Explaining, writing, testing and fixing code | Plausible but wrong or insecure code; access to files |
| Voice | Dictation, transcription, captions, spoken conversation, reading aloud | Recordings, other people's voices, transcription errors |

### Chat AI

Chat is the kind of help most people picture first: you type or speak a request in ordinary language and get a response you can refine. It is the doorway this book has used since Chapter 3, and it covers a remarkable spread of work: drafting and rewriting, explaining an unfamiliar topic, summarising material you supply, brainstorming options, planning, tutoring-style back-and-forth and, increasingly, answering questions about files you upload.

Its strengths are flexibility and low friction. You do not need special syntax, and you can steer it in conversation, which is why Chapter 5's habits of context, specificity, examples and iteration pay off so quickly here.

Its watch-outs are the ones Chapter 4 explained. A chat tool can be confidently wrong, invent details and citations, and sound equally sure whether it is right or not. Because the conversation feels personal, it is easy to share more than you would type into a form. And because one familiar chat tool can do so much, it is tempting to use it for everything, including jobs where a calculator, a search, a phone call or a specialist tool would serve better. Treat "chat" as a way of interacting, not as a guarantee of knowledge. It can be the right tool. It is not automatically the right tool.

One relative of chat deserves a mention: **AI companions**, services designed for ongoing personal or emotional conversation rather than getting a task done. They come up again in the section on children, because a product whose value depends on you confiding in it deserves a higher privacy bar than its friendly tone suggests.

### Image AI

Image AI is much broader than "make me a birthday card". It can generate a picture from a description, but also edit one you already have: remove an object, extend a background, change the lighting, produce several variations or turn a rough sketch into a cleaner concept. It can produce a mock-up of a garden layout or a shopfront before you pay a designer, and it can work in the other direction too, describing what is in a photo, which matters enormously to people who cannot see it. Video and music generation sit nearby as related creative tools.

Chapter 8 covered the creative and legal questions in depth, so the Chapter 13 question is narrower: *when is this the kind of help I need, and what should I check before I trust it or pay for it?*

A few things are worth checking. Uploading a photo can disclose far more than a text prompt: faces, children, your home, your location, documents in the background, other people who never agreed to be in an AI tool. If you plan to sell or publish the result, the provider's current terms on commercial use matter, and Chapter 8's five meanings of "I own it" apply. Images of real people raise likeness and consent questions. Many image services also run on **credits**, where each generation uses up an allowance, so the real cost depends on how many attempts a job takes. And quality varies by task: a tool that produces beautiful landscapes may still mangle a logo or the text on a sign.

### Research AI

Research tools, or research modes inside general assistants, are designed to search for information, gather sources, compare them, summarise what they say and show links or citations. Some run longer, multi-step searches and produce a report. AI-generated answers inside ordinary search engines sit in the same family.

This is the category most likely to be misunderstood. It is tempting to think: *chat tools make things up, but research tools show their sources, so they must be accurate.* That is not how it works. A citation can point to a real page that does not say what the sentence claims. The source can be weak, out of date, from the wrong country or a company describing itself. The tool can misread a good source, merge two sources into a claim neither makes, or state the finding more strongly than the evidence supports. Chapter 4 showed that a citation can be "citation-shaped" without being real; here the risk is subtler, because the source is usually real.

So the honest distinction is this: **research tools give you more material with which to check the answer. They do not do the checking for you.** That is a genuine advantage. Chapter 6 recommended asking for sources and opening the important ones, and Chapter 11 showed how to trace a claim back to where it came from. A research tool makes both habits faster. It does not make them optional.

### Productivity AI

Productivity AI is AI built into the software you already use: email that drafts replies, documents that summarise themselves, spreadsheets that explain formulas, meeting tools that produce notes and action items, calendars that suggest times, photo libraries you can search by description, and phones that can rewrite a message or summarise notifications.

This category matters because you may never consciously choose an "AI tool" at all. The feature simply appears in an update. That brings real benefits: no new account, no new app, and often no extra cost if it is bundled with something you already pay for. It also has better context. An assistant inside your email can see the thread you are replying to; one inside your files can find the document you half-remember.

That same context is the trade-off. **Better context usually means access to more of your data.** An assistant that can read your inbox, calendar, cloud drive or screen is more useful, and a mistaken permission, a compromised account or a misunderstood instruction can expose more. None of that makes built-in AI bad. It means "it's already included" answers the question of cost and availability, not the question of whether it suits the information you plan to use it with. At work, your organisation's policy decides that, as Chapter 7 explained; at home, you do.

Some of these features can also take actions: sending, booking, filing, editing. Chapter 3's advice applies: let them **prepare, not commit**, at least until you understand how they behave. Chapter 14 looks at where action-taking AI may be heading; for now, the principle is simply that the more a tool can see and do, the more carefully its permissions deserve checking.

### Coding AI

You do not need to be a programmer for this category to matter. Coding AI can explain what a piece of code does, write new code from a description, suggest why something is broken, propose tests and help someone find their way around an unfamiliar project. That is useful to professional developers, but also to someone maintaining a small business website, learning to code, wrestling with a complicated spreadsheet formula or automating a repetitive task.

Chapter 3 described this category as "useful, testable and uneven", and that remains the right summary. Generated code can look perfectly plausible and still be wrong, insecure, or reliant on something that does not exist or carries a licence you did not expect. Code also contains things people forget about: passwords, access keys, customer data, internal addresses. And some newer coding tools do more than suggest; they can read whole projects, run commands and change files. That makes them more capable and raises the stakes of a mistake.

For a beginner, the evaluation points are simple. Start by asking a coding tool to explain a small example rather than giving it your whole project. Never paste in passwords or keys. Test what it produces before relying on it. And at work, check whether the tool is approved for your organisation's code. The deeper questions belong to later books in this series.

### Voice AI

Voice AI is not just a smart speaker that sets timers. It includes dictation that turns speech into text, transcription of recordings, live captions, reading text aloud, translation of speech, and full spoken conversations with an assistant. Chapter 3 described voice as "another way in and out" rather than a separate intelligence, and that framing still holds.

For many people, voice is a convenience: hands busy, ideas quicker to say than type. For others it is the difference between using a tool and not being able to. Someone with limited hand movement, low vision, dyslexia or a hearing impairment may find that dictation, read-aloud or captions turn an inaccessible task into a manageable one. Accessibility is one of the strongest reasons to choose a particular tool, and it comes up again in the evaluation section.

Voice has its own watch-outs. Microphone access is expected for a voice feature, but persistent background listening deserves a reason. Recordings and transcripts can be stored like any other data. Recordings capture other people too, and in meetings and shared spaces their consent matters, as Chapter 7 discussed. And transcription is not perfect: accents, technical terms, names and background noise all cause errors, so a transcript of something important needs checking against what was actually said.

### A Map, Not a Taxonomy

You will meet AI tools that do not fit neatly into any one of the six: AI search, meeting transcription services, translation apps, AI companions, video and music generators, tutoring apps, and assistants that can operate a browser on your behalf. The six categories were never meant to be a scientific classification. Most of these fold in naturally: AI search sits beside research, transcription between voice and productivity, translation across chat, voice and productivity, video and music beside image. Accessibility cuts across almost all of them.

One more distinction, **on-device AI**, is about *where* the AI runs rather than what it does, so it belongs in the privacy section below. The value of the six categories is that they let you ask: *what kind of help do I need here, and what are the usual things to check for that kind of help?* If a tool spans several categories, check it for each one you use.

## Do You Need a New Tool at All?

This chapter does not assume that more tools are better. A personal AI toolkit does not mean collecting subscriptions. It means having a small set of capabilities that solve real tasks, under trade-offs you understand. For many people, that set is one general assistant and whatever is already built into their phone and software. For others, a specialised tool earns its place because one job is frequent or important enough.

Before adding anything, check what you already have. The phone in your pocket, your email service, your office software, your search engine and your photo library may already include the capability. Then evaluate that built-in feature just as you would a new app. "Already included" saves you money and setup; it does not answer the privacy and suitability questions.

If nothing you have fits, a handful of questions will tell you whether a new tool is worth it:

- How often do I actually do this task?
- Is it important enough to justify another account, another password and possibly another bill?
- Would the tool save meaningful time, or improve a result I genuinely care about?
- Is AI even the best approach, or would a template, a conventional app, a professional or a person I know do it better?
- Would I still want this once the free trial or introductory price ends?
- If I come to depend on it, what would leaving cost me later?

That fifth question is a particularly useful filter. Many tools are appealing mainly because they are new or because the first month is cheap. Australian research on subscriptions in general, not AI specifically, keeps finding the same pattern: people forget to cancel trials, keep paying for services they no longer use and find leaving harder than joining.[^ch13-subscriptions] AI tools are commonly sold through exactly those trials and recurring plans.

### Free, Paid and What You Are Actually Buying

Beginners often ask whether they should pay. There is no simple rule in either direction.

Paid tiers can buy real things: higher usage limits, access to more capable models, larger files and longer documents, faster responses, extra features such as image or video generation, connections to other apps, priority when the service is busy and, for business plans, different data terms and administrative controls. Any of those might matter for your task.

What payment does not buy is truth. A paid tool can still produce a confident error, an invented detail or a citation that does not support its claim. Chapter 4's verification habits apply at every price. Payment and privacy are also separate questions: a paid personal plan does not necessarily handle your data differently from a free one, and a free tier is not necessarily selling your information. The old saying "if it's free, you're the product" contains a grain of truth and a lot of oversimplification. A free AI tier might be a limited taster for paid plans, subsidised by business customers, bundled with another product, funded by investors, supported by advertising, or permitted to use your conversations for improvement under certain settings. "Free" tells you the price you pay, not the whole business model. The policy tells you the rest.

The durable question is: **what exactly am I paying for, and does that difference matter for my task?** Pay for a specific capability, limit or protection you can name, not for the vague label "premium". A free tier is often perfectly adequate for occasional, low-stakes use, and nobody needs several paid subscriptions to be AI-literate.

## How to Evaluate a New AI Tool

A long checklist is thorough and easy to ignore. A short one is memorable and easy to outgrow. So this chapter uses two levels: a quick first pass you can run in a few minutes before trying anything, and a deeper check for when the stakes rise.

Chapter 11 gave you five questions for judging *claims* about AI. They still apply here, especially to whatever the tool's marketing says about itself. What follows is about judging the *tool*.

### The First-Pass Check

Before you install, sign up or try something new, run through five short questions.

1. **Need.** What specific job do I want this for, and can something I already have do it?
2. **Maker.** Who actually makes this, and am I sure I am looking at the genuine app or website?
3. **Data.** What am I about to give it: information, files, account access, permissions?
4. **Result.** Can I judge whether what it gives back is any good?
5. **Cost and exit.** What will it cost after the trial or introductory offer, and how do I leave?

If any answer makes you uneasy, that is your cue to stop before giving the tool more money, information or access, not after. Uneasy does not mean "it's a scam". It means you have found a question worth answering first.

### The Deeper Check

Most first experiments with a well-established tool on harmless material need nothing more. But some moments deserve a closer look:

- signing up to a recurring paid plan;
- uploading private or sensitive information;
- connecting your email, files, calendar, contacts or other accounts;
- installing a browser extension;
- relying on the tool for work, money, health, legal matters or anything else with consequences;
- letting it take actions such as sending, buying, booking or deleting; and
- letting a child or teenager use it.

In those moments, the five questions expand.

**Does it really fit the task?** A tool can be impressive without solving your problem. Think about how often you will use it, how good the result needs to be, what its limits are and whether something simpler would do. A meeting-notes tool that produces beautiful summaries is no use if it cannot handle the accents in your team, or if your workplace has not approved it.

**Who is behind it?** You do not need to be an investigator, but a few checks go a long way. In an app store, look at the developer or publisher name and see whether it matches the company the app claims to be. Reach the app through the provider's own website rather than an advertisement or a search result. Look for a privacy policy and terms that name a real legal entity, and contact details that look genuine. Watch for website addresses that are slightly misspelled, or apps that call themselves "official" while being published by someone else. Australia's consumer regulator suggests checks like these for any business you are unsure about, including looking beyond the business's own claims and reviews.[^ch13-genuine] One balancing point matters: an unfamiliar company is not automatically untrustworthy, and a famous one is not automatically careful with your data. The question is whether the company is transparent and traceable, not whether you have heard of it.

**What happens to your data?** This needs more than one question, so it has its own section next.

**Does it ask for access that matches what it does?** Every useful app asks for some permissions; that alone tells you nothing. What matters is whether the access is **proportionate to the feature**. A voice assistant needs the microphone. An image editor may reasonably need your photos. A scheduling assistant may need your calendar. A meeting transcriber needs the microphone and access to the meeting. But why would a simple image generator need your contacts? Why would a writing helper in your browser need permission to "read and change all your data on all websites", including your banking and email pages, when it only needs to work in one text box? Why would a tool want permanent access to all your files when access to one chosen file would do? A mismatch between the feature and the permission is the warning sign. Phones and browsers let you review and change these permissions later, and it is worth doing after a trial, not only at installation.[^ch13-permissions] Browser extensions deserve particular care, because an extension that can read the pages you visit may see everything those pages show.

**Can you rely on the results?** Does it show sources when sources matter, and do they hold up when you open them? Does it say when it is unsure? Does it behave consistently if you ask the same thing twice? The safe-experimentation section below shows how to test this. Remember Chapter 11's ladder: one good result shows that something is *possible*, not that it is *dependable*.

**What will it really cost?** Look past the headline number: the billing period, when a free trial starts charging, the renewal price after any introductory offer, the currency and GST, how credits translate into actual tasks, usage limits and where the cancel button is. The subscription-traps section below shows what to watch for. One nuance: for a consumer app, a price you have to hunt for is a warning sign, but for complex business software, being asked to talk to sales for a quote can be perfectly normal.

**Is it suitable for the person using it?** If a child or teenager will use it, check the minimum age in the current terms, whether it is designed as a task assistant or as a companion, whether it can encourage personal disclosure, and whether it allows in-app purchases. The section on children has more. If you plan to publish or sell what it makes, check its commercial-use terms. And if it is for work, check that it is approved, as Chapter 7 explained.

**Can you actually use it?** Accessibility is part of fit, not an extra. Does the tool work with a screen reader and keyboard navigation? Does it support voice control, captions, larger text, high contrast or your language? Does it offer the specific help you need, such as describing images, reading text aloud or turning speech into text? A tool that is more capable in theory but awkward or impossible for you to operate is not the better tool *for you*. Accessibility features can also be wrong, so notice whether the tool is honest about that. One major provider's own scene-description feature, for example, states that it can make mistakes and must not be used for navigation or to detect obstacles.[^ch13-guided-vision] Chapter 6 showed how much these features can help, and why an important detail may still need checking another way.

**How would you leave?** Before you invest months of history in a tool, find out whether you can export what matters, cancel easily, delete your chats, files and account, and revoke any connections you give it. Ask what you would lose. This question is where practical lock-in shows up, and knowing the answer is what keeps loyalty a choice rather than a trap. It has its own section near the end of the chapter.

## Privacy Is Several Questions, Not One

"Is it private?" sounds like one question. In practice it bundles several different ones, and a tool can give you a reassuring answer to one while saying nothing about the others. Chapter 9 introduced this idea when it looked at what happens to what you share. Here it becomes a practical reading guide for the moment you are deciding whether to trust a tool.

When you look at a tool's privacy settings or policy, these are the questions you are reading for:

| Question | What it actually asks |
| --- | --- |
| Is it stored? | Is my content kept, where and for how long, including after I delete it? |
| Is it used for training? | Can my conversations be used to improve the company's models? Is that on or off by default, and can I change it? |
| Is it remembered? | Does the tool keep a separate memory or profile of facts about me, apart from the chat history? |
| Can people review it? | Can staff or contractors read some conversations, for safety or quality, and how long is reviewed material kept? |
| Who else receives it? | Is it shared with service providers, advertisers or connected apps? Can anyone with a shared link see it? |
| What can I delete or export? | What does "delete" remove, and what does it leave behind? |

Some content may also be processed on your device rather than in the cloud, and some may be kept to meet legal obligations. These are separate questions, and settings that answer one rarely answer the others. You do not need to become a privacy lawyer to use this table. You just need to stop treating a single toggle as the whole story.

### Turning Off Training Is Not Turning Off Storage

Many AI services now let you stop your conversations being used to improve their models. That is a useful control. It answers one question: whether your future conversations can be used for training. It does not, by itself, tell you how long your conversations are kept, whether anyone can review them, what the tool remembers about you or what connected services receive.

Current provider documentation makes the distinction clear. One major provider says that switching off its model-improvement setting stops new conversations being used for training, while saved chats remain in your history. Another documents that a subset of conversations may be reviewed by people, and that reviewed conversations can be kept for up to three years, separately from your ordinary activity, even if you delete that activity.[^ch13-provider-privacy] These are the providers' own descriptions of their own services, not independent audits, and the details change, which is why the companion website, not this book, carries the current settings. The lesson is durable: **"we don't train on your data" is not the same as "we don't keep your data".**

The same care applies to **temporary**, **private** or **incognito** chat modes. They usually reduce something: the chat may not appear in your history, may not update the tool's memory of you and may not be used for training. But at least one major provider's temporary mode keeps a copy for up to 30 days for safety reasons.[^ch13-provider-privacy] Read what a mode actually promises, rather than assuming the name describes it.

### Deleting a Chat Is Not Deleting Everything

An AI account can hold several different kinds of data, and they do not all disappear together: the visible chat history, files you uploaded or saved, a memory or personalisation profile, links you created to share conversations, data from connected apps, and copies kept for safety, backups or legal reasons. Deleting one visible conversation may not touch the others. One provider's documentation, for example, notes that a file saved separately in its library is not deleted just because the chat that used it is.[^ch13-provider-privacy]

Two real events show why this matters.

In 2025, one assistant briefly offered an option to make shared conversations discoverable by search engines. Some people ticked it without appreciating the consequences, and shared conversations began appearing in search results before the option was withdrawn.[^ch13-shared-links] This was about conversations users had chosen to share, not private chats being exposed. But it shows that a "share" link is closer to publishing than to private storage: anyone who gets the link can usually see the conversation and pass it on.

Also in 2025, during a copyright lawsuit in the United States, a major provider was required by court order to preserve some user conversations it would normally have deleted. The provider says ordinary deletion resumed later that year, while a limited set of earlier data remained preserved.[^ch13-legal-hold] The details belong to one case, described here largely in the provider's own words. The general lesson is that deletion policies usually carry exceptions for legal and security obligations.

None of this means deletion is pointless, or that your conversations are being read. It means the safest place to protect truly sensitive information is before you type it, not after. **If information would cause serious harm if it were kept, exposed or misunderstood, decide before you enter it, rather than counting on deleting it later.**

### Personal, Business and On-Device

The same brand can come with different rules. Chapter 7 showed that a personal account, an organisation-managed workspace and a connected workplace application can have different data terms, and that "not used for training" in a business service did not mean "not stored". Providers commonly document different defaults for consumer and business products: one, for example, says its consumer conversations may be used for training if the user allows it, while its commercial products are not used for training by default.[^ch13-provider-privacy] Business and enterprise versions may add retention controls, administrative settings, contractual commitments, security and compliance features, and choices about where data is stored.

That does not make business versions automatically safe, and it does not make consumer versions reckless. It means the account type can change the rules. Do not assume the policy you read for your personal account applies to your work account, or the other way round. For anyone handling customer, client or employee information, that difference matters a great deal.

On-device AI deserves the same precision. Processing a request on your own phone can mean less of your content travels to a company's servers. But many systems mix approaches: some tasks run on the device, more complex ones go to the cloud, and some features pass requests to partner services. One major phone maker, for instance, documents that it handles many requests on the device and sends more demanding ones to its own cloud system, with published security claims about how that cloud system is designed.[^ch13-on-device] Those are the company's own claims, unusually detailed ones, but still not a blanket guarantee for every on-device feature from every company. "On-device" describes where some processing happens. It is a good question to ask, not an answer in itself.

### What Australian Privacy Law Does and Does Not Promise

For Australian readers, a little legal context helps, with the firm caveat that this is general information, not legal advice.

The Privacy Act 1988 and its Australian Privacy Principles set rules for how covered organisations collect, use, disclose, secure and give people access to personal information. Coverage is not universal. Many small businesses with an annual turnover of $3 million or less are exempt, although there are important exceptions, such as many health service providers and businesses that trade in personal information. Where the principles do apply, you generally have rights to ask for access to your personal information and to have it corrected. Australia does not, however, have a general right to have all your data erased of the kind people sometimes assume from European law, so "I can make them delete everything" is not a safe assumption here.[^ch13-app-law]

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner has published guidance for organisations using commercially available AI products. It stresses due diligence before adoption, understanding where data goes, considering accuracy and human oversight, and, as a best-practice starting point, not entering personal information, particularly sensitive information, into publicly available generative AI tools.[^ch13-oaic] That is organisational guidance, not a consumer rule, but it is a sensible default for individuals trying an unfamiliar tool.

Two more points are worth knowing. Many AI services are run from overseas. Australian consumer law still applies to businesses selling to Australians, but sorting out a problem with an overseas company can be harder in practice.[^ch13-acl] And children's privacy is changing: the regulator is developing a Children's Online Privacy Code, which must be finalised and registered by 10 December 2026. At the time of writing it was not final, so check its current status.[^ch13-childrens-code]

## Experimenting Safely

The safest way to try a new tool is the approach this book has recommended since Chapter 3: start with a **low-stakes task you understand well enough to judge**. "Low stakes" means that if the answer is wrong, your input leaks, an unexpected charge appears or an action misfires, the result would be inconvenient rather than harmful.

Good first tests look like this:

- rewriting a non-sensitive paragraph you have already written;
- explaining a topic you already know well, so you can spot errors;
- summarising a public article, then comparing the summary with the article;
- planning a hypothetical meal or trip without your private health or financial details;
- generating an image from fictional material rather than photos of your family;
- asking a coding tool to explain a small example rather than opening your whole project to it;
- transcribing a short recording you made specifically for the test, before trusting it with an important conversation.

A first test is not only about the output. It also shows you how the tool behaves: whether the interface makes sense, what permissions it asks for along the way, what limits appear once you start using it, when it nudges you towards a paid plan, and whether it is worth a closer look.

### What Not to Paste Casually

"Don't share sensitive information" is good advice that is hard to apply, because a beginner may not know what counts. Here is a more concrete boundary for an unfamiliar, general consumer AI tool.

Never use these as test material, or paste them into a chat at all:

- passwords, passcodes, PINs and security-question answers;
- one-time codes, authentication codes and recovery codes;
- access keys, tokens and private keys for software or online services;
- full card numbers, bank login details and similar financial credentials.

Treat these as needing a deliberate decision about the specific service and the purpose, rather than a casual paste:

- images or numbers from passports, driver licences and other identity documents;
- detailed health or medical records;
- tax returns, bank statements and other financial records;
- legal documents, especially in an active dispute;
- confidential work material and customer or client information;
- another person's private information, including messages, photos and documents about them;
- information about children;
- intimate personal details and recordings of faces or voices.

This is not a rule that health, legal or financial information can never go near AI. There are legitimate specialised services, workplace tools with appropriate protections and situations where sharing personal context is the whole point. The point is that you decide, knowing what the service does with it, instead of letting the friendly conversational feel of the tool decide for you. For an unfamiliar tool, the default is caution. Chapter 9's reminder applies too: much of what you might paste is not only your information.

### Test Accuracy, Not Just Fluency

A new tool can impress you simply by sounding good. Fluency is not accuracy, as Chapter 4 explained, so build a little checking into your first tests:

- Ask something you already know the answer to and see whether it gets it right.
- Ask for sources, then open one or two and check that they actually say what the answer claims, and that they are current and relevant to Australia if that matters.
- Compare an important answer with an independent source.
- Notice whether it admits uncertainty or invents a confident detail.
- Try a task representative of why you want the tool, not just an easy demonstration.
- Ask the same question twice and see whether the answers agree.

Then hold onto the limitation. **Doing well on a familiar, easy task does not prove the tool will be reliable on an unfamiliar, hard one.** Your first test is a smoke test, not a certificate. It can reveal obvious problems; it cannot rule out hidden ones.

### Protect the Account

An AI account can become an unusually information-rich account. It may hold months of private conversations, uploaded documents, connections to your email and files, a memory of personal details and your payment information. That makes ordinary account security matter more than it might for a simple app.

The basics are the same as elsewhere: a strong, unique password or a passkey where offered; multi-factor authentication turned on; extra care with the email account you use to sign in, because it can reset everything else; and caution with messages claiming to be from the AI provider about billing problems or account warnings. When you use "Sign in with" another account, read the access screen before approving it, and review those connections later. This is not a cybersecurity course. It is recognising that an account full of your thoughts and files is worth locking properly.

### Tools That Can See and Do More

Some tools do more than answer what you type. They can read your email, documents, calendar, browser tabs, cloud drives or screen, and some can take actions on your behalf. That is where much of their usefulness comes from. It also widens the damage a mistake, a compromised account or a confused instruction can do.

One risk is new enough to name. A tool that reads web pages, documents or emails for you can encounter hidden instructions planted there by someone else, text designed to make the AI do something you did not ask for. This is called **prompt injection**. At least one major provider now offers a restricted mode that switches off some connected features for people at higher risk, which tells you the threat is taken seriously by the people building these systems.[^ch13-lockdown] You do not need to understand the mechanics. You need one principle: **the more a tool can see and do, the more carefully you should check its permissions, its confirmation steps and what happens if it gets something wrong.** Start with read-only access where you can, keep "ask before acting" switched on, and widen access only as you build confidence. Chapter 14 looks further ahead at action-taking AI.

### Children and Teenagers

Chapter 9 mentioned an Australian survey that deserves a closer look here, because it changes how a parent should evaluate a tool. In early 2026, the eSafety Commissioner surveyed 1,950 Australian children aged 10 to 17. Most, 78 per cent, had used an AI assistant, and 8 per cent an AI companion. Among those who had used either, around a third, 32 per cent, said they had shared personal or potentially sensitive information, and one in five, 20 per cent, reported a potentially inappropriate or harmful interaction. More than half used them for personal or social reasons, not only schoolwork.[^ch13-esafety] These are self-reported experiences from one national survey, not proof of lasting harm, and they describe Australian children in 2026, not every child everywhere. They do show that children use tools marketed for tasks in personal ways, and that a product's advertised purpose is not the whole story.

So for a child or teenager, the evaluation adds a few questions:

- What is the minimum age in the service's current terms, and is there a version designed for younger users?
- Is it a task assistant, or a companion built for ongoing personal or emotional conversation? Companion-style designs can invite disclosure and attachment, and eSafety's inquiries to companion providers have raised concerns about age checks and safeguards.[^ch13-esafety]
- What should your child never share: full name with school, home address, passwords, photos of themselves, and anything they would not tell a stranger?
- Are there reporting, blocking or parental controls, and does your child know how to use them?
- Can it charge money through in-app purchases or subscriptions?

Above all, keep talking. Children will not recognise every risk, and a friendly, always-available chatbot can feel like a confidant. A rule they understand and a parent they can tell when something feels wrong will do more than any setting. eSafety's guidance for parents and carers is a good current starting point.

### Older Readers, and Anyone Being Rushed

Nothing in this chapter changes with age. The same method works for everyone: start with the task, check the publisher, test with something harmless, read the price properly. Surveys suggest older adults use AI chatbots less often on average, which says nothing about any individual's ability; plenty of experienced readers are more careful buyers than younger ones. A few habits matter for anyone who is new to these tools or being hurried along. Treat urgency as a reason to slow down. Be especially careful with payment details. Never ring a "support" number found in an advertisement, a pop-up or a search result; go to the company's own website or app instead. And if a second pair of eyes on an unfamiliar permission or payment screen would be useful, asking someone you trust is a sensible choice, not a sign of incapacity.

### Small Businesses and Side Hustles

If you run a small business, a consultancy or a side hustle, you may be using consumer-style tools while handling customer details, invoices, contracts, employee records or commercially sensitive documents. That raises the stakes beyond personal experimentation.

Chapter 7 covered workplace rules in depth; here are the additions that matter when you are choosing a tool. Check whether the service tier is appropriate for business information, because business plans can have different training, retention and administrative terms. Understand what any connected apps can reach. Test with invented or de-identified examples before using real customer data. Keep a person reviewing anything customer-facing. And do not treat the small-business exemption from the Privacy Act as permission to be careless: it has exceptions, it does not apply to every small business, and your customers' trust does not depend on which law covers you. Finally, keep two questions apart: "Am I allowed to upload this?" is not the same as "Is it sensible to upload this?"

## Avoiding Scams and Subscription Traps

Chapter 9 dealt with scammers *using* AI: cloned voices, deepfake videos and fluent phishing messages. This section looks in the other direction, at the AI products themselves. The question here is: **is this AI app, extension, website, subscription or seller legitimate, and is it presented fairly?**

It is worth being precise about how common the problem is, because two lazy answers are both wrong. There is no reliable figure for what share of AI products are scams, so a claim that "most AI apps are scams" is unsupported, and so is "AI scams are so rare you can ignore them". What can be said is that legitimate mainstream and specialist AI products clearly exist in large numbers, that imitations, malicious software and deceptive subscriptions built around AI have been documented repeatedly, and that a few checks before installing, paying or granting access cost far less than undoing a bad choice afterwards.

### Look-Alike Apps, Fake Sites and Malicious Extensions

When a technology becomes popular, people impersonate it. Three documented patterns show what that looks like.

In 2023, security researchers found several apps in the official Apple and Google app stores that used the name and style of a famous AI chatbot, offered little real functionality, and pushed users into expensive recurring subscriptions. The researchers called this **fleeceware**: apps designed less to steal data than to keep charging you.[^ch13-fleeceware]

Also in 2023, a browser extension presented as a tool for a popular AI chatbot was distributed through the official extension store, where it reportedly reached more than 9,000 users before removal. Security researchers found it was quietly taking over people's social-media accounts. A major platform company separately warned that some malicious AI-themed extensions actually provided the promised AI features while doing harm in the background.[^ch13-extension] That is the uncomfortable lesson: **"it works" is not proof that it is safe.**

In 2025, threat researchers documented fake "AI video generator" websites promoted through social-media advertising. People who uploaded material and downloaded their "video" received malicious software designed to steal passwords, browser data and payment details.[^ch13-fake-sites] The excitement around a new AI capability was itself the bait.

These are documented incidents and campaigns, reported mostly by security companies. They show that these things happen and what they look like. They do not tell you how likely you are to be affected, and they do not mean most AI apps or extensions are malicious. What they do teach is a short set of habits:

- A familiar AI name, logo or colour scheme is not proof of who published the app. Check the developer name.
- Official app and extension stores add review processes and make removal possible, which reduces some risk. They do not guarantee safety, honesty or fair pricing.
- Browser extensions can have unusually broad access to what you see and type online. Check what they ask for.
- Do not install AI tools from advertisements or unexpected download links. Go to the provider's site yourself.

### Subscription Traps

Subscriptions are the most common way consumer AI tools are sold, and also where the most ordinary harm happens: not theft, just paying more, for longer, than you meant to.

The patterns are recognisable once you know them: a free trial that needs card details and quietly becomes a recurring charge; a price shown per week; an introductory price far below the ongoing one; automatic renewal that is easy to miss; credits whose real cost per task is hard to work out; usage limits revealed only after you pay; cancellation much harder than signing up; and a high ongoing price for a capability you could get elsewhere for less, or already have.

Weekly pricing deserves a moment of arithmetic, because it is misleading without being false. Imagine an app charging $9.99 a week. That sounds like a coffee. Over a year it is about $519. A service charging $9.99 a month, by comparison, costs about $120 a year. Converting every recurring price into an annual figure is not a prediction of how long you will keep it. It is simply a fair way to compare. If a service uses credits, translate them into the unit you care about: how many images, minutes of transcription or documents does a typical month of your use actually need?

Two more practical facts. On at least one major app store, **uninstalling an app does not cancel its subscription**; you have to cancel it separately, and refunds for unwanted renewals are not automatic.[^ch13-app-stores] And Australian consumer research has found that subscription cancellation is a widespread frustration: in one 2024 study, three-quarters of Australians with subscriptions reported some kind of negative experience trying to cancel, and around a third said they had felt pressured to keep a subscription.[^ch13-subscriptions] That research covers subscriptions in general, not AI tools specifically, but it is the environment AI subscriptions are sold into.

Regulators treat this seriously. In July 2026, the Federal Court ordered an online question-and-answer service to pay $10 million in penalties after action by the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission. Among other things, the service had advertised joining for "AU$2", while enrolling people in ongoing monthly subscriptions of $45 to $75; the court also ordered refunds for affected consumers.[^ch13-justanswer] It was not an AI product, but it is a textbook case of the pattern above: the first price was not the real price.

### What Australian Consumer Law Says

Australian Consumer Law already prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct and false or misleading claims, and that applies to AI products and services as it does to anything else, as Chapter 11 noted. Businesses selling to Australians generally have to follow it even if they are based overseas, although resolving a problem with an overseas business can be harder.[^ch13-acl]

New protections are on the way. In July 2026, Parliament passed the Competition and Consumer Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Act 2026, which includes reforms aimed at subscription traps and other unfair practices. Those protections **begin on 1 July 2027**; at the time of writing, they were law but not yet in force.[^ch13-utp-act] Depending on when you read this, check the ACCC's current guidance for what now applies.

Whatever the law says on a given day, the durable expectations are the same: the price and the recurring charge should be clear before you agree, renewal should not be hidden, and leaving should not be deliberately harder than joining. If something goes wrong, act quickly: cancel through the account or app store, keep confirmations and receipts, contact the business, request a refund through the app store where you paid, ask your bank about a chargeback where that is available, and report misleading conduct to the ACCC. This is general information, not legal advice.

### Thin Wrappers: What Are You Paying For?

You may hear an AI app dismissed as "just a wrapper". A **wrapper** is a product that uses another company's AI model underneath its own interface, workflow or features. The term is often used as an insult. It should not be.

Building on another company's model is a normal and legitimate way to make software. AI companies deliberately offer their models for other businesses to build on, and many genuinely useful products work this way. A wrapper can add real value: an interface that suits a particular job, specialised steps for a profession, connections to the software you already use, collaboration features, accessibility, careful handling of a particular kind of data, quality checks, administrative and compliance controls, and someone to call when it breaks. You probably use several such products without knowing it, and you do not need to.

The concern is not the architecture. It is conduct. A wrapper becomes a problem when it falsely claims to have built a breakthrough of its own, pretends to be the company whose model it uses, hides that your prompts and files are passed to another company, charges a lot without making clear what it adds, asks for permissions its features do not need, or offers poor support, refunds and deletion.

A beginner usually cannot tell which model sits underneath an app, so do not try to answer "is this a wrapper?" Ask instead: **What am I paying this company to add? Who handles my data, including anyone it passes my data to? Is the company open about how it works? And would the general-purpose capability I already have do the job?** Those questions treat a good specialised product fairly and expose a poor one.

### Reviews and Inflated Claims

"Read the reviews" is not enough on its own, because reviews can be manipulated, and AI makes writing convincing fake ones cheaper. In Australia it is against the law for a business to create fake or misleading reviews, or to arrange for others to do so. The ACCC suggests watching for sudden bursts of very positive or very negative reviews, vague and generic wording, and reviews that sound suspiciously alike.[^ch13-reviews]

More useful than any single review is a pattern across independent places: specific, checkable claims rather than vague praise; the same complaint showing up on several unrelated sites; a publisher with a real history; and descriptions of capability that sound realistic. Testimonials on the seller's own website are marketing, not evidence.

And some claims should trigger Chapter 11's five questions immediately. "Guaranteed 100 per cent accurate." "Replaces your accountant." "Never makes mistakes." "Earn thousands a week with our AI system." No current AI tool can honestly promise perfect accuracy, and a claim that it replaces a whole profession is a forecast dressed up as a feature. Ask what kind of claim it is, what exactly is promised, where the evidence comes from and who has independently checked it.

> **Watch Out:** No single sign proves a scam, but these patterns together should stop you before you install, pay or grant access: pressure to act now; a publisher name that does not match the famous brand or logo; a recurring price you have to hunt for; small weekly prices; a free trial with hard-to-find renewal terms; permissions that have nothing to do with the feature; promises of perfect accuracy or guaranteed income; an advertisement or unfamiliar site urging you to download; "support" contact details that did not come from the company's own website; and a sign-up that takes thirty seconds while cancelling looks like it will take an afternoon. When you notice two or three of these at once, walk away or verify independently first.

## Leaving a Tool Cleanly

A personal toolkit includes the ability to put a tool down. This is the step most people forget, and it is where both subscription inertia and data lock-in live.

The most important thing to know is that **stopping payment and removing your data are separate jobs**. Deleting an app may not cancel the subscription. Cancelling the subscription may not delete your chats. Deleting your chats may not delete saved files, memories or shared links, and none of it necessarily revokes the access you gave the tool to your other accounts.

When you leave a tool, work through it deliberately:

1. Export anything you want to keep: conversations, documents, images, prompts and settings that took time to build.
2. Cancel the subscription and turn off automatic renewal, in the place where you actually subscribed, which may be an app store rather than the provider's website.
3. Note when paid access ends and keep the cancellation confirmation.
4. Delete chats, uploaded files, projects and shared links where appropriate, and review or clear any saved memory or personalisation.
5. Delete the account itself if you no longer need it, and read what account deletion covers.
6. Revoke any connections you gave it to your email, files, calendar or other services, including "Sign in with" access.
7. Remove the app or browser extension, and check its device permissions are gone.
8. Check your next card or app-store statement to confirm the charges have stopped.

Not every provider makes every step easy, and some data may be kept for legal or safety reasons whatever you do. But knowing the steps turns "I can always leave later" from an assumption into a plan.

Leaving is also worth considering even when nothing has gone wrong. Every so often, ask whether each tool still earns its place: whether you still use it, whether it still does the job, whether its price or privacy terms have changed, and whether something you already have now does the same thing. That is literacy over loyalty in practice: not abandoning tools on a whim, but staying because you have checked, and being able to go when you have checked again.

> **Try This:** Choose one real task you do at least occasionally, such as summarising a long email thread, writing a difficult message, describing a photo, transcribing a voice memo or explaining a spreadsheet error. Write it down in one sentence, with what goes in and what should come out. Decide which of the six kinds of help it needs. Before looking for anything new, check whether an app or device you already use can do it. Then try it on a low-stakes example you can judge: one with no private details, where you already know what a good result looks like. Inspect the result: what did it get right, what did it get wrong, did it invent anything? Then spend five minutes on the tool itself: who makes it, what permissions and data settings it has, and whether using it more would cost money. Finish by deciding honestly whether it is useful enough to keep using for that task, and if not, remove it cleanly. You will have practised the whole method once, and the second time will be quicker.

### Putting the Evaluation Method to Work

I learned fairly quickly that evaluating an AI tool is different from being impressed by one.

I was looking at GitHub Copilot because I had a very ordinary problem: writing software involves a lot of work that is necessary but not particularly interesting. Boilerplate code, repetitive functions, tests, documentation, figuring out an unfamiliar piece of code—none of it is difficult all the time, but collectively it eats hours.

Copilot appeared to offer an obvious solution. An AI coding assistant sitting inside the development environment, watching what I was doing and suggesting code as I worked.

The demonstration was convincing.

But demonstrations have an annoying habit of showing you the five minutes in which everything works.

So I started asking different questions.

Before I became too enthusiastic, I wanted to know what I was actually giving the tool access to. If I connected it to a real development environment, what information could it process? What happened to my prompts and code? Was that information retained? Could it be used to train models? What controls existed for organisations handling commercial or sensitive source code?

Those questions turned out to matter more than I initially expected.

GitHub's documentation explains that Copilot processes prompts, code snippets and contextual information to generate its responses, and that the data arrangements differ depending on the type of account. For example, GitHub states that Business and Enterprise customer data is not used to train its AI models, while individual users have different data controls.[^ch13-copilot-data]

That changed the way I thought about the product.

I wasn't evaluating an autocomplete feature anymore. I was evaluating a service that would be allowed to see pieces of software I was working on.

That distinction is easy to miss when the product is presented as a clever assistant.

Then I looked at the cost.

Copilot has a free option and paid plans, with individual subscriptions starting at US$10 per month, while heavier usage can involve additional AI-credit considerations.[^ch13-copilot-price]

Ten dollars a month isn't much if the tool saves me several hours. It is also not the point. The real question is whether the time saved survives contact with reality.

So I tested it on work where I already knew enough about the problem to judge the answer.

That was important.

If I ask an AI to solve a problem I don't understand, I have a rather awkward testing methodology: I don't know whether the answer is correct.

Instead, I gave it tasks where I could inspect the result. Sometimes it was genuinely useful. It could produce repetitive code quickly, suggest an approach I hadn't considered, explain unfamiliar sections and get me past the irritating blank page that appears when I know what I want but don't particularly want to type it.

Other times, it produced code that looked perfectly reasonable until I examined it more closely.

That was probably the most useful part of the experiment.

The dangerous AI answer isn't necessarily the obviously ridiculous one. That's easy to reject. The more interesting problem is the answer that looks professional, compiles successfully and is still wrong.

Security research reinforced that concern. An independent study of Copilot-generated code found that newer versions produced fewer vulnerable suggestions than earlier versions, but vulnerabilities were still present.[^ch13-copilot-security] In other words, the system was improving, but improvement was not the same thing as reliability.

At the same time, I didn't want to fall into the opposite trap and dismiss the tool simply because it could make mistakes.

There was evidence that it could produce measurable productivity gains. In a controlled GitHub study involving professional developers, participants using Copilot completed the assigned programming task substantially faster than those who did not use it.[^ch13-copilot-productivity]

That result made sense to me.

I didn't need Copilot to replace a developer. I needed it to remove some of the friction around development.

That became my decision.

I was comfortable using an AI assistant for certain classes of work, provided I remained responsible for checking what it produced. I wasn't comfortable treating generated code as automatically trustworthy simply because it was produced by a sophisticated model.

That sounds like a small distinction, but it changes the entire relationship with the tool.

I stopped asking, "Can Copilot write this for me?"

The better question became, "Is this a task where Copilot can save me time without creating more risk than the time it saves?"

Sometimes the answer was yes.

Sometimes it was no.

And sometimes the answer was, "Yes—but I'll check it myself."

That last category turned out to be surprisingly large.

The experience also changed the order in which I evaluate AI products.

Initially, I was mostly interested in capability. What could the thing do? How impressive were its answers? How much time could it save?

Now I put access and data much closer to the beginning of the process.

Who made it?

What exactly am I allowing it to see?

What happens to the information I give it?

What does it cost?

What happens when it gets something wrong?

And, perhaps most importantly, can I test its usefulness on a task where I am capable of recognising a bad answer?

That last question is particularly important because AI tools are very good at creating the feeling that something has been accomplished.

Sometimes something has been accomplished.

Sometimes I have simply been given a beautifully formatted opportunity to create a new problem.

That, for me, is the difference between evaluating an AI tool and being impressed by one.

The goal isn't to decide whether the technology is "good" or "bad". The goal is to work out where it is useful, what it is allowed to touch, what its mistakes cost, and whether the benefits justify those trade-offs.

Once I looked at Copilot that way, the decision became much easier.

I didn't have to trust the AI.

I had to understand the conditions under which I was prepared to use it.

In the end, I kept Copilot in my workflow, but I didn't give it a blank cheque.

I used it for the things where it was consistently useful: getting a first draft of routine code, generating tests that I could review, explaining unfamiliar sections, suggesting alternative implementations and helping me get unstuck. I became much more cautious when the code involved security-sensitive behaviour, business logic I couldn't easily verify, or information that I didn't want leaving the environment.

That distinction became my real evaluation.

I wasn't asking whether Copilot was a good product. I was asking whether using it for this particular task, with this particular information, at this particular cost, made sense.

There were also occasions when I deliberately didn't use it. If I was dealing with sensitive source code or a problem where I couldn't confidently evaluate the generated answer, the supposed productivity benefit wasn't enough to persuade me. Saving ten minutes isn't much of a bargain if I create an hour of verification work—or, worse, quietly introduce a problem I don't discover until later.

The biggest change wasn't actually my opinion of Copilot. It was the way I evaluated AI tools after that.

I stopped starting with the demo.

I started with the consequences.

What will I give this thing access to? What will it do with that information? What happens if it is wrong? Can I measure whether it genuinely saves me time? And what is the smallest, safest way I can test it before making it part of my workflow?

That last question has probably become my favourite.

I don't need to marry an AI tool because it produced an impressive demo. I can give it a small job, watch what happens, check the result and decide what it has earned.

That is ultimately how Copilot earned a place in my workflow.

Not because I trusted it.

Because I had tested it enough to know where I didn't need to trust it.

And that, I think, is a much more useful way to evaluate AI.

The question isn't whether the machine is impressive.

The question is whether, after you've looked at the access, the data, the cost, the errors and the actual results, you still have a good reason to let it into the room.

## Myth vs Reality

**"You need a separate app for every kind of AI task."** Modern assistants and built-in AI increasingly combine chat, research, images, voice, file analysis and more. The six categories help you name the job; they are not a shopping list.

**"Paid AI is always more accurate than free AI."** Paid tiers can buy more capable models, higher limits and extra tools, and sometimes different data terms. Price is not an accuracy guarantee. Pay for a specific difference you can name and need.

**"If an app is in an official store, it's safe."** Store review reduces some risk, but fake, malicious and fleeceware AI apps and extensions have reached official stores. The publisher, permissions and pricing still need checking.

**"If I turn off training, the service doesn't keep my data."** Training and storage are separate questions. A service may still keep conversations for your history, safety, human review, legal obligations or a set retention period.

**"Deleting a chat deletes everything."** Chats, uploaded files, memories, shared links, connected-app data and legally preserved copies can each be handled differently. Think before you enter something you could not bear to have kept.

**"An app built on another company's AI is a scam."** Many legitimate products are built that way and add genuine value. The real questions are what it adds, how transparent it is, how it handles your data and what it charges.

**"AI built into my phone or office software is automatically private."** Some processing may happen on the device or under stronger business terms; other features send data to the cloud or reach into connected accounts. Check the specific feature and account type.

**"AI literacy means knowing how to use every AI tool."** It means understanding capabilities, limitations, trade-offs and responsible use well enough to judge the tools relevant to your own life, including ones that do not exist yet.

## Core Takeaway

AI literacy matters more than loyalty to any specific tool. After this chapter, that sentence should carry its conditions with it. It does not mean product choice never matters, that all tools are interchangeable, that familiarity is bad or that you should switch whenever something new appears. Staying with a tool that serves you well, with your history, settings and habits intact, is perfectly sensible.

What it does mean is this. Tools change faster than the principles for judging them. Start from the task, not the brand. One or two well-chosen tools, plus what is already built into your devices, may be all you need. Capability is only part of the decision: who makes the tool, what it does with your data, what it can access, what it really costs and how you would leave all matter too. And the durable skill is not knowing today's winner. It is being able to understand, test and re-evaluate whatever tool is in front of you, including one that had not been invented when this book went to print.

## Chapter Recap

> **Recap:** The six categories, chat, image, research, productivity, coding and voice, are kinds of help, not six subscriptions, and one tool may cover several. Start with the task, check whether something you already use can do it, and only then look further. Use a quick first-pass check (need, maker, data, result, cost and exit) before trying anything, and a deeper check before paying, sharing sensitive information, connecting accounts or letting a child use a tool. Storage, training, memory, human review, sharing and deletion are separate privacy questions, and consumer, business and on-device features can follow different rules. Test with low-stakes material you can judge, keep secrets and other people's private information out of casual experiments, and remember that an easy test is not proof of reliability. Fake apps, malicious extensions and subscription traps have recognisable patterns; a wrapper is not automatically a scam; accessibility, age and account type can change what "the right tool" means. And leaving a tool cleanly is part of using it well. Before moving on, ask yourself: which kind of AI help do I actually need most often? Do I already have it? Which tool do I use mainly out of habit? What have I been typing in without checking the data terms? If my main AI tool disappeared tomorrow, what would I lose? Which permission or connected account should I review? And which subscription would I cancel if its trial ended today?

## Chapter Preview

This chapter gave you a method for choosing among the AI tools that exist now. The final chapter asks a different question: what might change about those tools next? Chapter 14 looks at where AI appears to be heading, including assistants that act rather than just answer, robotics, and AI's growing role in education, healthcare and transport. It will separate what exists today from what is announced, forecast or speculated, and it will invite you to apply Chapter 11's five questions to its own predictions. The habits from this chapter travel with you: whatever arrives next, it will still be a tool someone wants you to trust with your time, money and data.
