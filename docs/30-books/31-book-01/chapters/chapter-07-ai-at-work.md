# Chapter 7 — AI at Work

## From Personal Tasks to Professional Stakes

You have the information. It is just in the wrong shape.

The project update is scattered across messages. The customer has explained the problem three times, to three different people. The spreadsheet contains the right numbers, but the formula produces an answer you do not quite trust. Tomorrow's presentation is still a report with a title slide attached.

These are useful places to start with AI at work. You do not need to automate your job. You need help with a particular piece of it.

Chapter 6 used the communication skills from Chapter 5 to make everyday tasks more manageable. At work, the same skills apply: explain the situation, supply relevant information, say what you need and inspect the result. What changes is who may depend on the answer. A mistaken dinner suggestion is annoying. A mistaken customer commitment can become somebody else's problem to resolve.

The opportunity is substantial. The professional-writing experiment introduced in Chapter 6 involved 453 college-educated professionals completing assigned writing tasks. Access to ChatGPT reduced average completion time by about 40 per cent and increased assessed quality by about 18 per cent.[^ch7-writing] That is a meaningful gain. It also describes particular tasks in an experiment, with fewer complications than a real report assembled from disputed figures, missing information and three rounds of approval.

A study involving 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants makes the qualification especially clear. On tasks within the tested model's capabilities, AI users completed more work, worked about 25 per cent faster and produced better-rated results. On a managerial task selected because it exposed a model weakness, AI users were less likely to reach the correct answer.[^ch7-frontier] The same broad category, professional knowledge work, contained both success and failure.

The researchers called this a *jagged technological frontier*. In ordinary language, AI's abilities have an uneven edge. A task that looks difficult may go well; a neighbouring task that looks straightforward may go badly. Success at summarising a report does not establish competence at deciding whether its recommendation is justified.

> **Key Idea:** AI can be a workplace multiplier when the task, information and checking process fit. Professional judgement remains with the person using it.

This chapter turns that principle into work you can try. The scenarios, source notes and sample outputs are fictional teaching examples, not records of product tests or the author's employment. For your own work, use a tool and account approved for the information involved. You can practise every request here with the fictional material first.

Chapter 6 used ChatGPT as a familiar doorway. You can use it for these conversational examples where your organisation permits, or use another approved assistant. The useful choice may already be inside your workplace software. Microsoft Teams, for example, offers intelligent meeting recap through eligible licences, while Copilot in Excel and Gemini in Google Sheets provide spreadsheet assistance under their respective subscription and account conditions.[^ch7-tools] Those integrations can reduce copying between tools, but they still need checking.

Start with the task rather than a subscription comparison. If all you need is a total, use the spreadsheet. If you need help expressing the rule that produces the total, an AI conversation may be exactly what is missing.

## Drafting Reports and Documentation

### From Project Notes to a Useful Update

“Write me a project update” asks the AI to do two different jobs: decide what happened and describe it. You probably want help with the second.

Imagine you coordinate a small warehouse improvement project. You need an update for the operations manager, who wants to know whether a decision is needed. Here are the facts you have checked for the reporting period, 7–11 September:

- The team labelled 120 storage locations against a period target of 150.
- The remaining 30 locations need replacement signs. The supplier missed its delivery date; no revised arrival date has been confirmed.
- Staff completed two practice sessions using the new picking checklist.
- The pilot is planned for 21 September, subject to receiving the signs and completing a final check.
- The manager must decide by 15 September whether to authorise up to $400 for locally printed replacement signs.
- No pilot performance results are available yet.

A useful request gives those facts a destination:

> Turn these notes into an update of no more than 220 words for our operations manager, covering 7–11 September. Start with the decision needed, then progress, risks and next steps. Use only the supplied facts. Keep the quantities, dates and spending limit exact. Preserve the conditions on the pilot date. Do not invent a delivery estimate, say the spending has been approved, or claim the new checklist has improved performance. List any missing information separately.

The difference is practical. The reporting period prevents old activity becoming this week's achievement. The audience explains why the decision belongs first. The spending limit is a request for authority, not an expense already incurred. The instruction about missing information keeps a gap visible.

An illustrative opening could read:

> **Decision needed by 15 September:** whether to authorise up to $400 for locally printed replacement signs. The supplier missed its delivery date and has not confirmed a replacement date.
>
> During 7–11 September, the team labelled 120 of the planned 150 storage locations and completed two practice sessions using the new picking checklist. The 21 September pilot remains conditional on receiving the signs and completing a final check. Pilot performance results are not yet available.

That is a useful draft because the manager can see what to decide and why. It does not need “We are delighted to report significant progress on our transformational warehouse journey.” The signs have not arrived. Let us keep our feet on the warehouse floor.

Now inspect what the draft says and what it leaves out. If it reports “80 per cent of the project is complete”, it has changed the meaning of the figures. The team labelled 80 per cent of the locations planned for that period; nothing establishes that labelling represents the whole project. If it says the pilot “will begin on 21 September”, two conditions have disappeared.

The same problem can occur through tone. “The new checklist will improve picking accuracy” sounds like an ordinary project sentence. The notes contain no results supporting it. You might reasonably describe improved accuracy as an objective if that objective is supplied, but an objective and a measured outcome belong in different sentences.

A useful follow-up is:

> Compare your draft with the notes. Identify every factual statement, number and commitment that is not directly supported. Show the source bullet for each supported statement.

This can make review easier. It is still the same AI checking its own work, so you must compare the important statements yourself. For a short update, that comparison may take less effort than composing the prose from scratch. For a report full of invented evidence, rebuilding the source trail may take longer than starting again.

You can also use AI for one difficult paragraph, a shorter executive summary or an audience change. An operations specialist may need the technical details; the managing director may need the effect on customers and the decision required. Different emphasis is legitimate. Different facts are not.

For recurring reports with fixed fields, keep the template and pull figures from the established business system. Use AI for the commentary if that is where it helps. Regenerating a standard report's entire structure every Friday creates work that the template already solved.

### Documentation Without Inventing the Organisation

Documentation is particularly useful territory for administrators, team leads and small-business owners. Someone knows the process, but everyone else keeps asking them how it works. AI can help turn that person's rough explanation into instructions another person can follow.

The source still needs to come from the organisation. “Write our procedure for handling damaged deliveries” invites a plausible procedure that may have nothing to do with the real one.

Suppose these are approved notes for a fictional office-supplies business:

> Damaged parcel: receiving officer photographs the outside and contents; records purchase-order number and damaged quantity in the receiving log; puts affected goods in the marked hold area; emails the purchasing mailbox with the log reference. Purchasing contacts supplier. Do not put damaged items into available stock. Notes do not say who can authorise disposal or what to do when an urgent customer order is affected.

A better request is:

> Make these approved notes into a draft procedure for a new receiving officer. Include purpose, numbered steps and questions the process owner must answer before publication. Keep the role names and existing sequence. Do not add software, approvals, deadlines or safety instructions. Put missing organisational details in a separate “To confirm” list.

The result should preserve the actual sequence: document the damage, record it, hold the goods and notify purchasing. It should also expose the two missing answers. An instruction such as “Dispose of damaged goods after 48 hours with supervisor approval” would be invented organisational knowledge, however sensible it sounds.

Try the draft against a realistic case before making it official. Can a new colleague find the receiving log? Do they know the purchasing mailbox address? Does the procedure explain how to identify the hold area? If the notes omit these details, obtain them from the process owner. AI can identify the questions; it cannot appoint itself as the owner of the answers.

A practical refinement is to have a colleague unfamiliar with the process read the draft and point to the first step they could not perform. That tests usability, which proofreading alone cannot establish. Then have the responsible person confirm the procedure reflects actual practice and any applicable requirements.

The same approach works for onboarding instructions, handover notes and a small-business FAQ. Supply the current service information, prices and terms. Ask for clear questions and answers, with gaps separated for review. A pleasant FAQ that quietly promises weekend support has created a service commitment, not merely improved the wording.

For an operations incident, give AI the verified timeline and ask it to organise events, impact, recovery and unanswered questions. Keep “what happened next” separate from “what caused it”. If a service failed after an update, the sequence is evidence to investigate. It does not, by itself, prove the update caused the failure.

Once you have established the facts, AI can make them easier to use. Meeting records present the same opportunity, with an extra complication: people rarely speak in finished documentation.

## Summarising Meetings

### A Meeting With Several Possible Futures

A meeting summary can save the work of turning half sentences into something the team can act on. The difficulty is preserving what people actually committed to.

Consider this fictional meeting about introducing a new appointment-booking process. The notes are intentionally untidy. They contain discussion, an agreed position, a firm action and several gaps.

> **Meeting: 6 October 2026, booking pilot. Attendees: Leila, Noah, Tess and Owen.**
>
> Leila: staff training done at North office. South still needs one session. Pilot target currently 20 October, both offices.
>
> Noah: booking confirmation messages failed twice in yesterday's test. Supplier thinks patch might be ready 9 October; no promise. Ticket BK-418.
>
> Tess: could start at North only on the 20th? Less disruption. Would need to tell reception what happens when someone books across offices.
>
> Owen: prefers both together. Splitting could confuse customers. Not agreed with Tess on this.
>
> Leila: keep 20 October for both as planning target. We are not approving launch today. Need successful retest and South training first. Decide whether to proceed at the 13 October review.
>
> Noah: I'll contact supplier and report patch status by 2 pm on 8 October. Can report even if they still have no date.
>
> Tess: I can arrange South training if Owen can release two staff. Owen: need to check roster, can't promise yet. Nobody assigned roster follow-up or a training deadline.
>
> Owen: I'll check the reception FAQ draft. No deadline mentioned.
>
> Noah: old confirmation template still says the former cancellation window. Separate content fix needed; patch doesn't cover it. No owner agreed.
>
> Leila: don't announce pilot to customers before 13 October review. Risk of more bookings than South can handle if training slips. Current bookings continue through existing process.

“Summarise this meeting” might produce a readable paragraph. But what does *summarise* mean here? A person who missed the discussion needs context. A project coordinator needs decisions, actions and missing assignments. Those are related outputs with different priorities.

Before reading the structured version, try extracting the actions yourself. Which person has made an unconditional commitment? Which date is merely an estimate? Where would you need to ask a follow-up question?

A plausible but wrong summary would be:

> Both offices will launch on 20 October. Noah will deliver the supplier patch by 9 October, Tess will complete South training, and Owen will finish the FAQ before the 13 October review.

Every name and date comes from somewhere in the notes. Almost every commitment is wrong. The error is not an obviously imaginary event. It is the rearrangement of real details into a tidier story.

### Ask for a Record You Can Check

A better instruction makes the distinctions explicit:

> Using only these meeting notes, produce a short record with separate headings for decisions, actions, unresolved issues and proposals not agreed. For actions, show the owner and deadline only when explicitly agreed; otherwise write “not assigned” or “not stated”. Preserve conditions and disagreement. Separate supplier estimates from our commitments. Do not convert the pilot target into approval to launch. Finish with the questions we need answered before the next review.

This is Chapter 5's specificity at work. The request does not need special symbols or a theatrical instruction to act as a world-class meeting expert. It needs to describe the useful output.

An illustrative checked record would look like this:

**Decisions and current position:**

- Keep 20 October as the planning target for both offices. Launch is not approved.
- Successful retesting and South training are prerequisites. The decision whether to proceed is due at the 13 October review.
- Do not announce the pilot to customers before that review. Existing bookings continue through the current process.

**Actions and gaps in assignment:**

| Work identified | Owner | Deadline or condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact supplier and report patch status for BK-418 | Noah | 8 October, 2 pm; report even if no fix date is available |
| Check reception FAQ draft | Owen | Not stated |
| Arrange South training | Not confirmed; Tess offered conditionally | Depends on releasing two staff; deadline not stated |
| Check whether two staff can be released | Not assigned | Not stated |
| Correct cancellation wording in confirmation template | Not assigned | Not stated; separate from supplier patch |

**Unresolved issues and risks:**

The patch may be ready on 9 October, but this is the supplier's estimate. Retesting has not happened. Staffing for South training is unconfirmed. Delayed training could leave South unable to handle the bookings. The old cancellation wording needs a separate correction.

**Proposal not agreed:**

Tess suggested starting with North only. Owen preferred both offices together because of possible customer confusion. No agreement was reached on a North-only start.

**Questions for follow-up:**

Who will check the roster, by when? Can Tess confirm ownership of training once staffing is settled? Who owns the template correction? When does Owen need to finish reviewing the FAQ? What evidence will show that retesting has passed?

The empty assignments are useful. Filling them with guesses would conceal unfinished coordination. A summary can reveal work the meeting failed to settle; it should not quietly settle that work itself.

The two dates attached to the supplier problem show why this distinction matters:

![The supplier's possible patch date is an estimate; Noah's promised status report has an owner and a firm deadline.](../diagrams/meeting-estimate-versus-commitment.mmd){ width=85% }

If you were updating the team's calendar, you could create Noah's reporting reminder for 8 October at 2 pm. You could record 9 October as a tentative supplier estimate, but you could not turn it into Noah's delivery deadline. The diagram separates two meanings that a compressed paragraph can easily merge.

### Check Meaning, Then Pass It On

Review this record beside the raw notes. There are several traps worth practising.

First, Noah owns a status report, not the supplier's patch delivery. Second, 9 October is an estimate, while 8 October at 2 pm is an actual reporting deadline. Third, Tess's offer depends on staffing. Fourth, Owen's disagreement matters because the North-only option remains unresolved. Finally, “launch not approved” is essential even though it is an awkward qualification in a short summary.

Check omissions as well as statements. Leaving out the obsolete cancellation wording would conceal a customer-facing problem that the technical patch will not fix. A shorter summary is not automatically a better one.

Meeting-summary research supports looking beyond spectacular hallucinations. A COLING 2025 paper introduced 200 generated meeting summaries annotated across nine error types, including omission, structural problems and irrelevant content.[^ch7-meetings] That does not give an error rate for your workplace tool. It shows why “nothing in this sounds absurd” is an inadequate review method.

Once the record is checked, ask for a brief follow-up message based on that version. You might request that it open with the two unassigned actions and ask participants to confirm ownership. Do not write “as agreed, Owen will check the roster” when that is precisely what remains unagreed.

If an assistant can create tasks or send messages, inspect the proposed assignments first and confirm what was actually saved or sent. A mistaken summary copied into the task tracker can become next week's apparently authoritative evidence.

### Notes and Recordings Are Different Choices

The example starts with existing notes. Recording or transcribing a meeting creates an additional record of people's speech. Before enabling it, follow your organisation's requirements for participant information, permissions, access and retention. In Australia, workplace monitoring and surveillance can involve state and territory rules; there is no single sentence about consent that safely covers every setting.[^ch7-recording]

Also remember that a transcript may mishear words or attribute them to the wrong speaker. A summary based faithfully on a bad transcript can still be wrong about the meeting. Where a consequential commitment is unclear, confirm it with the people involved.

This need not eliminate the saving. You are checking a structured record against known material, then resolving a small number of gaps. That can be much easier than writing everything from scratch and discovering the same gaps later.

## Customer Support

The customer does not care how many messages the support team has processed. They want the problem understood and the next step to make sense.

AI can help a support worker reach that point. A long thread may contain the symptoms, repeated troubleshooting, a previous promise and the customer's current request in different places. Extracting those details is useful work before any reply is drafted.

There is substantial evidence behind this use case. The published study *Generative AI at Work* examined a staggered deployment to 5,172 customer-support agents. Access to assistance increased issues resolved per hour by about 15 per cent on average. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers improved both speed and quality; the most experienced and highest-skilled workers saw small speed gains and small quality declines.[^ch7-support]

That is more informative than “AI makes support better”. It suggests that assistance can make useful working patterns available to newer staff, while offering less to people who already handle the task well. It does not show that a general chatbot should answer customers without supervision, or that every support team will obtain the same gain.

Here is a fictional case, condensed from a longer exchange:

> Monday: customer says the weekly stock export fails after selecting the date range. Support asks them to try another browser.
>
> Tuesday: customer confirms the same failure in two browsers. Support suggests clearing the cache.
>
> Wednesday: customer says that also failed; export still stops at 82 per cent. They need the file for Friday's stock review. They ask for a service credit and a confirmed fix date.
>
> Current internal guidance: repeated export failure after these steps should go to technical support with the failed export time and selected date range. Frontline staff may request a service-credit review but cannot approve a credit. No restoration time has been confirmed for this case.

“Reply to this customer” leaves the AI to decide which requests it can satisfy. Give it two separate jobs instead:

> First prepare an escalation summary: current problem, troubleshooting already tried, business impact and missing information. Then draft a reply under 130 words using the internal guidance. Acknowledge the repeated effort without asking the customer to repeat completed steps. Ask for the failed export time and selected date range. Do not promise a credit or fix date. Do not say escalation has happened; I will do that after checking the draft.

A useful internal summary would preserve the 82 per cent failure point, both attempted fixes, Friday's business need and the missing diagnostic details. “Customer cannot export data” is accurate but too thin for the next person to act efficiently.

An illustrative reply might say:

> Thanks for confirming that the export still stops at 82 per cent after trying both browsers and clearing the cache. I understand you need the file for Friday's stock review. Please send the time of the latest failed export and the date range selected so I can include them in the technical-support handover. I can also request a review of your service-credit request. I cannot yet confirm a credit or a fix date.

Before sending, verify that this is the correct and current guidance, that the customer has not already supplied the requested details elsewhere, and that you can perform the promised next step. Then actually make the handover. A well-written email does not move a ticket by itself.

The subtle failure to watch for is “Your service credit has been approved.” Another is “We expect to resolve this before Friday.” Both would make the customer happier for a moment. Neither is supported.

![AI can prepare the support reply, while the authorised business process determines whether a service credit may be promised.](../diagrams/support-draft-and-authority.mmd){ width=85% }

Our example follows the “No” branch: the worker can request a review, but no credit has been authorised. The “Yes” branch would require a real approval under the business's process. Asking the AI for a more reassuring tone cannot move the case from one branch to the other.

This distinction extends to sales. An assistant can summarise a conversation, separate stated needs from unanswered questions and prepare a follow-up. It should not turn “the customer asked about a discount” into “we offered ten per cent”, or invent a product feature to overcome an objection.

Translation and tone changes can also help, but preserve policy terms, quantities and commitments. If nobody reviewing a consequential translated response can assess the target language, route it through the organisation's language support. Fluent wording is not a substitute for knowing what the message says.

A newer worker may gain useful structure from all this assistance while still needing an experienced colleague for unusual cases. The ability to produce a convincing answer and the ability to recognise a wrong answer do not necessarily develop together.

## Presentations and Spreadsheet Help

### Give the Presentation a Job

A presentation usually needs to change something: the audience's understanding, a decision or the next action. “Make slides from this report” does not tell the AI which one.

Return to the warehouse project. The manager has eight minutes to consider the missing signs and the pilot conditions. A useful request could be:

> Using the checked project update, outline four slides for an eight-minute discussion with the operations manager. The purpose is to decide whether to authorise up to $400 for replacement signs. For each slide, give one takeaway, no more than three supporting points and brief speaker notes. Cover the decision, current progress, options and next steps. Use only the report's facts. Mark information needed to compare options instead of supplying estimates.

A sensible sequence is:

1. **Decision needed:** whether to authorise the local-printing option by 15 September.
2. **Current position:** 120 of 150 planned locations labelled; remaining signs missing; two practice sessions completed.
3. **Options and unknowns:** wait for the supplier or investigate local printing within the proposed limit; delivery timing and local availability need confirmation.
4. **Next steps:** record the manager's decision and check the conditions before the planned pilot.

The outline helps separate a decision from a progress recital. But watch slide three. The notes do not establish that a local printer can deliver in time. “Local printing guarantees the pilot date” would manufacture the main reason for choosing it.

Speaker notes can carry qualifications that would overcrowd the slide. They should not hide qualifications the audience needs to see. If the headline says “Ready for launch” and the notes say “subject to unresolved testing”, the slide is misleading even if both statements came from the report.

Ask a follow-up that focuses on the audience:

> What question would an operations manager need answered before approving this? Identify gaps in the supplied evidence. Do not answer those questions yourself.

That may reveal a need for a quotation, a delivery commitment or a fallback plan. Getting those answers is more valuable than generating another six slides.

For a different audience, the same facts can have a different sequence. Staff may need to understand what changes in their work and where to ask for help. A client may need the effect on delivery and confirmed dates. Tell the AI which audience it is serving, then inspect whether it has changed only emphasis or also meaning.

AI can help simplify dense explanations and propose chart types. You still need to check labels, units, denominators and the interpretation in the title. A chart showing complaints fell after a process change does not establish that the change caused the fall. A quieter period, fewer customers or a changed reporting method may matter.

Use your own voice when rehearsing. Read the notes aloud and remove sentences you would never say. If you cannot explain the main figure without reading the generated paragraph, return to the source. AI can organise a presentation; it cannot make weak evidence strong.

### Turn a Spreadsheet Question Into a Testable Rule

Spreadsheet assistance can be valuable even when you have no interest in programming. The difficulty is often translating “what I need” into the function the spreadsheet expects.

Tell the AI which application you use, what the columns mean, your current formula, the desired result and any error message. A few invented rows are often enough to explain the problem without uploading a live financial or employee workbook.

Suppose a sales coordinator has target revenue in column B and actual revenue in column C. They want percentage above or below target. Their formula, `=C2/B2`, returns 120 per cent when actual revenue is 120 and target revenue is 100. It calculates successfully. It answers a different question: actual revenue as a proportion of target.

“Fix my percentage formula” may produce the right formula by luck. A clearer request is:

> I'm using Excel with English function names and comma separators. B contains non-negative revenue targets and C contains actual revenue, in the same currency. In D I want percentage above or below target: target 100 and actual 120 should give 20%, while actual 80 should give -20%. My current formula is =C2/B2. If either input is blank, show blank. If the target is zero, show “No target” because our team does not report a percentage in that case. Actual zero with target 100 should give -100%. Explain the correction and provide test cases. Assume other entries are numeric.

One formula for those stated rules is:

```text
=IF(OR(B2="",C2=""),"",IF(B2=0,"No target",(C2-B2)/B2))
```

Format the numeric results in D as percentages. The formula first checks for a missing input, then the zero-target rule. Otherwise it subtracts target from actual and divides that difference by target. The result for 120 against 100 is 0.2, displayed as 20 per cent.

The phrase “our team does not report a percentage” matters. “No target” is a business choice in this example. AI should not choose how your organisation treats an undefined percentage without being told.

Before copying the formula down a large workbook, try a small table whose answers you know:

| Target B | Actual C | Expected result in D |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 120 | 20% |
| 100 | 80 | -20% |
| 100 | 100 | 0% |
| 100 | 0 | -100% |
| 0 | 50 | No target |
| blank | 50 | blank |
| 100 | blank | blank |

These checks test meaning, not just whether Excel accepts the formula. Excel accepted the original formula, but it expressed the wrong rule. If your real file contains text in numeric columns, negative targets or values imported with unusual formatting, the assumptions have changed. Show representative examples and ask how those cases should be handled. Do not simply wrap every error in a blank result; that can hide bad data.

Totals need a separate check. Imagine two branches: one has a target of 100 and actual revenue of 120; the other has a target of 900 and actual revenue of 900. Their variances are 20 per cent and zero. Averaging those percentages gives ten per cent, but combined actual revenue is 1,020 against a target of 1,000: a two per cent increase. If the question concerns total revenue, calculate from the totals. A neat average of percentages can be the wrong business answer.

You can use AI to explain a lookup, diagnose a missing match, suggest a pivot table that groups totals by department, or identify why dates are being treated as text. Preserve the same discipline: define the rule and test boundaries. For a monthly report, test the final day of the month and the first day of the next. For a lookup, test a missing identifier and a duplicate. State whether blanks mean zero, unknown or not applicable.

Current Excel and Google Sheets assistants can help with formulas, charts and pivot tables, but availability depends on subscription and configuration.[^ch7-tools] You can also ask an approved conversational tool to explain a formula without giving it the entire workbook. The AI translates your intention; the spreadsheet holds the inspectable calculation.

If all you need is the total of a column you already understand, use `SUM`. There is no extra credit for making a chatbot supervise addition.

## Research Assistance and Brainstorming

### Use AI to Build the Investigation

Research and brainstorming often begin with the same feeling: you do not know where to start. Their standards are different. A brainstorming suggestion can be useful because it provokes a better idea. A research claim needs evidence.

Suppose a small business is considering a different delivery supplier. “Research the best courier” invites an answer built around unknown priorities, possibly outdated prices and a confident ranking. Start with the decision:

> We send about 80 parcels a week, mostly within Australia, including some regional deliveries. We are comparing suppliers, but have not obtained quotations yet. Help me plan the research. List the questions we need to answer about total cost, delivery coverage, collection times, tracking, claims and customer support. Suggest the primary sources for each answer. Keep supplier facts separate from questions and do not rank companies without evidence.

Now the output can become a useful investigation. Pricing needs a current quotation matched to parcel sizes, destinations and volume. Coverage needs the supplier's service information for the relevant postcodes. Claims handling needs the actual terms and, where available, evidence about how the process works. A generic star rating cannot settle all three.

AI can explain unfamiliar terms before you speak to suppliers. You might ask what a fuel surcharge is, how volumetric weight differs from scale weight, or which questions would clarify an exclusion. Treat those explanations as preparation, then confirm the details in the documents governing the proposed service.

Once you have suitable documents in an approved tool, narrow the task:

> Compare these two quotations against our stated requirements. For each factual entry, give the source document and page or section. Separate quoted terms, calculations from those terms, and your interpretation. If a document does not answer a question, write “not stated”. Identify differences that prevent a fair comparison.

A useful comparison might expose that one quoted price excludes a surcharge while the other includes it. That is more valuable than awarding each supplier a score based on assumptions nobody approved.

### A Source Can Exist and Still Fail the Check

Imagine the AI writes: “Supplier A is more reliable because its report shows 98 per cent on-time delivery.” You open the report and the figure exists. Are you finished?

No. The report might cover metropolitan express parcels in the previous year. Your problem includes regional standard deliveries. The supplier might define “on time” using a window different from your customers' expectations. The report might exclude failed delivery attempts. It is also the supplier reporting its own performance.

The source is real; the broader conclusion is unsupported. The right response is to narrow the claim and identify the missing evidence. Ask the supplier for the measure relevant to your service, and compare like with like before deciding.

For consequential claims, check three things: the source exists, it says what the AI claims, and its population, period and circumstances fit your question. Open important original sources rather than relying on a summary of a summary. Confirm quotations in the source itself. Check dates, editions and whether a linked report has been superseded.

This is more than a hypothetical concern. A 2023 study of references produced by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 found both fabricated citations and errors in real citations.[^ch7-citations] Those historical results are not today's error rate for every research tool. They demonstrate why a bibliography-shaped answer does not establish that research has been done.

Ask for primary sources and use search independently when the consequences justify it. A primary source is the original material behind a claim: the study, published accounts, official policy, product documentation or contract. It can still have weaknesses. A supplier's own page is authoritative about its advertised offer, but it is not independent proof that customers receive the best service.

Keep a simple record as you work: claim, source, relevant passage, date and remaining uncertainty. AI can help organise that record. If it adds an inference, label it as an inference. “Both quotations leave this charge unclear” and “therefore Supplier B is dishonest” are very different statements.

Also search for evidence that could change your preferred answer. If the AI has helped build a persuasive case for switching suppliers, ask what would favour staying. Then investigate those questions. The aim is a decision you can explain, including its uncertainties, rather than an impressive report that agrees with the first idea.

### Use Brainstorming to Widen the Options

For brainstorming, you can be more playful. Ask for campaign angles, objections, possible explanations, workshop questions or different ways to organise a service. The output is a set of candidates to examine.

“Give me marketing ideas” will probably produce familiar suggestions. Add the actual constraints:

> We run a local repair workshop and want more weekday bookings from people who currently replace items rather than repair them. We have a $600 trial budget, two staff and no capacity for weekend events. Our approved offer is an assessment, not a guaranteed repair or saving. Suggest six approaches that differ in how they reach people or address hesitation. Explain the assumption behind each, then identify two concerns the list misses. Do not invent success rates or customer testimonials.

Useful possibilities might include a lunchtime demonstration with a local workplace, a guide to deciding whether an item is worth assessing, or a booking page that explains what happens when repair is not possible. These are different mechanisms: convenient access, better information and reduced uncertainty. Six variations of “post on social media” offer less breadth.

Then challenge the list:

> Which idea depends most on an assumption we have not tested? Suggest a small way to learn about that assumption before spending the full budget.

That could lead to asking existing customers why they delayed booking, checking whether lunchtime appointments are wanted, or testing two explanations of the assessment process. The generated stakeholder perspective is a hypothesis to investigate. It is not a substitute for hearing from actual customers.

The research gives reasons to try AI here and reasons to avoid letting it define the whole session. Lee and Chung's experiments found that ChatGPT assistance increased assessed idea creativity on several everyday and innovation problems, with stronger results for incremental than radically new ideas. A later published critique and reply examined the separate question of diversity across ideas.[^ch7-ideation] A better-rated idea from each participant does not necessarily produce a more varied pool for the group.

A separate short-story experiment found that AI ideas improved evaluated creativity and writing, particularly for less creative writers, while assisted stories became more similar to one another.[^ch7-creativity] That was creative writing, not a trial of workplace marketing teams. It illustrates how individual improvement and collective sameness can coexist.

A practical response is to write down a few ideas yourself before asking AI, or let team members think independently before sharing a generated list. Then ask for missing approaches, objections or a different framing. These are reasonable techniques to try, not research-proven cures for conformity. Their purpose is to keep the first plausible answer from setting every subsequent boundary.

The same approach works beyond marketing, especially where the useful output needs to help another person understand what to do. Two ordinary examples are explaining a job and helping a colleague practise a process.

Job advertisements provide a particularly useful warning about purpose. In a large online labour-market field experiment, offering employers AI-written first drafts reduced time spent writing posts by 44 per cent and increased posting by 19 per cent. Yet the researchers found no discernible increase in matches, and the posts became more generic and less informative.[^ch7-jobposts] This working paper concerns one platform intervention, not every edited job advertisement. Its lesson travels well: quicker writing can lose value if it removes the details other people need.

### Make a Job Advertisement More Informative

Imagine an HR coordinator receives an approved outline for a part-time reception role: 20 hours each week, Monday to Thursday mornings, at the office; answer calls, organise appointments and greet visitors; no staff supervision. The pay range has not yet been supplied.

“Make this job sound exciting” could produce the usual fog about passionate team players. The useful task is to help applicants recognise the actual work:

> Turn this approved role outline into a clear 180-word job advertisement. Describe the duties, hours and location plainly. Do not invent salary, benefits, remote work, qualifications or promotion prospects. Separate information we need from the hiring manager before publication. Replace vague phrases with concrete duties supported by the outline.

A useful sentence might be: “You will answer incoming calls, organise appointments and welcome visitors during our Monday-to-Thursday morning reception hours.” That tells the reader something they can picture. “Enjoy flexible hours and a pathway into management” would add two promises the outline does not support.

The missing pay range belongs in the questions for the hiring manager, not in a guessed salary band. Check the draft against the approved role and your normal recruitment requirements before publishing. AI has helped communicate the job; it has not decided who should get it.

### Turn a Procedure Into Staff Practice

An office supervisor can use the damaged-delivery procedure from earlier in the chapter to prepare a ten-minute induction exercise. “Create some training” is vague. The supervisor needs a new receiving officer to recognise the sequence and know where the instructions stop.

> Using only the checked damaged-delivery procedure, make three short practice situations for a new receiving officer. Ask what they would do next, then put an answer guide separately for me. Include one routine case and one case where the procedure leaves a question unanswered. Do not supply missing approval rules. Keep the language suitable for someone's first week.

One scenario could read: “Three boxes arrive, and two contain damaged folders. You have photographed the damage. What should happen next?” The answer guide should point to recording the purchase-order number and damaged quantity, moving affected goods to the hold area and notifying purchasing with the log reference.

Another could ask: “An urgent customer order needs those folders today. Can you release the damaged stock?” The useful answer preserves the instruction not to put damaged items into available stock, then identifies the unanswered urgent-order question for the process owner. It should not invent permission to release them or a substitute approval chain.

The supervisor checks the answer guide before using it, then asks the colleague to explain their choice. This turns generated material into a conversation about the actual work. If the colleague spots that an instruction is unclear, improve the procedure too. The exercise can reveal a documentation gap rather than teaching someone to memorise it.

## Watch Out: Confidentiality and Company Policy

### Check the Information and the Recipient

“Can AI summarise this?” is only part of the decision. Before supplying work material, ask: “Am I allowed to give this information to this system for this purpose?”

The task may be perfectly sensible. The information may require a different tool or account. A customer complaint could be appropriate inside an approved support system and inappropriate in an employee's personal chatbot account.

Think beyond names and email addresses. Work information can include customer histories, employee records, contracts, unpublished financial figures, negotiating positions, internal strategy, source code, unreleased products and incident details. Passwords, access keys and other credentials do not belong in an ordinary drafting request. Neither does an entire customer file when a fictional description would explain the problem.

Removing a name is not an automatic solution. “The only payroll officer in our three-person regional office who took leave on these dates” may still identify someone. A supplier's confidential pricing can remain commercially sensitive even when no person is identifiable. Australian privacy guidance treats de-identification as a contextual exercise involving re-identification risk, rather than simply deleting direct identifiers.[^ch7-deidentification]

Use the minimum information that does the job, within policy. To learn a formula, provide invented rows with the same relevant structure. To improve a generic job advertisement, provide the approved role requirements. To summarise a real support case, use the approved environment for that case. Replacing names in a prohibited upload does not turn it into an approved one.

Australia's privacy regulator recommends that organisations assess commercially available AI products, including who can access personal information and how oversight will work. Its best-practice guidance advises against entering personal information, especially sensitive information, into publicly available generative AI tools.[^ch7-privacy] This is Australian guidance, not a universal legal rule for every reader. It supports the practical habit of checking the information and the system together.

### The Account Matters as Much as the Brand

A brand name does not tell you enough about data handling. A personal account, an organisation-managed workspace and a connected workplace application can have different terms and controls.

Separate several questions: is information used for model training; is it retained; who can access it; what can connected services receive; and what settings or contractual protections apply? Paying for an individual subscription does not answer those questions or establish employer approval.

Microsoft's documentation for its organisational Copilot service provides a concrete example. It says prompts, responses and data accessed through Microsoft Graph are not used to train foundation language models. It also describes stored interaction history and administrative retention and search controls.[^ch7-copilot-privacy] “Not used for training” therefore does not mean “not stored”. These are vendor-described arrangements for specified products, not an independent safety assessment or a promise about every Copilot-branded service.

You do not need to become a procurement specialist. Find the approved account and the information categories your organisation permits there. If a tool offers connections to mail, files or other apps, check that those connections are also permitted. Convenience can change how much information the assistant can reach.

### Find the Actual Rule

A website loading on your work computer does not establish approval. Neither does a colleague using it or the absence of a visible ban.

Look for the approved tools, permitted information, review requirements and any rules about disclosure or recordkeeping. A useful question to the responsible person is specific: “May I use this organisation-managed account to summarise these internal project notes?” That is easier to answer than “Are we allowed to use AI?”

Public Australian Government staff guidance illustrates the distinction. It directs personnel to agency rules, distinguishes enterprise tools from public services, restricts sensitive and personal information in public tools, and retains human responsibility for outputs.[^ch7-policy] Those instructions apply to their stated government audience. Your workplace may have different rules, but it can make the same distinctions between tools, information and uses.

For a contractor, client agreements may matter. For a sole trader, you may be the person who needs to establish the rules, using the service terms and relevant professional guidance. If permission for real information is unclear, you can still learn with synthetic material while obtaining an answer.

> **Watch Out:** A paid account, a familiar brand and a removed name are not substitutes for knowing which information your organisation permits in the particular tool you are using.

## Watch Out: Verification at Work

### Check the Parts That Carry the Consequences

“Have a human review it” sounds reassuring until you ask what the human is reviewing. Spelling? Tone? Whether a linked paper exists? Whether a promised refund is authorised?

Choose the check that matches the possible mistake. For the report, compare quantities and status against source notes. For the meeting, check decisions, owners and deadlines. For support, inspect policy and commitments. For the spreadsheet, test the rule and reconcile important totals. For research, open the source and inspect the claim.

Consequences should set the intensity. A list of internal workshop titles may need only a quick read. A project update needs a factual comparison. A contractual statement, consequential financial figure or safety instruction may require authoritative evidence and a qualified reviewer. If nobody available can recognise a plausible error, narrow the task to preparing questions or organising material for someone who can.

This is also about time and access. A reviewer given five minutes and no source documents cannot perform the same check as someone who knows the material and can challenge the conclusion. Adding a person at the end does not automatically make a weak process reliable.

Research on critical thinking offers a useful caution. A survey of 319 knowledge workers, covering 936 reported examples of AI use, associated greater confidence in AI with less reported critical-thinking effort. Participants also described thinking shifting towards verification and integration.[^ch7-thinking] This was self-reported evidence, not proof that AI causes cognitive decline. It does challenge the comfortable assumption that a person looking at the output necessarily scrutinises it well.

Make review concrete before generating the work. Ask yourself which source will settle an important claim and who can resolve uncertainty. If the answer is “we will work that out later”, AI may be about to create a very polished research backlog.

### Apply the Workday Test

Once the result is checked, there is another question: did AI actually make the work easier, faster or better?

Count preparation, prompting, reading, correction, verification and any rework passed to colleagues. The thirty seconds spent generating text are only one part of the job.

Consider two fictional report workflows. Your usual update takes 30 minutes, including checking. With AI, preparing the notes takes six minutes, generating and refining takes three, editing takes five, and verification takes six. Twenty minutes for acceptable work is a ten-minute saving. If the draft instead needs 25 minutes of source reconstruction, the attractive first response has not helped the timetable.

The example's full time looks like this. These are illustrative timings, not a promised saving:

![Preparing, generating, editing and verifying the fictional update total 20 minutes, compared with 30 minutes for the usual workflow.](../diagrams/workday-test-completion-time.mmd){ width=48% }

The generation stage occupies only three of the twenty minutes. If verification grows to sixteen minutes, the total becomes thirty and the time saving disappears. That does not make verification wasted effort; it tells you the whole workflow needs to earn its place.

Quality matters too. A workflow might take the same time but expose a missing decision or produce clearer instructions. That can be worthwhile. A faster workflow that makes the next person's work harder may not be. The job-advertisement experiment showed why looking beyond the writer's time matters.

Measured research also warns against relying entirely on how productive the experience feels. In an early-2025 randomised study, 16 experienced open-source developers worked on 246 real tasks in projects they knew well. Allowing AI increased completion time by 19 per cent, although participants expected a speed-up and still perceived one afterwards.[^ch7-metr]

That narrow result does not establish that AI slows developers generally, let alone other occupations. It also should not be frozen into a verdict on newer tools. In February 2026, METR reported indications of greater benefit but said selection effects and measurement problems made its follow-up unreliable for estimating the current effect.[^ch7-metr-update] The durable lesson is to measure your workflow and be willing to change your conclusion.

Other workplace evidence shows more modest, targeted changes. A revised working paper covering a six-month experiment with 7,137 workers across 66 firms reported that treated workers who used the integrated assistant spent about two fewer hours per week on email in the later period. The researchers did not detect broader changes in the quantity or composition of tasks from providing individuals with AI.[^ch7-workpatterns] Some authors worked for Microsoft, whose tool was studied. Saving time on email can be useful without proving an entire job has been transformed.

For your own trial, choose one recurring task and note the normal completion time and standard of work. Try AI on several comparable instances. Record the full time and any important corrections. This is a practical comparison, not a controlled experiment: task difficulty and your familiarity will vary. It is still better than judging usefulness by the speed at which paragraphs appear.

> **Try This:** Choose a recurring work task and record the minutes spent preparing, generating, editing and checking. Add any rework discovered later. Compare the total and quality with your usual approach. Keep the parts that help; change or drop the parts that add work.

If the tool keeps producing more checking than usable work, change its role. Ask for an outline instead of a finished report, questions instead of conclusions, or an explanation instead of an answer. You can also stop using it for that task. A tool does not need to help everywhere to earn its place somewhere.

### Keep Understanding Within Reach

Chapter 6 distinguished getting a task finished from learning how to do it. At work, both goals still matter.

If you are learning spreadsheet analysis, ask the AI to explain a formula, predict a test result yourself and compare. If you are improving report writing, ask why the revised structure helps the audience. If a procedure is unfamiliar, ask for questions to take to its owner. These interactions can leave you better prepared for the next task.

The support study found evidence consistent with learning, particularly among less experienced workers.[^ch7-support] That does not mean every assisted task builds lasting skill. Sending work you cannot explain is a different pattern from examining the reasoning, checking the source and choosing what to keep.

You do not need to recreate every tool manually. An accountant does not have to abandon spreadsheets to prove competence. The practical standard is whether you can explain the result, recognise when it needs help and justify the decision to use it. If assistance removes tedious formatting, welcome it. If it conceals a gap in understanding that matters to the work, investigate that gap.

::: {.author-reflection}

One of the genuinely useful ways I have used AI at work has been preparing a presentation.

I already had the material. That was not the problem.

The problem was that the material existed in the usual professional state of affairs: facts scattered across documents, notes, technical information, background that mattered to me but probably not to the audience, and far too many things competing for the privilege of appearing on a PowerPoint slide.

Given enough time, I could have worked through all of that manually.

Instead, I gave the relevant material to AI, explained who I was presenting to, what I needed them to understand and what I wanted them to leave the room knowing.

The useful part was not simply, "make me a PowerPoint."

It helped organise the facts into a sensible sequence.

It identified which information was supporting detail rather than the message itself. It suggested where a call-out would make an important point visible instead of burying it somewhere in bullet number seven. It helped reduce several dense explanations into something somebody could actually absorb while sitting in a meeting rather than studying for an exam.

And it was quite good at identifying some of my garbage.

There is always garbage.

The technical detail that is completely fascinating to the person who spent three days investigating it and completely irrelevant to everybody else in the room. The paragraph that exists because I know the history but does absolutely nothing to move the presentation forward. The slide containing seventeen facts when the audience really needs three.

AI was useful as another set of eyes asking, in effect, "What are you actually trying to tell these people?"

That saved me a significant amount of preparation time.

It also formatted the deck into something clean and presentable. The hierarchy was sensible, the slides were consistent and the basic visual structure was already there. I still went through afterwards and tidied it up because apparently I am physically incapable of leaving a PowerPoint presentation alone once I have noticed that one text box is three pixels out of place.

But the heavy lifting was done.

The important part came next.

I checked the facts.

I went back through the source material, checked the numbers, removed statements that were stronger than the evidence supported and rewrote anything that sounded impressive but did not actually say what I meant.

A few things looked perfectly reasonable on the slide until I compared them with the underlying information.

That is where the time saving could easily have disappeared.

If I had needed to reconstruct every claim from scratch because the AI had produced a beautifully formatted collection of bullshit, I would have been better off making the presentation myself.

In this case, though, the balance worked.

The preparation, structuring and formatting were substantially faster, and the verification and tidy-up were manageable.

The other benefit surprised me more.

Because the slides had been reduced to the things that actually mattered, I did not need cue cards.

The presentation itself became my prompt.

Each slide gave me enough structure to know where I was in the argument, what point I needed to make and where I was going next, without putting paragraphs on the screen and reading them aloud like everybody in the room had suddenly forgotten how literacy worked.

That let me spend more time looking at the people I was talking to and less time looking down at notes.

I could explain the material rather than recite it.

And because I knew the underlying information, I could move away from the slide when somebody asked a question, then come back to it without losing the thread.

That is probably the best description of what AI contributed.

It did not give the presentation.

It helped me turn a pile of information into a structure that made it easier for **me** to give the presentation.

The facts still needed checking.

The judgement about what mattered was still mine.

And when I was standing in front of the room, the AI was nowhere to be seen.

Which, for that particular job, was exactly where I wanted it.

:::

## Myth vs Reality

**“AI at work is mainly for technical people.”** The examples here involve administration, support, management, finance, marketing and small business. Explaining your work clearly is often more relevant than knowing how to code.

**“AI automatically saves time.”** Fast generation may be followed by slow repair. Judge the completed task and its usefulness to the next person.

**“Professional-looking output is ready to send.”** A polished paragraph can still change a proposal into a decision, a review into a refund or a target into a promise. Check the meaning carried by those small words.

**“I pay for the account, so work information is fine.”** Payment establishes a subscription. Approval depends on the specific service, account, information and organisational rules.

**“Using AI means automating the whole job.”** You can use it for a paragraph, an action list or an explanation. Keeping assistance small can make its value easier to see and its errors easier to catch.

## Core Takeaway

AI can multiply what you get from information and expertise you already have. Give it a useful task, appropriate source material and a clear destination. Keep missing facts visible, check what matters and decide whether the finished work is better for having used it.

> **Recap:** The most useful professional habit is knowing which part to hand over and which part requires your judgement.
