# Chapter 10 — Will AI Replace Jobs?

## The Question Everyone Actually Wants Answered

In August 2026, a group of economists at Stanford University updated a study of payroll records covering millions of American workers. The study was built to answer a simple question: is AI already taking people's jobs?

Their answer contained two findings that seem to pull in opposite directions. The first was that they could find no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement. The second was that employment among workers aged 22 to 25, in the occupations most exposed to AI, was about 19 per cent below where it would otherwise have been expected to be. Older workers in the same occupations showed no comparable gap.[^ch10-canaries]

If you only read one half of that, you could write a headline either way. "AI is not taking jobs, says Stanford." Or: "AI is already shutting young people out of work." Both headlines would be quoting the same paper. Both would be misleading.

That is a good place to begin, because it shows why this chapter's question is so hard to answer with a yes or a no. You have probably heard both answers already. One says AI will take most jobs within a few years. The other says technology always creates more jobs than it destroys, so there is nothing to worry about. Both are slogans, and slogans are a poor basis for decisions about your career, your children's education or whether to start that apprenticeship.

Chapter 9 looked at problems with AI that deserve to be taken seriously. This chapter takes the employment question just as seriously, which means it will not simply reassure you. It will also not try to frighten you. What it will do is show you what is actually known, as of late 2026, what is still genuinely uncertain, why respectable experts publish numbers that seem to contradict each other, and how you can look at your own work more clearly than any headline can.

Here is the short version, so you know where we are heading. AI mostly meets the world of work **task by task**, not job by job. Some tasks are being taken over. Many more are being changed. Whether that adds up to fewer jobs, different jobs or more jobs depends on things that have little to do with how clever the technology is: whether employers adopt it, how they redesign the work, whether demand for the work grows, and who bears the costs of the change. The evidence so far shows real, concentrated effects on some groups and markets, and no economy-wide collapse in employment. Both of those are worth understanding properly.

> **Key Idea:** People using AI will often outperform people refusing to use it. The word "often" matters. The advantage is real where AI performs well on the task, is appropriate to use and can be checked properly. It is not universal, and it is not the same thing as job security.

That Key Idea is worth testing rather than accepting, and we will test it later in the chapter. First, we need some better tools for thinking.

### A Job Is a Bundle of Tasks

Think about what a real job involves across a normal week. Take a receptionist at a busy medical practice. They answer the phone, book and reschedule appointments, send reminders, check patients in, take payments, chase up referrals, update records, calm down an anxious parent, notice that an elderly regular seems more confused than usual and quietly mention it to the doctor, and decide which caller needs to be squeezed in today. That is one job title and at least a dozen distinct tasks.

Now think about what AI can currently do with those tasks. Booking and reminders can already be automated with ordinary software, and AI can make that software better at understanding requests. Drafting letters and summarising referrals can be AI-assisted. Calming the anxious parent, noticing the confused regular and deciding who gets squeezed in are a different kind of task altogether. They depend on being there, on knowing the people involved and on being trusted to make the call.

So "will AI replace receptionists?" turns out to be several questions at once. Some of this receptionist's tasks may disappear. Some may change. Some may become a larger share of the job. Whether the practice ends up needing fewer receptionists depends on what it does with the time saved. It might take on more patients, offer longer opening hours or let a receptionist leave and not replace them. The technology does not make that choice. The practice does.

This is the idea that holds the whole chapter together. A **task** is a single piece of work. A **job** is a bundle of tasks, plus responsibility, relationships, local knowledge and often physical presence. AI tends to arrive at the level of tasks. Its effects on jobs depend on how those tasks are bundled, reorganised, priced and managed.

### From "Could" to "Did": Exposure Is Not Impact

Thinking in tasks also shows how often public discussion skips steps. There is a long chain between "AI can do something" and "someone lost their job".

![The chain from AI capability to labour-market impact: exposure (AI can do or help with part of a task), adoption (an employer or worker actually uses it), workflow change, whether it helps people or means fewer people are needed, whether demand grows, holds or shrinks, and finally changes to hiring, hours, pay and job design. Each step can stop, slow down or reverse, so exposure alone is not job loss.](../diagrams/ai-capability-to-labour-impact.mmd){ width=34% }

The first link is **exposure**: AI's abilities overlap with some of the tasks a job involves. Exposure is about what the technology could plausibly touch, not whether anyone will use it. The second is **adoption**: are employers or workers actually using it for that task? Plenty of technically possible things are never adopted, because they cost too much to set up, are not trusted, are not allowed or are not what customers want. The third is the **workflow**: when the technology is used, does a person still do the task with help, or does it now happen without them? The fourth, which we come back to later, is whether AI is **helping people or replacing their contribution**. The fifth is **demand**: if the work becomes cheaper or faster, do customers want more of it, the same or less? Only at the end of that chain do you reach a **labour-market impact**, meaning changes in hiring, hours, wages, the number of positions or what the jobs involve.

Every link can break. A highly exposed task may never be adopted. An adopted tool may make workers more productive without reducing anyone's hours. A productivity gain may be absorbed by growing demand. The International Labour Organization, which publishes one of the most widely used measures of AI exposure, issued a brief in April 2026 specifically warning that exposure measures "should not be interpreted, on their own, as predictions of job losses or labour market outcomes".[^ch10-ilo-brief] When the organisation that builds the measure tells you not to read it as a job-loss forecast, that is worth listening to.

It helps to keep four words separate, because headlines constantly blur them:

| Word | What it means in plain English | Does it mean someone lost a job? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exposure | AI's abilities overlap with some tasks in a job | No. It measures what could be affected |
| Augmentation | AI helps a person do the work, and the person stays central | Not directly, though it can change how many people are needed |
| Automation | AI performs a task a person used to do | Not necessarily. The rest of the job may remain |
| Displacement | Demand for a person's work falls enough that a position or kind of work disappears | Yes, by definition |

### Reading the Big Numbers

With that chain in mind, you can make sense of the big percentages that appear in news stories. They are not all measuring the same thing, and very few of them are counting jobs lost.

The International Monetary Fund estimated in 2024 that almost 40 per cent of global employment was exposed to AI, rising to about 60 per cent in advanced economies like Australia's.[^ch10-imf] That sounds alarming until you notice the word "exposed". The IMF's own analysis went on to distinguish jobs where AI is more likely to complement workers from jobs where it is more likely to substitute for them. Its 40 per cent was a measure of overlap, not a forecast that four in ten jobs will vanish.

The International Labour Organization's refined 2025 index used a stricter, task-by-task method and found that about one in four workers worldwide were in occupations with some generative-AI exposure, and only 3.3 per cent were in the highest-exposure category. It concluded that, for most exposed occupations, transformation of the work was more likely than redundancy, because many tasks still need human input.[^ch10-ilo] Exposure was higher in wealthy countries, and women were over-represented in the highest-exposure group, partly because clerical work is highly exposed and is disproportionately done by women.

An earlier and widely quoted study, by researchers connected to OpenAI, estimated in 2023 that about 80 per cent of the US workforce could have at least a tenth of their tasks affected by language models, and about 19 per cent could have at least half their tasks affected.[^ch10-eloundou] The authors were explicit that this was not a prediction of when, or whether, the technology would actually be adopted.

Then there are numbers of a completely different kind. The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025* projected that, between 2025 and 2030, about 170 million roles would be created worldwide and 92 million displaced, a net gain of 78 million.[^ch10-wef] Those figures are often reported as "AI will create 170 million jobs". They are nothing of the sort. They come mainly from a survey of employers about what they expect, combined with modelling of broad trends that include technology, but also demographics, the green-energy transition and economic conditions. They are expectations, not observations, and they are not about AI alone.

The best teaching example is older than generative AI. In 2013, two Oxford researchers, Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne, estimated that about 47 per cent of US employment was in occupations at high risk of being computerised. That number travelled around the world for years. In 2016, researchers working for the OECD tried a different method. Instead of rating whole occupations, they looked at the tasks individual workers actually performed, recognising that two people with the same job title often do quite different work. Their estimate across 21 wealthy countries was that about 9 per cent of jobs were at high risk of automation.[^ch10-frey-oecd]

That is not a case of one study being right and the other wrong. They counted different things. One rated occupations as a whole; the other looked at tasks inside them. The headline number changed by a factor of five because the method changed.

So when you see "X per cent of jobs affected by AI", the most useful question you can ask is: **what exactly did they count?** In practice that breaks down into a handful of smaller questions:

- Does "affected" mean exposed, assisted, automated or lost?
- Is this something that has been observed, or a forecast or scenario?
- Did they look at tasks or whole jobs?
- Which country, and which years?
- What did they assume about how capable future AI will be, and how quickly it will be adopted?
- Who produced the estimate, and what might they want it to show?

That last question is not an accusation. AI companies, consulting firms, unions, governments and universities all produce useful research, and all have institutional interests. An AI company is well placed to report how its own product is used, but cannot tell you on its own what is happening to the whole labour market. A union is well placed to report what its members are experiencing, but a survey of members is not a national statistic. A consulting firm's scenario can be thoughtful and also convenient for a business that sells AI transformation projects. A government statistic is usually careful but often a year or more behind events. Knowing what a source *can* establish is more useful than deciding in advance whether to trust it.

There is one more category, and it is the most valuable one: **observed evidence**, meaning records of what actually happened to hiring, wages, hours and employment. That is the kind of evidence the Stanford payroll study provides, and it is the kind this chapter leans on most.

## Some Jobs Change, Some Disappear, Many Evolve, New Ones Emerge

There is a simple way to describe what is happening to work, and it has the advantage of being true: some jobs change, some disappear, many evolve, and new ones emerge.

It is worth being careful about what that sentence does and does not say. It describes four possible outcomes. It does not say they are happening in equal amounts, at the same speed, in the same industries, to the same people or in the same places. In fact they are not. Here is what the evidence shows about each.

### Some Jobs Change

The most common thing AI is doing to work right now is changing it without removing it.

Chapter 7 described one of the best-studied examples: a staggered rollout of an AI assistant to more than 5,000 customer-support agents. The assistant suggested responses and pointed to relevant information while the agents handled customer chats. On average, agents resolved about 15 per cent more issues per hour, and the least experienced agents improved most, in both speed and quality.[^ch10-support] The agents still did the job. The job itself had changed: less searching for answers, more choosing between suggestions, and a faster route for newer staff to the habits of their best colleagues.

A larger and in some ways more revealing picture comes from Denmark. Two economists, Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard, combined surveys of workers in AI-exposed occupations with Denmark's detailed national records of earnings and hours. They found widespread adoption: many employers had encouraged AI use, and workers reported that their tasks were being reorganised. New tasks appeared, including generating content with AI, overseeing AI output and working out how to fit AI into existing processes. Yet when the researchers looked at what had happened to these workers' recorded earnings and hours in the first two years after ChatGPT's launch, they found essentially nothing. Their estimates were precise enough to rule out average effects larger than about 2 per cent.[^ch10-denmark]

That is an important result, and it is easy to misread in both directions. It does not prove that AI will never affect Danish earnings. It covers a particular period, a particular country with strong labour institutions and the average worker in the groups studied. What it does show clearly is that **work can change substantially before the employment statistics move.** If you are waiting for national unemployment figures to tell you whether AI is affecting your industry, you will be told late. The change will already have happened inside the job.

The same pattern shows up in high-stakes Australian organisations that publish how they use AI. The Australian Bureau of Statistics, for example, reports using machine learning to help classify information, a generative-AI chatbot to help people with the Census, and generative AI for drafting, reviewing, summarising and transcribing meeting notes, with people reviewing the outputs.[^ch10-abs-ai] The Fair Work Ombudsman says AI may provide administrative support and insights in its own work, but that decisions about compliance, enforcement and people's entitlements are made by authorised staff who review and verify AI outputs.[^ch10-fwo-ai] Neither is a dramatic story. Both are typical of what "jobs change" looks like in practice: the work is reorganised around new tools, and human responsibility is kept where the stakes are high.

### Some Jobs Disappear

It would be dishonest to stop there, and this chapter will not. Some work is being substituted, and some people are losing income because of it.

The first thing to understand is what disappearance usually looks like. A job rarely vanishes from every employer on one day. Displacement tends to appear quietly: a position that is not refilled when someone leaves, a graduate intake that shrinks, a contractor who is not called back, commissions that stop arriving, two roles merged into one, or a whole category of small, paid tasks that buyers stop paying people to do. That makes it hard to see in the statistics and very easy to feel in a household budget.

The clearest early evidence comes from online freelancing platforms, where researchers can watch job postings change week by week. Chapter 8 described these studies in detail for creative work. After ChatGPT's release, postings for the kinds of writing and coding work most exposed to automation fell by about 21 per cent on one large platform, relative to more manual kinds of work used for comparison. After image generators spread, postings for image-creation work fell by about 17 per cent. The work that remained tended to be more complex and better paid.[^ch10-freelance] Another study found short-term falls in both employment and earnings for freelancers in more exposed occupations, and found that a strong track record did not protect people from the effect.[^ch10-freelance]

Freelance platforms are useful as early-warning systems because they are highly digital, prices adjust quickly and buyers can switch suppliers with a click. For the same reasons, they are not a good guide to the whole labour market. A 21 per cent fall in one category of online postings is not a 21 per cent fall in writing jobs. It is real evidence that some paid tasks were substituted quickly in a market built to react quickly.

Companies also provide evidence, of a different kind. The Swedish payments company Klarna launched an AI assistant for customer service in February 2024. In its annual report for 2025, filed with US regulators, it said the assistant handled 80 per cent of its customer-service chats that year, did work it estimated was equivalent to more than 850 full-time agents, and delivered about US$59 million in cost savings. It also reported that customers rated the AI-handled chats about as highly as chats with human agents.[^ch10-klarna]

That is unusually concrete evidence of automation at scale, and it is worth reading carefully. These are the company's own figures, from its own records and surveys. "Work equivalent to more than 850 agents" is the company's estimate of reduced workload, not an independently verified count of people who lost their jobs because of AI. A company also has reasons to present its use of AI favourably to investors. None of that makes the evidence worthless. It is good evidence of what one company says it did. It is not evidence, on its own, of what is happening across the economy.

That is a useful distinction to carry forward. When a business announces job cuts and mentions AI, the announcement tells you what the business says. Sometimes AI really was the main reason. Sometimes it was one reason among several, such as cost pressure, over-hiring in previous years or a change of strategy. Sometimes "AI" is simply a more attractive explanation to give shareholders. Treat every such announcement as a claim worth checking, not as proof.

### Many Jobs Evolve

For most people in most occupations, the near-term story is evolution: the job continues, but the bundle of tasks inside it shifts. This matches the conclusion of the ILO and of Jobs and Skills Australia, which we will come to shortly.

Consider an electrician running a small business. A language model is not going to crawl into a ceiling, trace a fault in a fifty-year-old switchboard or take legal responsibility for the safety of a new circuit. But AI can help draft quotes, write up job notes, explain a technical issue to a customer in plain language, organise the week's bookings, summarise a standard the electrician has supplied, or help work through a list of possible causes for an unusual fault. Some of that is genuinely useful. The core of the job, physical, skilled, accountable work in an unpredictable environment, is much less directly exposed to generative AI.

That does not make electricians "safe", and this book will not label any occupation that way. Robotics, better diagnostic equipment and changes in building methods are separate routes by which physical work can change. And the administrative parts of a trade business are often what decides whether it is profitable. But it does mean that for the electrician, "will AI take my job?" is a far less useful question than "which parts of my week could AI make easier, and which parts does my customer actually pay me for?"

A care worker in aged care is a similar case. Shift notes, rostering, summaries of a resident's records and reports to families are tasks where AI assistance is already being used or tested in some organisations. Physically helping someone out of bed, noticing that a resident is quieter than usual, judging whether that change matters and building the trust that lets a frightened person accept help are not. AI might take some paperwork off the care worker's plate. Whether that time goes back into care, or into a heavier workload, is a decision made by the employer and, in Australia, shaped by funding rules, awards and staffing requirements.

That last point is important, because "evolve" can be used as a comforting word to hide things that are not comfortable. When a job evolves, it may become more interesting, with less drudgery and more of the work people actually trained for. It may also become more intense: higher output expectations, tighter deadlines, more monitoring, fewer colleagues, less time to think, and more time spent checking a machine's work rather than doing your own. Chapter 8 described how, if ten design variants become cheap, a client may start asking for fifty. Evolution can benefit workers. It can also leave the same job title attached to a harder job. Both are possible, and which one happens is not decided by the technology alone.

### New Jobs Emerge

New work does appear. The economist David Autor and his colleagues traced new job titles in the United States from 1940 to 2018 and found that the majority of today's employment is in kinds of work that did not exist in 1940.[^ch10-autor] Think of software developers, sonographers, fitness instructors, logistics analysts and social-media managers. Technology creates new products, new industries and new problems that need people to solve them.

Something similar is happening with AI. The Danish study found new tasks appearing inside existing jobs: overseeing AI output, fitting AI into workflows, checking what it produces.[^ch10-denmark] On freelance platforms, demand grew for work that complements AI, such as building chatbots and machine-learning systems, even as demand fell for work AI could substitute for.[^ch10-freelance] And a September 2026 study of more than a billion job postings across 41 countries found that companies adopting AI tended to grow their senior workforce faster than their junior workforce, with suggestive evidence of modest overall employment growth.[^ch10-41countries]

Now the qualification that matters most. **New jobs appearing does not mean the people who lost the old ones get them.** Autor's research also found that the kind of new work changed over time. From 1940 to 1980, much new work was in middle-paid production and clerical jobs. After 1980, it was concentrated more at the top, in highly paid professional work, and at the bottom, in lower-paid service work.[^ch10-autor] New work can arrive years after the old work disappears. It can need different qualifications. It can appear in a different city. It can go mostly to younger people entering the workforce rather than to the 50-year-old whose role was cut. It can pay less. "New jobs will emerge" is true. It is not a promise to any particular person.

It is also worth being wary of betting a career on whichever new job title is fashionable this year. In 2023, "prompt engineer" was widely described as the job of the future. As AI tools became better at understanding ordinary requests, much of that skill became something many workers simply do as part of their existing job. Tool-specific roles can appear and fade quickly. Expertise in a domain, the ability to judge whether AI output is any good in that domain, and the capacity to keep learning tend to outlast any single title.

### The First Rung of the Ladder

One group deserves particular attention: people at the start of their working lives.

Here are two findings that seem to contradict each other. The first is that AI often helps less-experienced workers the most. In the customer-support study, newer agents gained most. In the writing experiment Chapter 7 described, where 453 professionals completed assigned writing tasks, access to ChatGPT cut average time by about 40 per cent and improved assessed quality by about 18 per cent, and lower-performing participants benefited more.[^ch10-writing] AI can act a little like a patient senior colleague, making the patterns of experienced workers available to beginners.

The second finding is the Stanford payroll study from the start of this chapter. Employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed US occupations had fallen about 19 per cent behind the trend for similar young workers in less-exposed occupations, while older workers showed no comparable gap. The difference appeared mainly through reduced hiring of young people, not through more of them being dismissed. And the declines were concentrated in occupations where AI was mostly used to automate tasks, not in those where it was mostly used to assist workers, where employment was flat or rising.[^ch10-canaries]

How can AI help beginners most and also coincide with fewer beginners being hired? Because those are answers to different questions. One measures how productive each junior worker is. The other measures how many junior workers an employer needs. If AI lets one graduate do the work that used to take two, that graduate may become more capable faster, and the firm may still hire fewer graduates. Both effects can happen at once, in the same firm.

This evidence should be read carefully. The Stanford study is observational: it shows a strong and consistent pattern in payroll records, and the researchers used several methods to rule out other explanations, but it cannot prove that AI caused every part of the gap. Economic conditions, interest rates and changes in particular industries also affect graduate hiring. It is US evidence, and the paper has been revised as new data has arrived. The 41-country study complicates the picture in a useful way: in AI-adopting firms, the *share* of junior workers fell mainly because senior employment grew faster, not because junior employment collapsed.[^ch10-41countries] A falling share and a falling number are different things.

Even so, the concern behind these findings is serious, and it connects to something this book has discussed before. Chapter 6 asked whether a tool is removing friction you do not value or practice you do. Chapter 8 noted that junior creative work, such as rough storyboards, simple graphics and first drafts, is both paid work and the way many people learned their craft. The same is true far beyond creative industries. Junior lawyers learn by reviewing documents and drafting first versions. Junior accountants learn by doing reconciliations and spotting what looks wrong. Junior developers learn by writing simple code and fixing their own bugs. Junior researchers learn by reading widely and summarising badly before they summarise well.

Those effort-heavy tasks are not just drudgery. They are often how judgement is built. If AI does most of them, a firm may become more productive this year. It still has to answer a harder question: how will the next generation develop the judgement that its senior people have now? Nobody has a proven answer to that yet. It is an emerging concern supported by early evidence, not a settled outcome, and it is worth watching in your own field.

### All Four at Once

Put these sections together and the apparent contradictions start to make sense.

Large productivity gains in particular tasks can coexist with little change in average earnings, because a task is not a whole job and a workplace is not a whole economy. Online markets can lose a fifth of certain postings while national unemployment barely moves, because a fast-moving corner of the market is not the whole market. Young workers in exposed occupations can fall behind while total employment holds up, because effects concentrated on one group are diluted in national averages. And an economy can adapt over time while particular people are badly hurt along the way, which is where history has a great deal to teach us.

## Historical Technology Shifts, Honestly

### What History Is Good For

Whenever AI and jobs come up, someone reaches for history. Usually it goes something like this: people panicked about machines in the Industrial Revolution, and about computers in the 1980s, and both times the economy ended up with more jobs and higher living standards, so stop worrying.

That story is not exactly false. It is just much too smooth. History is genuinely useful here, but for a different purpose. It cannot tell us what will happen with AI. It can show us the *mechanisms* by which technology has changed work before, including the ones the smooth version leaves out.

### The Luddites Were Not Afraid of Machines

The word "Luddite" is now an insult for someone who irrationally fears technology. The original Luddites, English textile workers who smashed machinery between 1811 and 1816, are a poor fit for that insult.

Historians who have studied the period describe disputes about much more than machines. New equipment and new ways of organising textile production were being used to undercut skilled workers' wages, bypass long-standing rules about how the trade operated and weaken workers' bargaining power, at a time of economic hardship and with little legal protection for workers.[^ch10-luddites] Their anger was directed at how the technology was being used, who was benefiting from it and who was paying the price.

The lesson for us is not that machine-breaking was right. It is that **people can rationally object to how a technology is deployed without being against the technology itself.** A worker who asks whether an AI rollout will mean fewer colleagues, more monitoring or a pay cut is not a Luddite in the insulting sense. They are asking a reasonable question about distribution.

### When Productivity Rises and Wages Do Not

The economic historian Robert Allen studied British output and wages during the first half of the nineteenth century. He found a long period, decades rather than years, in which output per worker rose while working people's real wages largely stagnated. The gains went mainly to profits. Only later did wages begin to rise in step with productivity.[^ch10-engels] Allen called this period "Engels' pause", after Friedrich Engels, who wrote about the conditions of English workers at the time.

Economic historians still debate the exact measurements, and nobody needs to accept every detail to take the central point. Rising productivity and rising pay for ordinary workers do not automatically arrive together. An economy can become more productive for a generation while the people doing the work see little of the benefit. When someone tells you AI will make the economy more productive, that may well be true. It does not yet tell you who will receive the gains, or when.

### Robots, Regions and the Firm That Grew

Industrial robots provide a more recent and better-measured example. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo studied US labour markets between 1990 and 2007. In the local areas where robots spread fastest, they found lower employment and lower wages than in comparable areas, with each additional robot per thousand workers associated with measurable declines in both.[^ch10-robots] Robots did not cause national mass unemployment. They did cause real, lasting damage in particular towns and industries, to people who could not simply move to where the new jobs were.

A related study of French manufacturing contains a twist that is worth remembering. Firms that adopted robots became more productive and actually *increased* their own employment. But they did so partly by winning business from competitors who had not adopted robots, and those competitors shed workers. Across the industries studied, employment fell.[^ch10-france]

That result teaches a lesson you can apply to almost any AI story. Imagine reading that a company adopted AI, grew fast and hired more people. That may be entirely true. It does not tell you what happened to the companies it competed with, or to the industry as a whole. **The level at which you measure success changes what you see.** A firm, an industry and an economy can each tell a different story about the same technology.

### The Costs That Do Not Show Up in the Averages

The most important historical evidence for this chapter is not about machines at all. It is about what happens to people who lose their jobs.

In a landmark 1993 study, three economists, Louis Jacobson, Robert LaLonde and Daniel Sullivan, used official earnings records to follow experienced workers in Pennsylvania who lost long-held jobs when their employers ran into trouble in the 1980s. Years later, those workers were still earning about a quarter less each year than they would have been expected to earn had they kept their jobs.[^ch10-displaced] They did not simply find an equivalent job and carry on. Many found work that paid less, used less of their experience or offered less security, and the gap persisted.

That study was not about AI, and it concerned a particular time, place and group of workers. Later research has found long-lasting losses after job loss in other periods too, especially when it happens during a downturn, though their size varies.[^ch10-displaced] The general finding is well established: losing a long-held job can cost a person far more than the months they spend unemployed.

> **Watch Out:** An economy eventually adapting is not the same as the people who lost their jobs being unharmed. Past technology transitions created new work and higher average living standards, but specific workers, often in specific towns and industries, suffered lasting income losses, long periods out of work, failed retraining, broken careers and family strain, even in transitions now remembered as broadly positive. When someone says "history shows it all works out", ask: worked out for whom, and how long did it take?

### The ATM Story, and Why It Is Not the Whole Story

You may have heard that when banks introduced automatic teller machines, the number of bank tellers in the United States actually grew. That is broadly true for a particular period. ATMs made each branch cheaper to run, banks opened more branches, and tellers spent less time handing out cash and more time on customer service and sales.[^ch10-atm]

That is a genuine example of a mechanism: automation of one task can lower costs, expand the business and increase demand for the remaining human work. But it is one mechanism, in one industry, over one period. It does not show that automation always increases employment, and later waves of banking technology, from internet banking to mobile apps, did not follow the same happy path for branch staff. If someone uses the ATM story as proof that AI will create more jobs than it removes, they are asking one example to carry far more weight than it can.

### Is This Time Different?

Some reasons to think AI could affect work differently from earlier technologies are worth taking seriously.

It is broad. The same general-purpose tool can touch writing, analysis, coding, customer service, design, research and administration across almost every industry at once. Earlier automation usually arrived industry by industry.

It reaches cognitive and professional work. Older automation mostly affected routine physical and clerical tasks. Generative AI overlaps strongly with work done by educated, well-paid people. "Only low-skilled jobs are at risk" was always too simple, and it is now clearly out of date.

It can spread quickly. Software can be distributed to millions of people overnight, and once it is set up, each extra use costs very little.

It is starting to perform sequences of tasks, not just answer single requests. So-called agents can carry out several steps of work with less human direction.

And, as the career-ladder discussion showed, it may affect how people learn their professions in the first place.

There are also good reasons not to assume an unprecedented catastrophe. Organisations adopt new technology much more slowly than the technology itself improves. Fitting AI into real workflows, with real data, real customers, legal obligations and existing systems, is hard, expensive work. Trust, regulation, professional responsibility and customer preference all slow things down. Many jobs bundle tasks that AI handles poorly. Lower costs can expand demand. And technical possibility has never been the same as economic inevitability.

So is this time different? Partly, probably, in ways that matter. History does not settle the question. What it does is tell you which questions to ask: who is adopting the technology, who gains from the productivity, which people and places bear the costs, how fast is it happening, and what institutions exist to help people through it?

## Augmentation vs. Replacement

### Two Words That Clear Up a Lot of Confusion

Most arguments about AI and jobs would be clearer if people agreed on two terms.

**Augmentation** means AI helps a person do the work, while the person remains central: deciding what to do, checking the result and taking responsibility for it. Most of what Chapters 6, 7 and 8 described was augmentation. AI drafts the report; you decide what it should say and whether it is right.

**Replacement** means AI removes enough of a person's contribution that human labour is no longer needed for that task, or for the role. Economists often use **automation** for the narrower case where AI performs a task a person used to do. Automation of a task does not necessarily remove the job, because the job contains other tasks. Replacement, or **displacement**, is when demand for a person's work falls enough that a position, or a kind of work, disappears.

Economists add a related pair of terms. AI is **complementary** to a worker when it makes that worker more productive or more valuable. It is a **substitute** when it reduces the amount of human work needed. The same technology can be complementary in one organisation and a substitute in another, depending on how the work is designed. An AI system that suggests answers to support agents is complementary. The same system answering customers directly, with no agent involved, is a substitute for that part of the work.

### When Helping People Still Means Hiring Fewer

Here is the part of the story that the simple augmentation-versus-replacement split misses, and it is probably the most important economic idea in this chapter.

Imagine a team of ten people processing insurance claims. An AI tool arrives. Nobody is removed from the process. Every claim is still reviewed and approved by a person. But with the tool summarising documents, flagging missing information and drafting letters, each person can now handle twice as many claims.

What happens next? It depends on demand. If the number of claims doubles, perhaps because the insurer grows, all ten keep their jobs and process twice as much. If the number of claims stays the same, the insurer now needs about five people to do work that used to take ten. It may not fire anyone. It may simply not replace the next four people who leave, and redeploy the fifth. But five fewer people will be employed in that work than there would have been.

Nothing was "fully automated". Every claim still had a human in the loop. AI was, by any reasonable definition, augmenting the workers. And labour demand still fell by half.

That is why "AI assists people, so it won't take jobs" is not a safe conclusion. **Augmentation can reduce headcount.** Equally, it does not have to. When a productivity gain arrives, at least five different things can happen, and often several at once:

- **More output.** The same people produce more, because there is more work to do.
- **Lower prices and growth.** The business becomes cheaper and more competitive, wins customers and eventually needs more staff.
- **Higher expectations.** Workers are expected to produce more in the same time, for the same pay.
- **Fewer workers.** The same output now needs a smaller team.
- **New demand.** Lower costs make something affordable that was not before, creating a market that did not exist.

A productivity number on its own cannot tell you which of these will dominate. A 30 per cent productivity gain could mean a growing team or a shrinking one. That is why this chapter has been careful not to treat the productivity studies as forecasts of employment in either direction.

It also leads to an uncomfortable but important point: **becoming more productive with AI does not automatically make your job safer.** It may make you more valuable to your employer. It may also make it possible for your employer to need fewer people like you. Being good with AI is useful. It is not a guarantee.

### What the Productivity Studies Do and Do Not Show

With that in mind, it is worth returning to the Key Idea from the start of the chapter: people using AI will often outperform people refusing to use it.

There is real evidence for it. The customer-support agents resolved more issues. The professionals in the writing experiment finished faster and produced better-rated work. In a study of 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group, which Chapter 7 described, those using AI on tasks within its capabilities completed more work, worked about a quarter faster and produced substantially better-rated results.[^ch10-frontier]

But the same consultant study found the other side too. On a task deliberately chosen because it sat just outside what the AI could do well, consultants using AI were *less* likely to reach the correct answer than those working without it. They trusted a fluent, confident answer that was wrong. The researchers called this uneven boundary the **jagged frontier**: AI can be excellent at one task and surprisingly poor at a neighbouring task that looks just as easy. That is why "does AI work for consultants?" or "does AI work for lawyers?" is the wrong question. The useful question is which tasks inside that work AI currently performs well.

And then there is the study that should give everyone pause. In early 2025, the research organisation METR ran a randomised trial with 16 experienced open-source software developers working on 246 real tasks in large projects they knew very well. When they were allowed to use the AI tools of the time, they took about 19 per cent *longer*. Before the study, they had predicted AI would make them about 24 per cent faster. Afterwards, they still believed it had made them about 20 per cent faster.[^ch10-metr]

That study is small and specialised. It does not show that AI slows down developers in general, and METR itself warns against reading it that way. It concerned experts in familiar projects, where their own knowledge was hard to beat, using tools from a particular moment. METR's 2026 attempt at a follow-up found hints of greater benefit from newer tools, but concluded that problems with who chose to take part made its new estimates unreliable.[^ch10-metr] The durable lesson is not about software. It is this: **feeling faster is not the same as being faster.** Perceived productivity and measured productivity can point in opposite directions, even for experts.

Put those studies together and the Key Idea survives, but in a more precise form. Where AI performs well on a task, is appropriate to use, and its output can be checked properly, people who learn to use it well can gain a real advantage. Where the task sits outside AI's abilities, where checking costs more time than it saves, where confidentiality rules or professional obligations make AI use inappropriate, or where doing the work yourself is how you learn, the person who declines to use AI may be making the better decision. The Fair Work Ombudsman, for example, warns that AI-generated answers about workplace law can be wrong, out of date or based on another country's law.[^ch10-fwo-ai] A worker who checks the official source instead of trusting a chatbot is not falling behind. They are doing their job properly.

There is one more puzzle these studies help explain. If AI makes particular tasks so much faster, why has it not shown up as a boom in national productivity? In Australia, the Productivity Commission reported in September 2026 that labour productivity was flat in the June quarter and had fallen 0.2 per cent over the year, and that productivity across the economy was barely above its 2015–19 average.[^ch10-pc] The economist Daron Acemoglu has argued that, under reasonable assumptions about which tasks AI can profitably perform, its total effect on productivity over a decade may be modest, well under 1 per cent by his estimate, partly because early experiments tend to test the easiest tasks to measure.[^ch10-acemoglu]

Both things can be true. A task can become 40 per cent faster while an economy barely moves, because only some tasks benefit, not every worker uses the tools, setting them up properly takes time and money, saved time is not always turned into more output, and organisations take years to redesign work around a new technology. Economic historians often point out that electricity and computers both took decades to show up clearly in national productivity figures. That does not prove AI will follow the same timetable. It does mean that "AI makes this task faster" and "AI has transformed the economy" are very different claims.

### How People Actually Use AI

Is most AI use augmentation or automation? It depends on how it is being used, and the answer changes faster than you might expect.

Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude AI assistant, publishes regular analyses of how people use its products. In its first report, in February 2025, it classified 57 per cent of conversations on its consumer chat service as augmentation, such as learning, iterating on a piece of work or getting feedback, and 43 per cent as automation, where the user simply handed over a task. By its September 2025 report, automation had edged ahead, at about 49 per cent to 47 per cent. By its January 2026 report, augmentation was ahead again, at 52 per cent to 45 per cent, which the company linked partly to new features that encouraged more back-and-forth.[^ch10-anthropic] Meanwhile, businesses connecting Claude directly into their own systems, rather than chatting with it, used it overwhelmingly for automation: about 77 per cent of those interactions in the September 2025 report.[^ch10-anthropic]

Two lessons stand out. First, the interface matters. A person chatting with an assistant tends to collaborate with it. A business wiring AI into a process tends to have it do the work directly. As more AI use moves from chat windows into systems that act on their own, the balance can shift toward automation. Second, these are one company's measurements of its own users, using its own classification method. They are useful evidence about how that product is used. They are not a measurement of the economy, and a single percentage from them should never be quoted as "how AI is used".

### What This Looks Like in Australia

Most of the research in this chapter comes from the United States and Europe, and it would be a mistake to assume it describes Australia automatically. Australia has different industries, a different system of awards and enterprise agreements, different vocational education, a large public sector and a high proportion of small businesses. Here is what the Australian evidence shows.

AI adoption by Australian businesses is rising quickly from a low base. In the Australian Bureau of Statistics' survey for the 2024–25 financial year, 12 per cent of businesses reported using AI, up from 1 per cent in 2022–23. Use varied enormously by industry: 38 per cent of businesses in information media and telecommunications, 24 per cent in professional, scientific and technical services, and 24 per cent in financial and insurance services, but 6 per cent in construction, 5 per cent in accommodation and food services, and 3 per cent in agriculture, forestry and fishing.[^ch10-abs-adoption]

Those figures tell you about **adoption**, the second link in the chain from the start of the chapter. They do not tell you how intensively businesses use AI, what they use it for or whether it has changed anyone's job. A business that uses AI to draft its newsletter counts the same as one that has redesigned its call centre around it. And the low figures in construction and hospitality are not a ranking of which jobs are safe. They are a snapshot of who was using AI in one year.

The most thorough Australian study is by Jobs and Skills Australia, the government's labour-market advisory body. Its Generative AI Capacity Study, released in stages in 2025, looked across the whole Australian labour market at exposure, adoption, how workers and industries adapt, and the skills system. Its central finding was that generative AI is more likely to augment human work than replace it, while adoption remains early and uneven, and effects differ across occupations, industries, regions and groups of workers.[^ch10-jsa] That is a valuable, locally grounded conclusion. It is also a study-level judgement based on exposure, adoption and case studies at a particular moment. It is evidence that augmentation is the more likely pattern for most Australian work. It is not a guarantee that no Australian jobs will be lost.

As for productivity, the Productivity Commission's figures above show that whatever AI is doing inside particular Australian workplaces, it has not yet produced a visible boom in national statistics.[^ch10-pc]

Finally, Australian workplaces are not an economic vacuum. Modern awards and enterprise agreements generally contain consultation terms, and enterprise agreements must include them. These terms usually require an employer to consult employees when it decides to introduce major changes, including changes to technology, that are likely to have significant effects on them. The Fair Work Ombudsman's guidance on consultation explains these obligations.[^ch10-fwo-consult] Exactly what applies depends on your award or agreement and on how significant the change is, and this book does not give legal advice. The broader point is that in Australia, an employer introducing AI is making a workplace decision inside a system of rules, not just installing software.

### Technology Does Not Decide by Itself

That brings us to one of the most important ideas in the whole debate. AI's effect on jobs is not determined only by what AI can do.

Acemoglu and other economists argue that technology can be developed and deployed in different directions: to replace workers, or to make workers more capable.[^ch10-acemoglu] Whether a business uses AI to let a support agent handle harder problems, or to remove the agent, is a choice. So is whether productivity gains are shared through better pay or shorter hours, or kept as profit. So is whether a firm keeps hiring graduates and trains them differently, or stops hiring them. Those choices are shaped by competition, costs, regulation, workplace bargaining, customer expectations and the values of the people making them.

That is not a reason for optimism or pessimism. It is a reason to stop thinking of AI's effect on jobs as weather that simply happens to us. It is closer to a set of decisions, made by organisations and institutions, that people can understand, question and influence.

## A Practical Self-Assessment

So far this chapter has been about the evidence. Now it is time to use it on your own work.

Rather than trying to predict the future of your industry, which no book can do responsibly, the most useful thing you can do is look at your own tasks. Not your job title. Your tasks.

Chapter 6 introduced a simple distinction between effort-heavy and judgement-heavy work, and Chapter 8 showed where it bends. It is a good place to start, with one adjustment. **Effort-heavy** tasks are dominated by producing, transforming, summarising, organising, generating options or processing information in repeatable ways. **Judgement-heavy** tasks are those where success depends more on context, accountability, unwritten knowledge, trust, relationships, high stakes or interpreting a situation nobody has seen before.

The adjustment is this: judgement-heavy does not mean AI cannot help. AI can already assist with comparing options, spotting patterns, summarising evidence, preparing for a difficult conversation and generating alternatives you had not considered. A manager can use AI to prepare for a tough performance discussion, even though the conversation itself, and responsibility for its outcome, remains theirs. So treat effort and judgement as two ends of a spectrum, and expect most tasks to sit somewhere in between. The distinction is a useful rule of thumb, not a law of economics.

Once you have sorted your tasks roughly, a few extra questions sharpen the picture considerably. You do not need to answer all of them for every task. Use the ones that seem relevant.

- **Is it repeatable?** Is the task done in much the same way each time?
- **Is it digital?** Is the information already in emails, documents, spreadsheets or systems, or does the task happen in the physical world?
- **Could you check it?** If AI produced the output, how easily could you tell whether it was right? Some things are quick to generate and slow to verify.
- **What if it is wrong?** Would an error be a minor annoyance, or expensive, dangerous or unfair to someone?
- **Does it depend on unwritten knowledge?** Does success rely on things nobody has written down: how this client really thinks, why this machine makes that noise, what this resident is like on a bad day?
- **Does it depend on trust?** Does the work only succeed because a particular person trusts *you*?
- **Who is accountable?** If it goes wrong, who answers for it, legally or professionally?
- **Does it need you physically there?**
- **Is it how people learn?** Is doing this task how a beginner in your field develops judgement?
- **Does AI actually save time on it?** After you count the time spent explaining, checking and fixing, is AI a real help, or does it create more work than it saves?

Instead of labelling a task "safe" or "unsafe", which would claim a certainty no one has, put each task into one of four groups:

- **Good candidate for AI assistance now.** AI may save time or improve quality, and you can check the result easily. Start experimenting here.
- **Possible automation candidate.** The task is repeatable, digital, low-stakes and easy to verify. This is the kind of task that may eventually happen with little human involvement, whether you use AI or not.
- **Human-heavy.** Context, stakes, relationships, physical presence or accountability are central. AI may help around the edges, but your contribution is most of the value.
- **Important learning task.** AI can help, but handing it over completely might remove the practice that builds your judgement, or the judgement of the people coming after you.

Here is how that might look for an aged-care worker. Writing shift notes and summarising records are good candidates for assistance, provided the organisation has approved the tool and the notes are checked for accuracy, because they matter clinically. Rostering may be a possible automation candidate, depending on how the organisation runs it. Physically helping residents, noticing small changes, deciding when something is worrying and talking with families are human-heavy. For a new care worker, writing their own notes for a while may also be an important learning task: it is how they learn what to look for.

For an electrician, quoting, scheduling and customer emails are good candidates for assistance. Fault-finding in a real building and signing off on safe, compliant work are human-heavy. For an apprentice, working through the reasons for a fault before checking with AI is an important learning task.

For a junior accountant, drafting routine client emails is a good candidate for assistance. Standard reconciliations may be a possible automation candidate. Advising a nervous small-business owner on whether they can afford to hire is human-heavy. And for a first-year graduate, doing reconciliations by hand for a while may be exactly how they learn to notice when numbers look wrong.

Notice that every one of those jobs contains tasks in more than one group. That is the point. Almost no job sits entirely in one category, which is why "is my job safe?" is a less useful question than "what is happening to each part of my job?"

> **Try This:** List eight to ten tasks you do regularly. For each, note whether it leans effort-heavy or judgement-heavy, then run through the questions above and place it in one of the four groups. Then pick one low-risk task that involves no confidential information and that you would be allowed to use AI for, and try it both ways over a week. Time the whole job, including explaining, checking and fixing, as in Chapter 7's workday test, and note the quality. You will end up with something more useful than a prediction: evidence about your own work.

This exercise will not give you a probability that your job will be automated. Any tool that offers you one, such as "your job has a 63 per cent chance of automation", is pretending to know things nobody knows about your employer, your industry, your customers and the next five years of technology. What the exercise gives you is a clearer picture of where AI could help you now, where change is most likely, where your value is concentrated, and which skills you should be careful not to hand over too early.

## Why Adaptability Matters More Than Any Specific Skill

### What Adaptability Means, and What It Does Not

When the future of work is uncertain, it is natural to look for a single skill that will protect you: learn to code, learn prompt engineering, get a particular certificate. The trouble is that specific skills, especially tool-specific ones, can lose value quickly when the tools change. That is why this chapter's practical takeaway is adaptability: the general capacity to notice change, learn, experiment and adjust as the tasks around you shift.

That word can easily become empty motivational language, so it is worth being precise about what it means here. Adaptability is not a personality type. It is a set of habits:

- **Notice which of your tasks are changing,** in your workplace and in your industry, rather than waiting for headlines.
- **Test AI on suitable, low-risk tasks,** and measure whether it actually helps, using the full time and the quality of the result, not how fast the first draft appears.
- **Find out your employer's AI policy** and the confidentiality rules that apply to your work, before you need them.
- **Keep your ability to check the work.** The person who can tell good output from plausible output becomes more valuable as output becomes cheaper.
- **Deepen your knowledge of your field.** Domain expertise is what makes AI output easier to judge and what lets you spot the jagged frontier.
- **Look after the relationships** that make people trust you with work: colleagues, clients, customers, residents, patients.
- **Watch hiring patterns in your field,** especially at entry level. Which junior roles are being advertised less? Which tasks are being bundled together? Changes in hiring often show up before layoffs do.
- **Notice the learning tasks,** your own and those of people more junior than you, and protect the ones that build judgement.
- **Stay able to learn new tools** without assuming any one tool will last.

Chapter 12 goes further into the durable human skills that sit underneath many of these habits. For now, the important point is that adaptability means doing things you can actually start this month.

Now for what adaptability does not mean. It does not mean that people who lose their jobs failed to adapt. It does not mean that retraining reliably leads to an equivalent job. And it does not mean that every worker has the same ability to change direction.

The displaced-worker evidence earlier in this chapter was about experienced, capable people. Many of them did adapt, found new work and kept going, and still earned substantially less for years. Workers do not control their employer's strategy, a downturn in their region, the cost of moving cities, age discrimination, caring responsibilities, disability, whether a course is affordable or whether an equivalent job exists within reach. A 55-year-old in a regional town with a mortgage and an ageing parent does not face the same choices as a 24-year-old in a capital city, however adaptable each of them is.

So the honest version is this: **adaptability increases the options you can control. It does not guarantee protection from structural change.** It is worth building because it improves your position, not because it makes you immune.

### Not Only an Individual Problem

That also means the responsibility for getting through this transition does not sit with individual workers alone. Employers decide whether AI is used to augment staff or reduce them, whether people are retrained or replaced, and whether junior roles are redesigned or simply cut. Education and training providers decide whether courses keep pace. Governments shape the safety net, the cost of training, support for people in affected regions and the rules about consultation and redundancy. Unions and professional bodies negotiate how change is introduced and represent workers' experiences. History suggests that the costs of a technological transition depend heavily on how well these institutions work, not just on the technology.

"Just retrain" is therefore not a complete answer, even though training matters. New jobs may need qualifications that take years to earn, be in a different city, pay less or prefer younger applicants. Retraining works best when it is connected to real jobs, affordable and supported while people do it. Those are mostly things individuals cannot arrange on their own.

None of this is a reason to wait passively. It is a reason to do the things within your control while being clear-eyed that they are one part of the picture. If you are worried about how AI is being introduced in your workplace, asking questions, understanding your award or agreement, and raising concerns through the proper channels are legitimate things to do. They are not the same as being against technology.

## Myth vs Reality

**"AI will take everyone's job."** Current evidence shows uneven effects on particular tasks, markets and groups, not economy-wide mass unemployment. Some work is being substituted, much more is being reorganised, and different people are experiencing very different effects.

**"AI won't take jobs, because technology always creates more jobs than it destroys."** History shows new work emerging over time. It also shows displaced workers suffering long-lasting losses, whole regions falling behind, and decades in which productivity rose without wages following. The economy adapting does not mean everyone is fine.

**"If 40 per cent of jobs are exposed to AI, 40 per cent of jobs will disappear."** Exposure measures how much AI's abilities overlap with tasks. It does not measure adoption, displacement or unemployment, and the organisations that publish exposure figures say so.

**"AI is already causing mass unemployment" or "AI hasn't affected jobs at all."** Neither fits the evidence as of late 2026. The most detailed payroll study so far found no economy-wide displacement, and Australian studies point to augmentation as the more likely pattern. But tasks are being reorganised, some freelance categories have lost demand, and young workers in highly exposed US occupations have fallen behind their peers. No national shock is not the same as no effects.

**"AI makes everyone more productive."** Strong gains have been measured on some writing, support and consulting tasks. Other studies have found people slower, or more likely to be wrong, when they relied on AI outside its abilities, sometimes while believing they were faster.

**"If AI only helps workers rather than replacing them, jobs are safe."** If each worker becomes much more productive and demand does not grow to match, an organisation may need fewer workers, even though nobody was replaced by a machine.

**"Only low-skilled jobs are at risk."** Generative AI overlaps strongly with professional, technical, clerical and creative cognitive work. Exposure is often highest in well-paid, highly educated occupations.

**"Trades and care work are safe because AI can't do physical work."** The physical core of those jobs is much less exposed to generative AI, but quoting, scheduling, documentation, diagnostics and customer communication can still change, and robotics is a separate path of automation. No occupation is simply safe or doomed.

**"New AI jobs will absorb the people who are displaced."** New work can emerge without going to the same people, places or age groups. Displacement and new opportunity often happen to different people.

## Core Takeaway

People using AI will often outperform people refusing to use it. Now that you have seen the evidence, that sentence carries its conditions with it. The advantage is real where AI performs well on the task, is appropriate to use and can be checked properly. It disappears, or reverses, where the task sits outside AI's abilities, where checking costs more than it saves, or where doing the work yourself is how you learn. And even where the advantage is real, being more productive is not the same as being secure, because productivity can lead to more work or to fewer workers.

Some jobs change. Some disappear. Many evolve. New ones emerge. All four are happening at once, at different speeds, to different people, and for most people the near-term story is change within jobs rather than the disappearance of whole occupations. The question to ask is not "is my job safe?" but "what is happening to each part of my work, who is deciding how it changes, and what can I do about the parts within my control?" Adaptability is the best general answer to that last question. It improves your options. It is not a guarantee, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

## Chapter Recap

> **Recap:** AI meets work task by task, and its effect on jobs depends on adoption, workflow design, demand and choices made by employers and institutions. Exposure is not impact, and the big percentages in headlines usually measure exposure, employer expectations or scenarios rather than jobs lost. The evidence so far shows large gains on some tasks, slower or worse work on others, task reorganisation that runs ahead of the employment statistics, real substitution in some online markets and companies, and early signs that young workers in highly exposed occupations are being hired less. It does not show economy-wide mass unemployment. Augmentation can still reduce headcount if demand does not grow. History shows new work emerging and also lasting costs for displaced workers and regions. Australian evidence points to rising adoption and augmentation as the more likely pattern, without guarantees. Before moving on, ask yourself a few questions. Which of your tasks would you happily hand to AI, and which are how you, or someone junior, learn the work? Where could AI genuinely make you better, and where would it create more checking than it saves? Which parts of your value come from context, trust, relationships or accountability? And if your role changed significantly, what would you need to learn next?
