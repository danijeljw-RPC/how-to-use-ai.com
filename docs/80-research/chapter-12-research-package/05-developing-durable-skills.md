# 05 — Developing and Protecting Durable Skills

## Core finding

The capabilities in Chapter 12 should not be presented as personality traits a reader either has or lacks. Evidence across education, workplace training and adult learning supports the idea that many component behaviours can be improved through practice, feedback and experience. The strength of evidence varies by capability, and short courses do not reliably create deep competence by themselves.

A useful chapter principle is:

> Build the skill by doing real tasks that require it, receiving feedback, reflecting on the result and repeating—not by merely consuming advice about the skill.

---

# 1. Deliberate practice and feedback

For judgement-heavy capabilities, practice needs a feedback loop. A person cannot calibrate judgement if they never learn whether a decision worked.

Practical pattern:

1. make a prediction, decision or communication choice;
2. record why;
3. observe the outcome;
4. compare outcome with expectation;
5. identify what was missed;
6. repeat under slightly different conditions.

AI can support steps 2–5, but it should not become the sole judge of whether the outcome was good.

---

# 2. Emotional and interpersonal capabilities are trainable to a degree

A 2024 preregistered systematic review/meta-analysis of workplace emotional-competency interventions included 50 studies; a controlled-trials subset included 27. Average effects were moderate and persisted beyond three months, but heterogeneity was high and study quality often low.

Source:  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11616120/

### Implication

The chapter can say emotional competencies are **not fixed**, while avoiding promises that a weekend EI course transforms relationships or leadership.

### Low-cost actions

- ask for specific feedback after difficult conversations;
- practise paraphrasing and perspective-taking;
- identify emotional triggers and alternative responses;
- volunteer or join group activities requiring coordination with different personalities;
- use role-play, including AI role-play, as rehearsal rather than substitute for real interaction.

---

# 3. AI as practice partner

AI has a useful role when it creates **practice opportunities** rather than simply completing the task.

Documented examples include language-pragmatics role-play where learners practised requests and refusals with ChatGPT. Results suggest AI practice can help, while peer interaction may differ in longer-term learning and conversational dynamics.

Sources:

- Sim, Kim & Ku (2025), *TESOL Quarterly*.  
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tesq.70010
- Eguchi et al. (2025), human-versus-AI interactional role-play.  
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tesq.70028

### Reader exercises AI can support

- **Communication:** role-play a difficult customer/family/colleague conversation, then compare the simulated response with feedback from a real person.
- **Judgement:** give AI your decision and ask for the strongest case against it.
- **Leadership:** simulate dissenting stakeholder positions before a meeting.
- **Creativity:** generate your own ideas first, then use AI to challenge categories or produce counterexamples.
- **Systems thinking:** ask AI to propose a causal map, then identify missing actors, feedback loops and assumptions.
- **Emotional capability:** rehearse wording, but do not outsource interpretation of a real person's emotional state to the model.

These are editorial practice ideas. The evidence for AI role-play is promising but domain-specific, not proof of universal transfer.

# 4. Adult learning and adaptability

OECD evidence shows adult-learning participation is uneven and depends strongly on access, employer support, relevance, flexibility and personal circumstances. This matters because "keep learning" can otherwise become empty advice that assumes unlimited time and money.

Sources:

- OECD (2025), *Trends in Adult Learning*.  
  https://doi.org/10.1787/ec0624a6-en
- OECD (2025), *Education Policy Outlook 2025*.  
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/education-policy-outlook-2025_c3f402ba-en.html

### Low-cost, realistic approaches

- choose one real problem and learn what is needed to solve it;
- use public libraries, government resources, open courses and vendor documentation where appropriate;
- teach another person what you learned;
- rotate between reading, doing and explaining;
- keep a small portfolio of completed projects rather than a list of courses;
- use community groups, volunteering and hobbies as practice environments;
- pair younger and older learners where each has different strengths or context.

### Older adults

Do not repeat stereotypes that older adults cannot learn new technology. Adult-learning participation and barriers vary by opportunity, health, prior education, confidence, work status and access. Age may affect learning conditions, but it is not a reason to treat capability as fixed.

---

# 5. Growth mindset: keep the useful idea, drop the slogan

"Growth mindset" is often used as shorthand for adaptability, but intervention evidence is contested.

Macnamara & Burgoyne's 2023 meta-analysis found very small average achievement effects and no significant effect in its highest-quality subset after publication-bias considerations. Burnette and colleagues reported benefits in some targeted/high-fidelity contexts.

Sources:

- https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000352
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36227318/

### Practical editorial conclusion

A belief that improvement is possible may help some learners, but it cannot substitute for:

- opportunity;
- instruction;
- time;
- feedback;
- supportive environment;
- relevant practice;
- material resources.

Chapter 12 should therefore define adaptability through behaviour: learning, revising, experimenting and transferring knowledge.

---

# 6. Systems thinking: teach through mapping and real problems

Educational reviews show systems thinking is taught through approaches including system mapping, simulation, interdisciplinary collaboration and community-engaged learning, although definitions and assessment are inconsistent.

Sources:

- https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09500693.2023.2245106
- https://www.mdpi.com/2079-8954/14/9/1115

### Low-cost exercise

Choose an ordinary problem—household spending, lateness, a recurring work bottleneck—and draw:

- actors;
- inputs/resources;
- outputs;
- incentives;
- feedback loops;
- delays;
- likely unintended effects of one proposed change.

Then ask AI to create an independent map and compare omissions. The learning occurs in the **comparison and revision**, not in accepting the generated map.

# 7. Communication: preserve unaided formulation

Because GenAI can turn a rough thought into polished prose instantly, readers may confuse polish with understanding.

Practical protection methods:

- write the one-sentence purpose yourself before asking AI to draft;
- identify the recipient's likely question or concern yourself;
- after generation, explain in your own words why each important claim is included;
- for consequential messages, read aloud and remove language you would not actually say;
- occasionally draft without AI to maintain fluency and reasoning practice.

The 2026 linguistic-diversity evidence adds a reason to retain personal editing: repeated LLM rewriting can reduce variation across texts.

Source:  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02550-0

---

# 8. Judgement: build calibration rather than confidence

Confidence alone is a poor target. A more useful skill is **calibration**: confidence that roughly tracks actual reliability.

Practice:

- assign rough confidence to a prediction;
- identify what evidence would falsify it;
- check later;
- keep a short decision journal for high-value recurring decisions;
- compare your unaided answer with AI and with the final outcome;
- notice domains in which AI routinely beats you and domains in which local knowledge matters more.

The goal is neither "trust yourself" nor "trust AI". It is learning **when each deserves trust**.

---

# 9. Leadership: real groups matter

AI simulation can help a person prepare, but leadership skill develops in conditions involving real commitments and consequences.

Low-cost practice environments:

- volunteer organisations;
- sporting clubs;
- community committees;
- family planning/care decisions;
- study groups;
- small workplace projects;
- mentoring or peer support.

Useful feedback questions after a leadership episode:

- Did people understand the goal?
- Did relevant dissent surface before the decision?
- Were responsibilities clear?
- Did I change my mind when evidence changed?
- What happened to trust after the decision?
- Did I take responsibility or blame the tool/data/team?

---

# 10. Creativity: preserve divergent search

If AI supplies the first ten ideas, it can anchor the search space. A simple protective method is **human-first divergence**:

1. generate several options unaided;
2. group them into categories;
3. ask AI for options from categories you missed;
4. combine and test;
5. choose based on explicit criteria.

This uses AI to widen rather than prematurely define the search.

Chapter 8 should remain the primary source for deeper creativity evidence.

# 11. Australian frameworks readers may encounter

## Australian Curriculum general capabilities

Relevant capabilities include:

- critical and creative thinking;
- personal and social capability;
- ethical understanding;
- digital literacy;
- literacy and numeracy;
- intercultural understanding.

ACARA emphasises development through subject content, supporting the idea that these capabilities are practised in context.

Source:  
https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/f-10-curriculum/general-capabilities

## Jobs and Skills Australia

JSA's GenAI work emphasises both digital/AI capability and non-digital capabilities in workforce transition.

Sources:

- https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study/our-gen-ai-transition-skills

UNESCO's 2024 AI competency frameworks for students and teachers combine human-centred mindset, ethics, AI knowledge/application and critical judgement. These are useful international corroboration but belong mainly at the Chapter 12/13 boundary.

Sources:

Article 4 of the EU AI Act introduced an AI-literacy obligation for providers/deployers, with rules evolving through 2026. This is useful evidence that institutions increasingly treat AI literacy as an organisational capability, but it is legal/regulatory context rather than a human-skill argument and should probably sit in Chapter 13 or the companion site.

# 12. A practical weekly pattern for ordinary readers

This is an editorial possibility synthesised from the research, not a validated intervention:

- **One unaided attempt:** do one meaningful task before asking AI.
- **One critique:** ask AI to challenge something you produced rather than replace it.
- **One human conversation:** practise explaining, listening or resolving a disagreement with a real person.
- **One decision review:** revisit a previous decision and compare outcome with expectation.
- **One new connection:** map how a decision affects another part of your work/life.
- **One learning step:** learn something outside the immediate task and apply it.

The value is the structure: continued practice of capabilities that otherwise risk being silently offloaded.
