# 09 — Editorial Opportunities

Everything in this file is an **editorial possibility**, not a factual claim or required change. The later chapter-writing system should choose only what serves the approved plan.

---

# 1. A stronger organising question

Instead of asking "What can humans do that AI cannot?", organise the chapter around:

> **When AI can do more of the task, what becomes more important for the person using it?**

Why this works:

- less vulnerable to capability changes;
- compatible with the effort-versus-judgement framework;
- allows acknowledgement that AI performs skill-like tasks;
- naturally leads to practical actions.

Where it breaks down:

- not every AI capability increase raises the value of the remaining human input;
- some human tasks may genuinely shrink or disappear.

---

# 2. Proposed comparison table

Potentially the most efficient addition to the printed chapter:

| Skill | What AI can already do | What the person still needs to do | How to protect/build it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Communication | draft, rewrite, translate, simulate tone | decide purpose, audience, relationship, final meaning | formulate purpose first; edit in own voice; get human feedback |
| Judgement | recommend, analyse, rank, compare | verify evidence, calibrate trust, weigh consequences, own decision | independent criteria; decision journal; primary-source checks |
| Leadership | plan, analyse, coach, schedule components | create commitment, handle conflict, set legitimate direction, own outcomes | lead real small groups; invite dissent; review decisions |
| Creativity | generate and recombine ideas | frame problem, develop taste, select/test | human-first ideation; seek non-AI inputs; experiment |
| Systems thinking | map/analyse relationships in supplied data | set boundaries, find missing context, anticipate side effects | causal maps; "what happens next?" twice; revisit outcomes |
| Emotional capability | generate empathetic language, infer likely states | regulate self, sustain trust, read unfolding relationship, remain present/accountable | listening practice; feedback; real interpersonal experience |

This table should not say these are permanent boundaries.

# 3. Judgement callout demonstration

The plan requires at least one concrete example showing judgement-heavy skill in action.

## Option A — Three plausible AI recommendations

Give a benign everyday decision with three individually reasonable AI-generated options—for example, resolving a scheduling conflict in a volunteer group. Ask the reader which is best.

Then reveal that the answer depends on context intentionally omitted from the prompt:

- one person has a care responsibility;
- one deadline is externally fixed;
- one option damages a key relationship.

Concept taught: AI can produce plausible choices, but judgement requires knowing which context matters.

**Important:** This would be an authored demonstration, not a claimed real case.

## Option B — Verification failure

Use the Australian legal example: generated authorities look plausible; professional fails to verify; regulator imposes consequences.

Concept taught: judgement is not "reading the answer and seeing if it looks sensible." It includes tracing important claims to evidence.

# 4. Reflection prompt required by plan

Possible structure:

Ask readers to choose:

- one skill they already rely on heavily;
- one skill AI is currently helping them with;
- one skill they may be practising less because AI handles part of it;
- one small action to strengthen/protect that capability this month.

Why it works: moves beyond self-esteem into observable use and possible offloading.

---

# 5. "Use AI without outsourcing yourself" practice box

Short exercise pattern:

1. **Try first** — produce a view/outline/decision.
2. **Ask AI second** — request critique, alternatives or missing assumptions.
3. **Compare** — identify what changed and why.
4. **Verify** — check consequential facts.
5. **Decide yourself** — record the criterion used.
6. **Review later** — did the decision work?

Concept taught: AI can be part of deliberate practice rather than merely a substitute for it.

Evidence basis: PNAS guardrailed learning, automation-bias research, critical-thinking survey. This exact six-step routine is an editorial synthesis, not a validated protocol.

# 6. Possible Myth vs Reality set

### Myth: Soft skills are what AI cannot do.
**Reality direction:** AI already performs many bounded tasks involving empathy, persuasion, creativity and social inference. Human value is better located in context, domain knowledge, relationships, verification and responsibility.

### Myth: Staying relevant means learning to code.
**Reality direction:** Technical skills matter in many jobs, but current workforce frameworks also emphasise analytical, social, adaptive and AI-literacy capabilities. The right mix depends on role.

### Myth: If AI makes you faster, it is making you better.
**Reality direction:** Immediate performance and durable learning can diverge.

### Myth: Keeping a human in the loop makes AI safe.
**Reality direction:** Humans can over-rely on wrong AI advice; meaningful oversight requires expertise and active evaluation.

# 7. Analogy — Autopilot with a qualification

**Concept:** automation handles routine operation while a human may retain responsibility for exceptions.

**Why it works:** readers understand that less manual operation can coexist with a need for skill during failure.

**Where it breaks:** aviation automation has highly regulated training, interfaces and failure procedures unlike ordinary GenAI. Do not imply direct risk equivalence.

Use Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" as the conceptual source rather than turning aviation anecdotes into proof.

---

# 8. Analogy — The calculator distinction

**Concept:** offloading can be sensible when the goal is solving a higher-level problem, harmful when the goal is learning the offloaded skill.

**Why it works:** immediately accessible to nontechnical readers.

**Where it breaks:** language, judgement and creative tasks are less mechanically verifiable than arithmetic, so AI errors are harder to detect.

# 9. Diagram possibility — Human contribution shifts rather than disappears

A small vertical diagram could show:

```
AI makes production cheaper
          ↓
More drafts / options / analysis
          ↓
Selection + verification + context matter
          ↓
Responsibility remains somewhere
          ↓
Human capabilities must be practised, not assumed
```

This is compact enough for a book page and avoids an ultra-wide diagram.

It is an explanatory model, not an empirical causal law; label accordingly.

# 10. Website interactive — "What are you outsourcing?"

A self-assessment could ask readers to mark tasks they use AI for:

- writing;
- summarising;
- decisions;
- planning;
- emotional conversations;
- learning;
- creative ideation.

For each, ask:

- Is the goal output or learning?
- Could you detect a wrong answer?
- What would happen if the tool were unavailable?
- Are you receiving feedback from the real outcome?

Return a non-scored reflection, not a pseudo-scientific "AI readiness" number.

---

# 11. Website practice — Role-play without pretending AI is a person

Offer benign scenarios:

- explain a decision to a sceptical colleague;
- ask a neighbour to change a recurring behaviour;
- give constructive feedback in a club;
- explain a complex topic to a beginner.

AI plays the interlocutor and then identifies possible ambiguities. The page should explicitly remind readers that simulated reactions are not evidence of how the real person will react.

---

# 12. Website practice — Systems map

Reader enters an everyday decision and fills five boxes:

1. What is changing?
2. Who/what is affected?
3. What feedback loops might appear?
4. What effect could show up later?
5. What evidence would tell you the intervention is working?

Optional AI step: generate a second map and invite the reader to find missing or unjustified connections.

---

# 13. Website practice — Decision calibration

Readers make low-stakes predictions with confidence ranges and revisit later. The purpose is to practise calibration rather than confidence.

No gamified score is needed; a simple history of predictions, outcomes and lessons is more educational.

---

# 14. Evidence box — "Immediate performance is not learning"

Use the PNAS student study to explain:

- unrestricted AI: excellent assisted practice performance;
- later unaided performance can be worse;
- guardrailed use produced a different outcome.

This could be one of the chapter's strongest evidence boxes because the distinction applies beyond school without claiming identical effects everywhere.

---

# 15. Evidence box — "AI can sound empathetic"

Present the JAMA study with its limits:

- AI responses preferred/rated higher in the study;
- text-only public questions;
- longer answers;
- not proof of felt emotion or better clinical relationship.

Then ask: **What exactly do we mean when we say emotional intelligence is human?**

This turns a weakness in the current draft into an intellectually useful moment.

---

# 16. Possible subsection — Protect the skill you still need

A short section after the six skills could synthesise the deskilling material:

- use AI aggressively for output where appropriate;
- identify skills you need to retain for checking/backup/learning;
- keep some deliberate unaided practice;
- use AI as critic/tutor sometimes, not always generator.

This would add genuinely new value without turning Chapter 12 into Chapter 13.

---

# 17. Personal reflection placeholder guidance

The approved plan requires a personal author reflection placeholder and forbids invented experience. A useful prompt for the author later:

- identify a real occasion when technical/AI capability was not the bottleneck;
- what mattered instead: context, judgement, explanation, relationship, prioritisation or responsibility?
- what would have gone wrong if the AI-generated or technically obvious answer had been accepted without that human input?

Do not fabricate the story; leave until the author supplies one.
