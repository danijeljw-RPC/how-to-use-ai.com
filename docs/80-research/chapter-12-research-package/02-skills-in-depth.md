# Communication

## Beginner definition

Communication is not merely producing fluent words. It is using language, timing, medium, tone, listening and feedback to create a particular understanding or outcome with particular people.

For Chapter 12, the useful distinction is:

- **text production:** generating grammatically plausible language;
- **communication judgement:** deciding what needs to be said, to whom, for what purpose, with what context, in what form, and what should be omitted or changed after seeing the response.

AI is already highly capable at the first category and increasingly capable at components of the second. That makes categorical language such as "AI writes; humans communicate" too strong. The durable capability is better framed as **contextual communication responsibility**.

## Evidence of value

Deming's labour-market research found strong historical growth in jobs requiring social interaction and especially in jobs combining social and mathematical skills. This supports communication as economically relevant, but predates modern GenAI.

- Deming (2017), *The Growing Importance of Social Skills in the Labor Market*.  
  https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/132/4/1593/3861633

The customer-support field experiment by Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond shows that GenAI can transfer communication patterns to less-experienced workers and raise their immediate performance. That means AI can **compress part of the communication-skill gap** rather than simply increasing the value of every communicator.

- Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, *Generative AI at Work*.  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

## What AI can already do

AI can:

- draft messages in many tones and formats;
- rewrite for clarity or concision;
- simulate audiences or objections;
- translate and adapt register;
- generate persuasive messages;
- produce responses that raters sometimes judge highly empathetic;
- role-play conversations for practice.

A 2025 *Nature Communications* study across three preregistered experiments (total N=4,829) found LLM-generated persuasive messages produced attitude change relative to neutral controls and performed comparably to lay-human persuasive messages in the study conditions. This is strong evidence against saying persuasion remains uniquely human, but it is still bounded experimental evidence, not proof of universal real-world influence.

- *Nature Communications* (2025), study of LLM persuasion.  
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-61345-5

**Recheck before publication:** capability results are model- and task-dependent.

## Source disclosure, sincerity and trust

Research indicates that recipients can react differently to identical or similar communication when told AI generated it. A 2024 study on source disclosure in AI-generated health-prevention messages found a modest negative bias against messages disclosed as AI-generated, especially among people with negative attitudes toward AI. Another experimental line on corporate apologies found AI attribution could reduce perceived sincerity and forgiveness compared with human attribution.

These results are useful because they show communication is partly about **relationship and provenance**, not just text quality.

- *Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans* (2024), "The effect of source disclosure on evaluation of AI-generated messages."  
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949882124000185
- Corporate-apology source-attribution study (2025).  
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0363811124000997

Limitations: effects vary by context; simulated messages are not long-term relationships; attitudes toward AI are changing quickly.

## Homogenisation risk

A 2026 *Nature Human Behaviour* paper examining seven datasets and more than 880,000 texts reported that LLM rewriting preserved central content while reducing variation in writing complexity and amplifying dominant linguistic characteristics. This provides current evidence for a risk that widespread AI rewriting can make communication more homogeneous even when individual outputs remain competent.

- Sourati, Karimi-Malekabadi & Dehghani (2026), "The shrinking landscape of linguistic diversity in the age of LLMs," *Nature Human Behaviour*.  
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02550-0

**Recheck before publication:** very recent evidence; the exact systems and usage patterns will change.

## Can communication be developed?

Yes, but the strongest educational framing is practice in context with feedback, not generic confidence-building. Useful methods include:

- write or speak before asking AI to rewrite;
- identify audience, goal and risk before generating text;
- compare several versions and explain why one fits better;
- practise difficult scenarios through role-play;
- ask another person for feedback on what they understood, not merely whether the prose sounded good;
- periodically communicate without AI so the underlying skill remains exercised.

AI itself can be a practice partner. A 2025 *TESOL Quarterly* study with 40 Korean EFL learners found ChatGPT role-play produced better short-term learning for requests in that setting, while peer interaction produced better long-term retention. The small, language-learning-specific study should not be generalised to all communication training, but it demonstrates both the promise and limitation of AI role-play.

- Sim, Kim & Ku (2025), "Exploring Generative AI as a Roleplay Interlocutor in L2 Task-Based Pragmatics Learning."  
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tesq.70010

## Misconceptions to avoid

- **"If AI writes well, writing no longer matters."** Clear writing can be part of clear thinking, prompting, evaluation and accountability; AI also makes weak writers' outputs look better without guaranteeing they understand or endorse them.
- **"AI-generated communication is always insincere."** Sincerity is a property of the communicator's intention and relationship, not something that can be inferred solely from tool use.
- **"Good prompting equals good communication."** Prompting is one narrow human-machine communication setting. Human communication includes listening, conflict, trust, timing and responsibility.

## Reader takeaway

Do not compete with AI at producing the fastest first draft. Strengthen the ability to define the communication goal, understand the audience, recognise what the draft misses, and take responsibility for what is actually sent.

# Judgement

## Beginner definition

Judgement is the ability to choose what to believe or do when the answer is not mechanically determined. It combines evidence, goals, domain knowledge, uncertainty, consequences and responsibility.

Different fields define it differently, but Chapter 12 does not need a single formal theory. It needs the practical point that **a plausible answer is not the same as a warranted decision**.

## Why judgement may become more valuable

The economic complementarity argument says cheaper prediction/output can increase demand for the complementary act of deciding what to do with it. More practically, generative AI can produce many plausible options cheaply, making selection, verification and application more consequential.

But this does not mean judgement is automatically preserved. Automation can make people less practised at the very task they must perform when the tool fails.

## Automation bias and over-reliance

A 2024 *Scientific Reports* paper reported five preregistered experiments involving 1,403 participants in personnel-selection tasks. Incorrect AI advice degraded decisions because people often failed to reject it; adding explanations did not reliably eliminate over-reliance.

- Cecil et al. (2024), *Scientific Reports*.  
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60220-5

A 2024 PLOS ONE experiment (N=292) found many participants preferred to delegate decisions to an algorithm even when human and algorithmic advice had equal accuracy. A human-in-the-loop option increased willingness to use the system, yet participants did not always intervene where intervention would help most.

- Sele & Chugunova (2024), *PLOS ONE*.  
  https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0298037

These studies support a concrete chapter message: **having final authority is not the same as exercising good judgement.**

## The ironies of automation

Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 "Ironies of Automation" remains relevant: automation can leave people responsible for rare, difficult abnormal situations while reducing their routine practice and situational involvement. GenAI changes the technology but not the underlying supervisory problem.

- Bainbridge, L. (1983), "Ironies of Automation," *Automatica*.  
  https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8

## Domain expertise is part of judgement

Judgement is not a general-purpose superpower detached from knowledge. A novice often cannot recognise subtle errors, missing evidence or implausible assumptions in a field they do not understand.

Tambe's work on AI workforce reskilling argues for combining domain expertise with algorithmic literacy rather than replacing expertise with generic AI skill.

- Tambe, P. (2025), *Management Science*.  
  https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.03968

This should temper any claim that "people can simply let AI do the technical work and provide judgement." The human needs something substantive from which to judge.

## Accountability: Australian professional example

The Federal Court of Australia's 2026 practice note on generative AI explicitly recognises useful applications but warns that AI can invent cases, citations and facts and states that people responsible for filed material must verify it. It makes accountability concrete: use of AI does not transfer professional responsibility to the tool.

- Federal Court of Australia (16 April 2026), *Practice Note: Generative Artificial Intelligence*.  
  https://www.fedcourt.gov.au/law-and-practice/practice-documents/practice-notes/gpn-ai

The Victorian Legal Services Board and Commissioner reported in 2025 that a lawyer's practising certificate was restricted after false AI-generated authorities were used. This is a particularly strong Australian example because the consequence attached to failure of verification, not merely to using AI.

- Victorian Legal Services Board + Commissioner (2 September 2025), "Statement — Mr Dayal matter."  
  https://lsbc.vic.gov.au/news-updates/news/statement-mr-dayal-matter

## Can AI improve judgement?

Yes. The research does not support a one-way "AI causes automation bias" narrative. Decision support can improve performance when the tool is appropriately calibrated and the workflow makes good use of it. Recent clinical trials show that AI support can improve diagnostic or management decisions under defined conditions. The lesson is **workflow design and calibration**, not "always ask AI second" or "always decide alone first."

## Practical development

- Make an initial assessment before revealing AI's recommendation when the task is important and you have relevant expertise.
- Ask what evidence would change your mind.
- Force yourself to identify at least one reason the AI answer could be wrong.
- Verify high-consequence facts against primary sources.
- Keep practising core tasks without AI periodically.
- Record decisions and revisit outcomes; judgement improves through feedback, not confidence.
- Learn enough domain material to recognise nonsense.

## Misconceptions

- **"Humans are the judgement part, therefore humans will catch errors."** Evidence shows humans often defer to wrong automation.
- **"More explanation fixes over-reliance."** Explainability alone does not reliably remove automation bias.
- **"Judgement is intuition."** Good judgement often includes structured evidence, expertise, feedback and explicit uncertainty.

## Reader takeaway

Judgement becomes valuable only if it is exercised. A person who rubber-stamps AI output is not supplying meaningful judgement merely because a human clicked the final button.

# Leadership

## Beginner definition

Leadership is the process of helping a group choose direction, coordinate action and deal with uncertainty, conflict and responsibility. It can be formal or informal and occurs in workplaces, families, volunteer groups and communities.

This is more useful for the book's audience than equating leadership with management title or charisma.

## What the current draft gets right

Direction-setting, priorities, ambiguity and accountability are reasonable leadership themes. However, "none of that is something AI does" is too categorical. AI can already:

- generate strategies and priorities;
- analyse alternatives;
- draft plans;
- coach or advise managers;
- monitor performance;
- allocate tasks in algorithmically managed workplaces.

The durable issue is that an AI system does not hold an organisational role with human/social responsibility in the same sense as a person, and people/institutions still assign accountability.

## Evidence that leadership behaviour can be measured

A 2025 NBER working paper used AI agents in a large preregistered laboratory experiment to test human leadership skill. Performance on an AI-agent leadership task correlated strongly with causal performance leading human groups. Successful leaders showed behaviours including asking more questions, more balanced conversational turn-taking and stronger social/decision capabilities.

This is valuable because it grounds leadership in observable behaviour rather than titles or motivational language.

- NBER (2025), "Measuring Human Leadership Skills with AI Agents."  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w33662

Limitations: laboratory tasks are not equivalent to long-term organisational leadership; working-paper findings require publication/status recheck.

**Recheck before publication.**

## AI and management evidence is still immature

A 2025 systematic review of top-management AI research found a fragmented literature covering adoption, strategic use and AI-related leadership capabilities. This supports discussing transition and capability-building but not declaring a settled model of "AI-era leadership."

- *Review of Managerial Science* (2025), systematic review.  
  https://doi.org/10.1007/s11846-025-00836-7

## Algorithmic management as counter-evidence

Software already allocates, schedules, rates and nudges workers in platform and other workplaces. This means some activities historically performed by managers are automatable. It also raises new leadership questions: contestability, fairness, explanation, trust and responsibility for rules embedded in systems.

The chapter can therefore say leadership **changes** rather than survives untouched.

## Relevance outside management

Examples suitable for the consumer-heavy audience:

- a family deciding how to care for an ageing parent;
- a volunteer group choosing priorities with limited resources;
- a sports team or club resolving conflict;
- a parent helping children set rules for AI-assisted schoolwork;
- a community organiser balancing competing stakeholder needs.

These are editorial scenarios rather than sourced cases unless tied to a documented example.

## Practical development

- Practise stating the decision and the reason separately.
- Invite dissent before commitment.
- Ask who is affected but absent from the discussion.
- Delegate analysis to AI but keep the decision criteria visible.
- After a decision, review both outcome and process.
- Lead small real groups—volunteering, clubs and projects provide practice AI cannot substitute for.

## Misconceptions

- **"Leadership is safe because AI cannot lead."** Too categorical; components of management and influence can be automated.
- **"Leadership means being the person with the answer."** Increasingly, useful leadership may be defining the question, coordinating expertise and accepting responsibility under uncertainty.
- **"Leadership only matters to managers."** Informal leadership appears in daily life.

## Reader takeaway

AI can supply analysis, suggestions and even management-like actions. The durable leadership contribution is coordinating people and commitments around decisions whose consequences someone must own.

# Creativity

## Scope boundary

Chapter 8 already owns the book's detailed research on AI and creativity. Chapter 12 should not repeat debates about art, authorship or creative industries. Its narrow purpose is **creative problem-solving as a durable capability**.

## Beginner definition

For this chapter, creativity is the ability to form useful new possibilities, reframe a problem, combine ideas in unexpected ways and recognise which possibilities are worth pursuing.

This definition deliberately includes **problem finding/framing**, not just idea generation.

## AI is already good at ideation

A 2024 *Nature Human Behaviour* paper reported five experiments in which GPT-assisted participants produced ideas rated more creative than comparison conditions on several everyday and innovation tasks, while effects differed by type of creativity.

- Lee & Chung (2024), *Nature Human Behaviour*.  
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01953-1

Therefore "AI cannot be creative" is not a useful Chapter 12 claim. The chapter's stronger question is: **what happens to human creative capability when generating options becomes cheap?**

## Human contribution that remains important

- deciding what problem is worth solving;
- bringing specific lived/domain context;
- setting constraints and values;
- noticing a surprising possibility rather than averaging toward the obvious;
- testing an idea in the real world;
- combining feedback over time;
- choosing and committing.

None of these should be described as permanently beyond AI. They are the current human responsibility within a broader process.

## Homogenisation and anchoring

The 2026 linguistic-diversity evidence provides a useful caution: AI rewriting can preserve content while narrowing stylistic variation. More broadly, AI suggestions can create anchoring—the first generated possibilities shape subsequent human search. Chapter 8 may already contain stronger research on this; Chapter 12 should reference that work rather than duplicate it.

## Protecting creative capability

Research-compatible practices:

- generate several ideas yourself before asking AI;
- ask AI for critique or counterexamples rather than the first idea;
- collect inputs from outside the model—people, observation, reading, physical environments;
- deliberately compare diverse options rather than accepting polished first output;
- retain hands-on craft practice in domains where craft itself builds taste.

## Misconception

**"Creativity becomes more valuable because AI only remixes."** This is too easy to challenge and unnecessary. The stronger argument is that abundant idea generation shifts attention toward framing, taste, selection, experimentation and synthesis.

## Reader takeaway

When ideas are cheap, the differentiator is less often producing *an* idea and more often recognising the right problem, exploring beyond the obvious and turning a possibility into something that works.

# Adaptability

## Role in Chapter 12

The approved plan is correct to use adaptability as the throughline rather than a seventh repeated section. Chapter 10 has already argued that uncertain change makes adaptability important. Chapter 12 should operationalise it through the other capabilities and through **continued learning**.

## Beginner definition

Adaptability is the capacity to change behaviour, strategy or knowledge when circumstances change. It is not merely being positive about change.

Related constructs include learning agility, cognitive flexibility, lifelong learning and growth mindset, but they are not interchangeable.

## Adult learning evidence

The OECD's 2025 work on adult learning stresses the growing importance of learning across working life while showing participation remains uneven and often stagnant. Employer support, flexible provision and relevance to concrete needs strongly affect participation.

- OECD (2025), *Trends in Adult Learning*.  
  https://doi.org/10.1787/ec0624a6-en
- OECD (2025), *Education Policy Outlook 2025*.  
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/education-policy-outlook-2025_c3f402ba-en.html

This is a better foundation for practical adaptability than exhorting readers to "embrace change."

## Growth mindset: useful idea, contested intervention evidence

A major 2023 meta-analysis by Macnamara and Burgoyne found very small overall effects of growth-mindset interventions on academic achievement and no statistically significant effect in the highest-quality subset after considering publication bias. Another 2023 meta-analysis by Burnette and colleagues found larger benefits in some targeted populations and high-fidelity settings.

Sources:

- Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023), *Psychological Bulletin*.  
  https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000352
- Burnette et al. (2023), meta-analysis indexed by PubMed.  
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36227318/

Editorial implication: do not reduce adaptability to "adopt a growth mindset." Beliefs may matter, but opportunity, practice design, feedback, resources and context matter too.

## Practical development

- maintain a recurring learning habit tied to real problems;
- deliberately enter beginner status in small areas;
- keep a record of what assumptions changed after feedback;
- learn from people of different generations/roles;
- choose projects that require a new tool or method but still use existing expertise;
- periodically practise without AI so new capability is internalised rather than merely rented from the tool.

## Reader takeaway

Adaptability is not predicting the next winning tool. It is keeping the capacity to learn, revise and transfer judgement as tools and work change.

# Systems Thinking

## Beginner definition

Systems thinking means looking beyond an isolated event to the parts, relationships, feedback loops, delays, incentives and side effects that help produce it.

A beginner-friendly contrast:

- **event thinking:** "Sales fell this month. What caused the drop?"
- **systems thinking:** "What changed across price, supply, customer behaviour, staffing, seasonality, marketing and feedback over the last several months, and how might these factors interact?"

The second question does not guarantee a better answer; it deliberately widens the frame.

## Important conceptual caution

"Systems thinking" is not one universally agreed psychological trait. Different traditions use overlapping terms, tools and methods. A 2023 systematic review of systems-thinking work in science education found inconsistency in how characteristics, skills and abilities were defined. A 2026 scoping review in public-health education similarly found varied definitions, pedagogy and assessment, with few validated measures.

Sources:

- *International Journal of Science Education* (2023), review of systems-thinking research.  
  https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09500693.2023.2245106
- *Systems* (2026), "Systems Thinking in Public Health Education: A Scoping Review."  
  https://www.mdpi.com/2079-8954/14/9/1115

## Classic explanatory source

Donella Meadows's work on leverage points is valuable for explanation: systems contain stocks, flows, feedback, delays and rules, and intuitively obvious interventions can produce weak or counterproductive results.

- Meadows, D., "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System."  
  https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/

This is a conceptual source, not an experimental demonstration that "systems thinkers outperform AI."

## Can it be measured or taught?

Measures exist, including the Systems Thinking Scale, but validation is domain- and instrument-dependent. Educational reviews suggest systems concepts can be taught through mapping, simulations, interdisciplinary and community-based work, yet evidence quality and assessment consistency remain uneven.

- Moore et al., "Development and Validation of the Systems Thinking Scale."  
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7403244/

## AI and systems thinking

The current draft says AI answering narrow questions may miss wider connections. That is sometimes true, but it is not a stable boundary. Modern systems can process long contexts, retrieve data, call tools and search for correlations. If supplied with the relevant sales, supplier and timing data, an AI system might identify the draft's supplier-change correlation itself.

The stronger human contribution is:

- deciding which system boundary is relevant;
- recognising missing stakeholders/data;
- asking about second-order effects;
- deciding which correlation deserves investigation;
- understanding values and constraints not represented in the dataset;
- accepting responsibility for intervention.

**Recheck before publication:** claims about AI's ability to reason over large systems will date rapidly.

## Everyday examples

Useful, non-corporate applications include:

- household budgets: a cheaper purchase can increase transport, maintenance or subscription costs elsewhere;
- family care: changing one person's schedule affects work, transport, schooling and respite;
- health: a treatment decision interacts with adherence, cost, side effects, family support and other conditions;
- community policy: a rule intended to solve one problem changes incentives and behaviour elsewhere.

These are explanatory scenarios, not empirical cases. They should be labelled accordingly unless grounded in a sourced case.

## Practical development

- draw a simple map of actors, inputs, outputs and feedback;
- ask "what happens next?" twice after any proposed change;
- look for delays between cause and visible effect;
- ask who benefits, who bears cost and who is missing;
- ask AI for alternative causal maps, then compare them with real evidence;
- revisit forecasts after outcomes occur.

## Misconception

**"Systems thinking means seeing the big picture."** Too vague. The useful skill is identifying relationships, feedback, delays, boundaries and unintended consequences, then testing those hypotheses against evidence.

## Reader takeaway

AI can help analyse a system. The durable capability is knowing what belongs in the system, what relationships need testing and which consequences matter.

# Emotional Intelligence

## Definition problem

"Emotional intelligence" (EI) is not one settled construct. Major approaches include:

- **ability models** — emotion-related problem solving measured through performance tasks;
- **trait models** — self-perceptions of emotional tendencies, commonly self-reported;
- **mixed models** — broader bundles incorporating motivation, social skills and personality-like characteristics.

A review of more than 30 measures highlights substantial differences in what instruments claim to measure and in reliability/validity. The chapter should therefore avoid writing as though EI were a single precisely measured human faculty.

- O'Connor et al. (2019), "The Measurement of Emotional Intelligence: A Critical Review of the Literature and Recommendations for Researchers and Practitioners."  
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6546921/

A useful beginner approach is to talk concretely about **recognising emotions, regulating one's response, perspective-taking, empathy, listening and relationship judgement**, with "emotional intelligence" as the umbrella label used by the approved plan.

## Empathy is also several things

Research commonly distinguishes:

- **cognitive empathy/perspective-taking:** understanding another person's likely perspective or state;
- **affective/emotional empathy:** sharing or resonating with another's emotion;
- **compassionate response:** acting in a helpful way.

AI can be evaluated on the first and on language associated with the third without thereby demonstrating felt emotion.

## The most important counter-evidence: AI can sound highly empathetic

A 2023 *JAMA Internal Medicine* study compared physician responses to questions posted on Reddit with ChatGPT responses. Three licensed health-professional evaluators assessed 195 question-response pairs (585 evaluations). They preferred the chatbot response in 78.6% of evaluations and rated it higher for both quality and empathy.

- Ayers et al. (2023), "Comparing Physician and Artificial Intelligence Chatbot Responses to Patient Questions Posted to a Public Social Media Forum," *JAMA Internal Medicine*.  
  https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2804309

Important limitations:

- this was a retrospective comparison of written online responses, not clinical care;
- raters assessed text, not lived emotional experience;
- chatbot answers were much longer on average;
- the study does not show that an AI feels empathy or can maintain a therapeutic relationship;
- it does show that "AI cannot be empathetic" is too crude if readers interpret empathy as observable response quality.

The current draft phrase **"stays squarely human"** should therefore be revised later.

## Theory-of-mind-style tasks

A 2024 *Nature Human Behaviour* study compared GPT and LLaMA models with 1,907 human participants across theory-of-mind tests. GPT-4 performed at or above human levels on several tasks but showed weaknesses on others, including faux-pas reasoning. This demonstrates sophisticated task performance involving inferred mental states, not evidence of consciousness or lived social understanding.

- Strachan et al. (2024), "Testing theory of mind in large language models and humans," *Nature Human Behaviour*.  
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01882-z

**Recheck before publication:** rapidly changing model capability.

## Relationship and accountability remain different dimensions

The chapter can make a defensible distinction without denying AI's performance:

- A model can generate a tactful response.
- A person in a relationship has history, obligations, reputation and consequences.
- A manager who delivers difficult feedback must observe what happens next and remain accountable.
- A carer, teacher, nurse or friend often contributes physical presence, continuity and reciprocal trust that a one-off text evaluation does not measure.

This is a **different criterion**, not evidence that AI cannot produce emotionally effective language.

## Can emotional capabilities be trained?

A 2024 preregistered systematic review/meta-analysis of workplace emotional-competency training included 50 studies and a controlled-trials meta-analysis of 27 studies. It found moderate average improvements that persisted beyond three months, but heterogeneity was high and methodological quality often low.

- *BMC Psychology* (2024), "Training emotional competencies at the workplace: a systematic review and metaanalysis."  
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11616120/

This supports a measured claim: components of emotional competence **can improve with training**, but the evidence is not strong enough to promise large, universal effects from short courses.

## Practical development

- paraphrase what another person said before responding;
- distinguish observed facts from your interpretation of their emotion;
- notice your own physiological/emotional reaction before acting;
- ask rather than assume what support the other person wants;
- role-play a difficult conversation, including with AI, then practise with real people where appropriate;
- seek feedback about the effect of your communication, not merely your intention;
- stay accountable after the difficult conversation rather than treating the right wording as the endpoint.

## Misconceptions

- **"AI cannot be empathetic."** Too broad: AI can produce responses rated highly empathetic.
- **"AI is more empathetic than humans."** Also too broad: text-rater studies do not establish superior relationships, care or emotional experience.
- **"EI is a scientifically settled score."** False; definitions and measures differ substantially.
- **"EI is innate."** Training evidence suggests at least some component capabilities can improve.

## Reader takeaway

Do not define human value by whether AI can write compassionate sentences. The human contribution is broader: recognising context, regulating oneself, building trust over time, responding to consequences and remaining present and accountable in the relationship.
