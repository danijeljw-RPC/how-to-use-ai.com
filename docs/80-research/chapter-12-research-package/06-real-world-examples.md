# 06 — Strong Real-World Examples and Studies

These examples were selected because they can do editorial work: make a skill concrete, challenge a simplistic claim, show a risk, or give the reader something practical to recognise. They are not presented as anecdotes to decorate the chapter.

---

## Example 1 — Social plus analytical skills in the labour market

**Skill:** Communication / collaboration  
**What happened:** David Deming analysed US labour-market changes from 1980–2012 and found employment growth shifted toward jobs requiring social interaction, with particularly strong outcomes for occupations combining social and mathematical skills.  
**Source:** Deming (2017), *Quarterly Journal of Economics*.  
https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/132/4/1593/3861633  
**What it demonstrates:** The strongest historical case is not "soft skills replace technical skills" but that interpersonal and analytical capabilities can be complementary.  
**Chapter fit:** Communication; introductory evidence box.  
**Reader usefulness:** Reframes the chapter away from a false technical-versus-human contest.  
**Print:** Yes.  
**Website:** Yes, with a simple timeline/skills-bundle graphic.  
**Practice:** Indirect.  
**Limitations:** US evidence; predates GenAI; occupational proxies are not direct measurements of individual skill.  
**Dating risk:** Low for the historical finding.

## Example 2 — GenAI raises novice customer-support performance

**Skill:** Communication / adaptability / expertise  
**What happened:** A field study involving 5,179 customer-support agents found GenAI assistance increased productivity by about 14% on average, with much larger gains for novice/lower-skilled workers and smaller gains for high performers.  
**Source:** Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, *Generative AI at Work*, NBER.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161  
**What it demonstrates:** AI can spread patterns associated with stronger performers and narrow immediate performance gaps. Some communication capability can become less scarce, not more.  
**Chapter fit:** Communication; contrasting viewpoint; skill-list nuance.  
**Reader usefulness:** Shows why "human skill automatically gains scarcity value" is too simple.  
**Print:** Yes.  
**Website:** Yes.  
**Practice:** No direct recreation needed.  
**Limitations:** One workplace/context and particular tool; productivity is not identical to underlying learning.  
**Dating risk:** Medium.

## Example 3 — The jagged frontier: consultants helped and hurt by GPT-4

**Skill:** Judgement  
**What happened:** In a study of 758 BCG consultants, GPT-4 substantially improved performance on tasks within the model's capability frontier, while reliance on it on a task outside that frontier could reduce performance.  
**Source:** Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), *Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier*.  
https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4573321  
**What it demonstrates:** Knowing **when** AI is competent is part of judgement. A polished tool can increase both capability and error.  
**Chapter fit:** Judgement; Myth vs Reality.  
**Reader usefulness:** Memorable reason not to treat AI as uniformly good or bad.  
**Print:** Yes.  
**Website:** Yes, potential interactive "inside/outside frontier" demonstration.  
**Practice:** Readers can compare AI on familiar versus unfamiliar tasks, with caution.  
**Limitations:** Specific model generation and consultant tasks; frontier moves rapidly.  
**Dating risk:** High. **Recheck before publication.**

## Example 4 — Humans fail to reject wrong AI advice

**Skill:** Judgement  
**What happened:** Five preregistered personnel-selection experiments involving 1,403 participants found incorrect AI advice degraded performance because participants often failed to dismiss it. Explanations did not reliably eliminate over-reliance.  
**Source:** Cecil et al. (2024), *Scientific Reports*.  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60220-5  
**What it demonstrates:** A human being "in the loop" does not guarantee meaningful oversight.  
**Chapter fit:** Required judgement example / Example callout.  
**Reader usefulness:** Directly challenges the idea that merely checking AI is enough.  
**Print:** Strong.  
**Website:** Strong; could support a benign decision exercise.  
**Practice:** Yes—show an AI recommendation and ask readers to state independent criteria first.  
**Limitations:** Experimental personnel-selection tasks, not every decision domain.  
**Dating risk:** Low-medium.

## Example 5 — Australian lawyer sanctioned after false AI authorities

**Skill:** Judgement / accountability / verification  
**What happened:** The Victorian Legal Services Board and Commissioner reported that a lawyer's practising certificate was restricted after false AI-generated authorities were used. Conditions included supervised employment and limits on practice.  
**Source:** Victorian Legal Services Board + Commissioner (2 September 2025), "Statement — Mr Dayal matter."  
https://lsbc.vic.gov.au/news-updates/news/statement-mr-dayal-matter  
**What it demonstrates:** Responsibility does not transfer to the AI system; professional judgement includes source verification.  
**Chapter fit:** Judgement; Australian example; Myth vs Reality.  
**Reader usefulness:** Concrete consequence, easy to understand.  
**Print:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Yes, with link to regulator and court guidance.  
**Practice:** Reader can practise tracing a citation to the primary source.  
**Limitations:** Legal profession has unusually formal verification duties; do not imply identical sanctions in all fields.  
**Dating risk:** Low.

## Example 6 — Federal Court of Australia formalises AI verification duties

**Skill:** Judgement / leadership / accountability  
**What happened:** The Federal Court's April 2026 GenAI Practice Note recognises potential benefits but explicitly warns of fictitious cases/citations and inaccurate information and requires responsible human verification.  
**Source:** Federal Court of Australia (16 April 2026).  
https://www.fedcourt.gov.au/law-and-practice/practice-documents/practice-notes/gpn-ai  
**What it demonstrates:** Institutions are turning "human responsibility" into operational rules rather than treating it as a slogan.  
**Chapter fit:** Judgement; leadership/accountability; Australian context.  
**Reader usefulness:** Shows what verification means in a high-stakes environment.  
**Print:** Yes.  
**Website:** Yes.  
**Practice:** Adapt as a generic "verify consequential claims" checklist without presenting legal advice.  
**Limitations:** Legal-specific; professional duties vary.  
**Dating risk:** Medium; practice notes can change. **Recheck before publication.**

## Example 7 — AI practice success but worse later unaided maths performance

**Skill:** Adaptability / learning / judgement  
**What happened:** In a randomized study of nearly 1,000 high-school students in Türkiye, unrestricted GPT assistance greatly improved practice performance, but those students performed worse than controls on a later exam when AI was removed. A guardrailed tutor produced strong practice gains without the same statistically significant unaided penalty.  
**Source:** Bastani et al. (2025), *PNAS*, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning."  
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122  
**What it demonstrates:** Better output today and better capability tomorrow are different objectives; tool design matters.  
**Chapter fit:** Adaptability throughline; deskilling; practical advice.  
**Reader usefulness:** One of the clearest examples in the package.  
**Print:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Excellent; could support a "answer machine vs tutor" comparison.  
**Practice:** Yes—attempt first, use AI for hints/questions rather than direct answers when learning.  
**Limitations:** School maths; one educational setting; not direct adult-work evidence.  
**Dating risk:** Low-medium.

## Example 8 — Possible professional deskilling after AI-assisted colonoscopy

**Skill:** Judgement / skill protection  
**What happened:** A 2025 multicentre observational study across four Polish centres found clinicians' unaided adenoma detection rate was lower after a period of routine exposure to AI-assisted detection than before that exposure.  
**Source:** Budzyń et al. (2025), *The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology*.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40816301/  
**What it demonstrates:** Repeated assistance may alter unaided professional performance.  
**Chapter fit:** Deskilling / supervision paradox.  
**Reader usefulness:** Shows the issue is not confined to students or writers.  
**Print:** Yes, with careful wording.  
**Website:** Yes, paired with counter-evidence.  
**Practice:** No reader recreation.  
**Limitations:** Observational; causality not established. A 2026 prospective study in inexperienced endoscopists did not find the same persistent deskilling pattern: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13552228/  
**Dating risk:** High. **Recheck before publication.**

## Example 9 — Knowledge workers report critical thinking shifting under GenAI

**Skill:** Judgement / critical thinking  
**What happened:** 319 knowledge workers described 936 real GenAI uses in a CHI 2025 Microsoft/CMU study. Greater confidence in AI was associated with less self-reported critical-thinking effort; users described work shifting toward checking, integration and stewardship.  
**Source:** Microsoft Research / Carnegie Mellon University (CHI 2025).  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/  
**What it demonstrates:** AI may redistribute rather than simply remove cognitive work; calibration matters.  
**Chapter fit:** Judgement; deskilling; practical skill protection.  
**Reader usefulness:** Directly relevant to ordinary office use.  
**Print:** Yes.  
**Website:** Yes.  
**Practice:** Decision/verification journal.  
**Limitations:** Self-report, not objective measurement of skill decline; associations are not causal.  
**Dating risk:** Medium.

## Example 10 — Chatbot responses rated more empathetic than physicians'

**Skill:** Emotional intelligence / communication  
**What happened:** In a 2023 JAMA study using 195 Reddit patient questions and 585 evaluator ratings, health-professional evaluators preferred chatbot answers in 78.6% of comparisons and rated them higher for empathy and quality.  
**Source:** Ayers et al. (2023), *JAMA Internal Medicine*.  
https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2804309  
**What it demonstrates:** AI can perform an empathy-like communication task extremely well by human ratings; "AI cannot be empathetic" is not an adequate explanation.  
**Chapter fit:** Emotional intelligence; Myth vs Reality.  
**Reader usefulness:** Forces the chapter to make a more sophisticated distinction.  
**Print:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Excellent side-by-side discussion.  
**Practice:** Could let readers compare anonymised original-style responses only if copyright and research-reproduction rules are respected; better recreate a new benign scenario.  
**Limitations:** Text-only, retrospective, public forum rather than clinical encounter; AI answers longer; rating empathy is not felt empathy.  
**Dating risk:** Medium. **Recheck for newer replications.**

## Example 11 — LLMs perform strongly on theory-of-mind tests

**Skill:** Emotional intelligence / social inference  
**What happened:** A 2024 study compared GPT/LLaMA models with 1,907 humans on theory-of-mind tests. GPT-4 matched or exceeded humans on several tasks but failed differently on others.  
**Source:** Strachan et al. (2024), *Nature Human Behaviour*.  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01882-z  
**What it demonstrates:** Some social-inference tasks once assumed strongly human can be performed by language models.  
**Chapter fit:** Emotional intelligence counter-evidence.  
**Reader usefulness:** Helps separate test performance from lived relationship.  
**Print:** Yes, briefly.  
**Website:** Yes.  
**Practice:** Possible simple false-belief demonstration, clearly non-diagnostic.  
**Limitations:** Benchmark/task performance; anthropomorphism risk.  
**Dating risk:** High. **Recheck before publication.**

## Example 12 — LLM persuasive messages change attitudes in experiments

**Skill:** Communication  
**What happened:** Three preregistered experiments (total N=4,829) found LLM-generated persuasive messages shifted attitudes relative to neutral controls and could be comparable to lay-human persuasive messages in the tested settings.  
**Source:** *Nature Communications* (2025).  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-61345-5  
**What it demonstrates:** Persuasion is not a safe "AI can't do this" category.  
**Chapter fit:** Communication; Myth vs Reality.  
**Reader usefulness:** Challenges an intuitive boundary without requiring technical detail.  
**Print:** Yes, cautiously.  
**Website:** Yes, discussion only.  
**Practice:** Do not turn into persuasion optimisation; use as critical-literacy example.  
**Limitations:** Experimental attitude measures, not universal real-world behaviour change.  
**Dating risk:** High.

## Example 13 — AI improves creative idea generation

**Skill:** Creativity  
**What happened:** Five experiments in a 2024 *Nature Human Behaviour* study found GPT-assisted participants produced ideas rated more creative than comparison conditions on several everyday/innovation tasks.  
**Source:** Lee & Chung (2024).  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01953-1  
**What it demonstrates:** Idea generation itself is increasingly AI-assisted; durable human creativity cannot be defended by saying AI only copies.  
**Chapter fit:** Creativity, kept short because Chapter 8 owns the topic.  
**Reader usefulness:** Supports a shift from "generate" toward "frame, select, test."  
**Print:** Yes, brief.  
**Website:** Chapter 8 cross-link.  
**Practice:** Human-first ideation followed by AI divergence.  
**Limitations:** Bounded creativity tests; model version.  
**Dating risk:** Medium-high.

## Example 14 — LLM rewriting reduces linguistic diversity

**Skill:** Communication / creativity  
**What happened:** A 2026 *Nature Human Behaviour* paper using seven datasets and more than 880,000 texts found LLM rewriting could preserve content while reducing variation in writing complexity and amplifying dominant linguistic patterns.  
**Source:** Sourati, Karimi-Malekabadi & Dehghani (2026).  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02550-0  
**What it demonstrates:** AI can improve/standardise individual text while creating population-level sameness.  
**Chapter fit:** Communication; creativity cross-reference.  
**Reader usefulness:** Gives a concrete reason to keep human voice and independent formulation.  
**Print:** Yes.  
**Website:** Strong visualisation opportunity.  
**Practice:** Compare original, AI-rewritten and personally edited versions.  
**Limitations:** Recent; effects depend on models/prompts/corpora.  
**Dating risk:** High. **Recheck before publication.**

## Example 15 — Leadership skill measured using AI agents

**Skill:** Leadership  
**What happened:** A 2025 NBER preregistered laboratory study used AI agents to measure human leadership performance and found the measure correlated strongly with causal performance when people led human groups. More successful leaders displayed behaviours such as asking more questions and facilitating conversational turn-taking.  
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 33662.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w33662  
**What it demonstrates:** Leadership can be discussed as observable coordination behaviour rather than an ineffable trait; AI can also be used as a measurement/simulation tool.  
**Chapter fit:** Leadership.  
**Reader usefulness:** Makes leadership specific.  
**Print:** Yes, if status remains sound.  
**Website:** Potential leadership reflection/simulation.  
**Practice:** Small-group facilitation exercise.  
**Limitations:** Laboratory setting; working-paper status.  
**Dating risk:** High. **Recheck before publication.**

## Example 16 — Human-AI teams do not automatically beat the best member

**Skill:** Judgement / leadership  
**What happened:** A preregistered meta-analysis synthesised 106 experiments and 370 effect sizes. Human-AI combinations did not, on average, outperform whichever of the human or AI was better alone; results varied by task and relative competence.  
**Source:** Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone (2024), *Nature Human Behaviour*.  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02024-1  
**What it demonstrates:** Collaboration with AI is itself a capability; "human oversight" is not a magic performance boost.  
**Chapter fit:** Judgement; Myth vs Reality; leadership.  
**Reader usefulness:** Corrects one of the most common augmentation clichés.  
**Print:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Yes, interactive decision tree possible.  
**Practice:** Ask whether human, AI or combination has comparative advantage for a task.  
**Limitations:** Diverse tasks/systems; averages conceal heterogeneity.  
**Dating risk:** Medium.

## Example 17 — AI role-play as a low-cost practice partner

**Skill:** Communication / adaptability / emotional capability  
**What happened:** In a randomized 2025 language-learning study with 40 learners, ChatGPT role-play supported short-term learning of some pragmatic speech acts while peer interaction showed stronger longer-term retention in part of the comparison.  
**Source:** Sim, Kim & Ku (2025), *TESOL Quarterly*.  
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tesq.70010  
**What it demonstrates:** AI can help people practise human skills without replacing the value of human interaction.  
**Chapter fit:** Practical development / companion website.  
**Reader usefulness:** Gives readers something constructive to do with AI.  
**Print:** Yes, small callout.  
**Website:** Excellent; reusable practice prompts.  
**Practice:** Yes.  
**Limitations:** Small sample, second-language pragmatics, not general social-skills training.  
**Dating risk:** Medium.

## Example 18 — Systems thinking is teachable-looking but measurement is fragmented

**Skill:** Systems thinking  
**What happened:** Reviews of systems-thinking education find approaches such as system mapping, simulation, interdisciplinary work and community engagement, but substantial inconsistency in definitions and assessment. A 2026 public-health scoping review found validated assessment was uncommon across the small evidence base it reviewed.  
**Sources:**  
https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09500693.2023.2245106  
https://www.mdpi.com/2079-8954/14/9/1115  
**What it demonstrates:** Systems thinking is more than a motivational "see the big picture" phrase, but the scientific construct is less standardised than emotional or cognitive skills are sometimes presented.  
**Chapter fit:** Systems thinking; caveat + practical mapping exercise.  
**Reader usefulness:** Lets the chapter be useful without pretending a universal systems-thinking score exists.  
**Print:** Yes.  
**Website:** Strong mapping exercise.  
**Practice:** Yes—map feedback loops and unintended consequences in a real personal problem.  
**Limitations:** Educational/domain-specific evidence; not AI-specific.  
**Dating risk:** Low-medium.
