# 01 — Core Research

## 1. What the central claim can safely mean

The statement **"Human capability becomes more important, not less"** is strongest when "more important" is defined carefully.

It can mean at least four different things:

- **Economically complementary:** when AI makes one input cheaper (for example, producing a first draft or prediction), complementary inputs such as contextual judgement or accountability may become more valuable.
- **A larger share of the remaining human task:** even if total human effort falls, the work left to people may contain a higher proportion of framing, checking, deciding and relationship work.
- **More important for safe use:** powerful automation raises the consequences of weak verification, misplaced trust and unclear accountability.
- **Personally/socially important:** capabilities such as communication and emotional regulation can matter in family, community, learning and care even where their wage premium is unclear.

The research does **not** justify saying that every listed skill will command higher wages, that AI cannot perform any component of these skills, or that all workers will benefit equally.

### Economic complementarity

Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb argue that when machine prediction becomes cheaper, the value of complements to prediction—including human judgement—can rise. This is a useful economic framing, but it is an argument about complements, not direct evidence that every judgement-heavy occupation will gain employment or wages.

Source: Agrawal, A.; Gans, J.; Goldfarb, A. (2017), "How AI Will Change Strategy: A Thought Experiment," *Harvard Business Review*.  
https://hbr.org/2017/10/how-ai-will-change-strategy-a-thought-experiment

David Autor's 2024 work makes a related but different case: AI could extend the reach and value of human expertise, allowing more workers to perform higher-stakes tasks that previously required scarce elite expertise. This cuts against the claim that AI necessarily concentrates all value in a tiny group of experts, but it is a forward-looking economic argument rather than proof of the eventual distributional outcome.

Source: Autor, D. (2024), "Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs," NBER Working Paper 32140.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140

## 2. Labour-market evidence before generative AI

### Social skills became more valuable in the US labour market

David Deming analysed US occupational and wage data from 1980–2012. Jobs requiring relatively high levels of social interaction grew substantially as a share of employment. Jobs combining strong mathematical and social skills showed particularly strong employment and wage growth. This is more useful than a generic "soft skills matter" claim because it suggests **complementarity**: social capability and analytical capability together were valuable.

This evidence predates modern generative AI. It cannot establish that GenAI caused or will continue the trend, but it provides a historical baseline showing that interpersonal coordination remained economically valuable through earlier waves of computerisation.

Source: Deming, D. J. (2017), "The Growing Importance of Social Skills in the Labor Market," *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 132(4), 1593–1640.  
https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/132/4/1593/3861633

Related job-posting work by Deming and Lisa Kahn found that cognitive and social skill requirements were associated with higher pay and better firm performance and appeared complementary.

Source: Deming, D. J.; Kahn, L. B. (2017), "Skill Requirements across Firms and Labor Markets: Evidence from Job Postings for Professionals," NBER Working Paper 23328.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w23328

### Do not turn this into "soft beats technical"

The evidence is better read as **skill bundles** than a contest between technical and non-technical skills. The strongest labour-market story is often that workers combine domain/technical knowledge with communication, coordination, judgement or problem-solving.

## 3. Evidence in the generative-AI era

### AI can raise less-experienced workers' immediate performance

A large field study of 5,179 customer-support agents found access to a generative-AI assistant increased productivity by about 14% on average and by substantially more for novice/lower-skilled workers, with little benefit for the most experienced workers. The authors interpret the system as diffusing patterns from high-performing workers.

This complicates the chapter in an important way: if AI transfers some communication patterns and tacit know-how to less-experienced workers, portions of previously scarce skill can become **less scarce**. That does not make human skill irrelevant; it changes where differentiation lies.

Source: Brynjolfsson, E.; Li, D.; Raymond, L. R. (2023 working paper; later published in QJE), "Generative AI at Work," NBER Working Paper 31161.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

### AI performance has a jagged frontier

In an experiment involving 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants, access to GPT-4 improved performance on tasks that were within the model's capability frontier, but performance could deteriorate on tasks outside that frontier when participants relied on the system. The central editorial value is not the exact model-specific performance number; it is the pattern: **using AI well requires knowing when it is likely to help and when it is likely to mislead.**

Source: Dell'Acqua, F. et al. (2023), "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier," SSRN.  
https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4573321

### Human plus AI is not automatically best

A preregistered meta-analysis covering 106 experimental studies and 370 effect sizes found human-AI combinations, on average, performed worse than the better of the human or AI working alone, although combinations showed benefits in some creation tasks and under some relative-skill conditions. Most studies still left the final decision with the human.

This is a strong reason to avoid reassuring readers that "AI assists; humans judge; therefore the combination is always superior." Judgement includes deciding **whether and how to combine** human and machine contributions.

Source: Vaccaro, M.; Almaatouq, A.; Malone, T. W. (2024), "When combinations of humans and AI are useful: a systematic review and meta-analysis," *Nature Human Behaviour*.  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02024-1

## 4. Current employer expectations: useful, but not outcomes

The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025* reports employers placing high importance on analytical thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility, leadership/social influence, creative thinking, empathy/active listening, curiosity/lifelong learning and systems thinking, while AI and big data are among the fastest-growing technical skills.

Use this as evidence of **employer expectations and stated priorities**, not as proof that these skills will actually produce future employment or wage growth. Surveyed firms have commercial and strategic incentives, and intentions can differ from observed behaviour.

Source: World Economic Forum (2025), *Future of Jobs Report 2025*.  
https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/digest/

## 5. OECD evidence adds an important qualification

The OECD's 2026 review of AI and skills reports evidence of rising demand for some analytical and data-interpretation capabilities in firms adopting GenAI, but it also reports cases in which algorithmic management is associated with workers or managers perceiving less need for some social/emotional skills. The OECD explicitly cautions that the evidence is still developing.

Editorial implication: **do not claim a universal one-way shift toward human skills.** The effects vary by task, occupation, organisation and form of AI deployment.

Source: OECD (2026), *AI and skills: What we know so far*.  
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/ai-and-skills_f843b352-en/full-report.html

**Recheck before publication:** this is a fast-moving evidence area.

## 6. Australia: the most useful national framing

Jobs and Skills Australia's 2025 national GenAI capacity study presents GenAI primarily as an **augmentation and task-transformation** issue rather than a simple whole-job replacement story. Its skills material emphasises both digital/AI capability and non-digital capabilities.

This fits Chapter 12 well because it avoids the false choice between "learn AI" and "build human skills." Both are complements. Chapter 12 can focus on human capability; Chapter 13 can focus on practical AI-tool literacy.

Sources:

- Jobs and Skills Australia (2025), *Australia's AI Transition: Jobs, Skills and the Future of Work*.  
  https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study
- Jobs and Skills Australia (2025), *Our Gen AI Transition — Skills*.

The National Skills Taxonomy and its predecessor, the Australian Skills Classification, provide useful Australian vocabulary around transferable/core capabilities, specialist tasks and technology tools.

Source: Jobs and Skills Australia, *National Skills Taxonomy*.

## 7. Australian education frameworks support "capabilities," not motivational slogans

The Australian Curriculum's general capabilities include critical and creative thinking, personal and social capability, ethical understanding, digital literacy and other cross-curriculum capabilities. Importantly, ACARA frames these as developed **through learning areas and content**, not as free-floating traits taught in isolation.

That is directly useful for Chapter 12: readers build judgement, communication and systems thinking by working on real subject matter and receiving feedback, not by merely deciding to "be more adaptable."

## 8. Assessment of the approved skills list

### Skills strongly supported as useful chapter headings

- **Communication** — supported by labour-market social-skill evidence, workplace studies and the growing need to direct/evaluate AI-mediated communication.
- **Judgement** — arguably the strongest throughline because it covers verification, automation bias, use of domain expertise and accountability.
- **Leadership** — supported as direction-setting, coordination, trust and decision-making under uncertainty; should not be defined as "tasks AI cannot do."
- **Creativity** — supported, but Chapter 12 should only address creative problem-solving and selection/framing because Chapter 8 owns the deeper creativity discussion.
- **Adaptability** — well suited as the throughline rather than a standalone section; adult-learning evidence supports continuing development, while "growth mindset" intervention evidence is mixed.
- **Systems thinking** — valuable because it directs attention to interactions, feedback, second-order effects and problem framing, but definition and measurement are less standardised than the chapter might imply.
- **Emotional intelligence** — relevant to relationship-heavy work and everyday life, but the label is scientifically contested and measured in several incompatible ways. Prefer concrete components (emotion recognition/regulation, perspective-taking, empathy, relationship judgement) rather than treating EI as one settled trait.

### Important cross-cutting capabilities missing from the visible list

These do not necessarily need new headings; adding too many would weaken the plan. They should be woven through existing sections:

#### Domain expertise

You cannot reliably judge an AI answer in a domain you do not understand. Evidence from human-AI work repeatedly shows that effective use depends on knowing the problem, recognising errors and understanding when tool output falls outside its competence.

Relevant source: Tambe, P. (2025), "Reskilling Workforce for AI: Domain Expertise and Algorithmic Literacy," *Management Science*.  
https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.03968

#### Critical thinking and verification

This is already implicit in Chapters 3 and 11 and belongs inside judgement rather than becoming a seventh/eighth displayed skill. It is especially important because AI confidence can encourage over-reliance.

#### Problem framing

AI can generate answers to the question it receives; identifying the actual problem, constraints, stakeholders and success criteria is often the higher-value step. This fits creativity, judgement and systems thinking.

#### Ethical responsibility/accountability

Professional guidance increasingly makes clear that responsibility remains with the human professional even when AI produces the material. This belongs under judgement and leadership.

#### Learning how to learn

This is the practical mechanism under adaptability. It is better grounded in adult-learning evidence than in motivational claims.

## 9. Six versus seven skills

There is no substantive inconsistency if the chapter explains the architecture clearly:

- the approved plan identifies **seven capabilities**;
- **adaptability** is the chapter's throughline inherited from Chapter 10;
- the chapter then examines **six durable skills** through which adaptability becomes concrete.

The problem is editorial, not conceptual. A reader encountering "The Six Durable Skills" without the seven-to-six explanation could reasonably think adaptability was accidentally omitted. The eventual writer should make the relationship explicit near the start.

## 10. What "soft skills" gets wrong

The term is convenient but misleading in this chapter:

- it groups very different constructs together;
- it can imply the skills are vague, innate or secondary;
- it disguises the role of domain knowledge;
- several are cognitively demanding and can be assessed or trained to some degree;
- "soft" versus "technical" creates a false opposition when evidence often supports bundles of both.

A better beginner-facing description is **durable human capabilities** or **judgement-heavy capabilities**, while acknowledging that none is guaranteed to remain exclusively human.

## 11. A more evidence-compatible effort-versus-judgement framework

The book's effort-versus-judgement distinction remains useful if it is treated as a spectrum rather than a hard partition.

### Effort-heavy examples

- first drafts;
- summarising a long document;
- formatting;
- extracting repeated information;
- generating a long list of options;
- routine classification or transformation.

### Judgement-heavy examples

- deciding what problem should be solved;
- deciding which evidence is trustworthy;
- recognising relevant local context absent from the prompt;
- deciding what risk is acceptable;
- choosing among several plausible options;
- taking responsibility for the outcome;
- handling disagreement or emotion in a relationship;
- monitoring second-order consequences.

### Why the boundary moves

Tasks migrate from judgement-heavy to more automatable as models, data access and tooling improve. Therefore Chapter 12 should teach readers to identify the **current human contribution** rather than promising that any named task is permanently protected from AI.

## 12. Suggested evidence-backed central formulation for later editorial consideration

Not chapter copy; this is a research synthesis:

> As AI makes many forms of production cheaper and faster, the human share of valuable work often shifts toward framing the problem, applying domain knowledge, evaluating output, understanding context and people, and taking responsibility. Those capabilities are not immune from automation, but they become critical to using automation well—and they need deliberate practice if people are not to lose them.

This preserves the plan's intent while surviving the strongest counter-evidence found in this research.
