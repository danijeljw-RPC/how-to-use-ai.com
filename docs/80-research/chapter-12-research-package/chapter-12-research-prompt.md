# Central chapter framing

The Chapter 12 plan's core claim is:

> Human capability becomes more important, not less.

The draft's supporting argument is:

> As AI absorbs more effort-heavy work, the judgement-heavy work around directing, evaluating, and applying that output becomes a larger share of what is valuable, and that work runs on a specific set of human skills.

Research this framing rather than simply accepting it.

Investigate:

- labour-market evidence on the changing value of social, interpersonal, analytical, and judgement skills over recent decades;
- evidence on whether generative AI is increasing or decreasing the value of these skills so far;
- economic arguments that when prediction (or production) becomes cheap, complementary human inputs such as judgement become more valuable;
- counter-evidence and counter-arguments, for example:
  - AI systems performing well on tasks associated with communication, empathy, persuasion, creativity, and analysis;
  - the possibility that some "human" skills become **less** scarce, not more, if AI raises everyone's baseline;
  - the possibility that value shifts to a smaller number of people with deep expertise, rather than to everyone with "soft skills";
  - the possibility that judgement depends on domain knowledge that is built through the very effort-heavy work AI is absorbing;
- whether "more important" means more economically valuable, more personally important, more socially important, or more distinctive;
- whether the claim applies equally across occupations, industries, and life contexts;
- and whether better language or qualifications are needed (for example, "human capability becomes **differently** important," or "the human part of the work becomes a larger share of the value").

Do not abandon the approved chapter framing without strong reason, but identify weaknesses or necessary nuance so the eventual writer can avoid overstating it.

# Research each skill in depth

For each of the seven skills in the plan (communication, judgement, leadership, creativity, adaptability, systems thinking, emotional intelligence), research:

- a precise, beginner-appropriate definition, and whether researchers agree on the definition;
- how the skill is measured, if at all, and how reliable the measures are;
- labour-market evidence of its value (wages, employment, hiring demand);
- evidence of how generative AI interacts with it:
  - where AI assists the skill;
  - where AI performs tasks that look like the skill;
  - where AI cannot substitute for the skill, and why;
  - where AI use may weaken the skill;
- concrete, documented examples of the skill mattering in AI-assisted work or life;
- how the skill is developed, and evidence on whether it can be taught or trained;
- practical, low-cost ways an ordinary reader could strengthen it;
- common misconceptions;
- areas of disagreement;
- and what the reader should take away.

Specific research threads for each skill follow.

## Communication

- Evidence about the value of writing and speaking skills as AI takes on more drafting.
- The difference between producing text and communicating: knowing the audience, purpose, relationship, timing, and what should not be said.
- Research on AI-assisted communication, including studies of how recipients react when they learn a message was written with AI (for example, effects on perceived sincerity, effort, or trust).
- Research on whether AI-written text tends to sound generic or homogeneous, and whether that matters.
- Whether the ability to write clearly also underpins the ability to **prompt** and **evaluate** AI well (connect to Chapter 5).
- Research whether heavy use of AI for writing affects people's own writing ability or thinking.

## Judgement

- Definitions of judgement from decision science, law, medicine, and management.
- Economic arguments that judgement becomes more valuable as prediction becomes cheaper.
- Research on **automation bias** and over-reliance: people accepting AI recommendations even when they are wrong.
- Research on the **"ironies of automation"**: when automation handles routine work, humans are left with the hardest, rarest decisions while getting less practice.
- Evidence on **deskilling** after AI assistance, including studies in medicine and other professional fields.
- Evidence that AI assistance can improve judgement when used in particular ways (for example, as a second opinion rather than a first answer).
- The relationship between judgement and domain expertise: can someone judge AI output in a field they do not understand?
- Accountability: why responsibility remains with people, including legal and professional-conduct examples (for example, professionals sanctioned for submitting AI-fabricated citations).

## Leadership

- Research on how leadership roles change when teams use AI.
- Evidence on managers' use of AI and on the tasks of management that AI does and does not affect.
- Responsibility, accountability, setting direction, making decisions under uncertainty, and trust.
- Research on algorithmic management (software directing or evaluating workers) and what it means for human leadership.
- Leading people through technological change: evidence from change-management research about what helps teams adapt.
- The draft's example of a team lead prioritising projects: whether stronger, documented examples exist.
- Ensure leadership is relevant beyond formal management roles (for example, community, family, informal leadership), in keeping with the book's consumer emphasis.

## Creativity

- Chapter 8 has already researched AI and creativity in depth, including creativity studies, homogenisation, and anchoring. **Do not re-research this from scratch.**
- For Chapter 12, focus on **creative problem-solving outside the arts**: in work, business, community, and daily life.
- Research on problem framing and problem finding as distinct from idea generation.
- Research on whether AI-assisted idea generation increases individual output while reducing collective diversity, only insofar as it affects the argument that creativity is a durable human skill.
- Evidence on how people keep their own creative capability when using AI (for example, generating one's own ideas first).

## Adaptability

- Chapter 10 covers adaptability as a general disposition. For Chapter 12, research only what is needed to present adaptability as the **throughline** connecting the other skills, as the plan specifies.
- Research related concepts such as learning agility, growth mindset (including the replication debate around growth-mindset interventions), cognitive flexibility, and lifelong learning, and whether these are well supported by evidence.
- Research evidence on what helps adults keep learning through their working lives.

## Systems thinking

- Definitions of systems thinking from its main traditions, and a beginner-appropriate explanation.
- Evidence on whether systems thinking can be taught and measured.
- Why AI tools answering narrow questions may miss wider connections and second-order effects, and whether this is a stable limitation or one that is changing as AI systems gain larger context and tool access.
- Concrete, everyday examples of systems thinking (household budgets, health decisions, small-business decisions, community issues), not only corporate ones.
- The draft's example of a business owner connecting a sales dip to a supplier change two months earlier: whether the example works, whether AI tools with access to the right data might also find that connection, and what that implies for the argument (for example, that the human value lies in knowing what data to connect and what question to ask, rather than in the calculation).

## Emotional intelligence

- Definitions and models of emotional intelligence, and the scientific debate about the concept's validity and measurement.
- Research on empathy, including the distinction between cognitive empathy, emotional empathy, and compassionate action.
- **Important:** research studies in which AI-generated responses were rated **as more empathetic** than human responses (for example, studies comparing chatbot and physician responses to patient questions), and studies of how people's ratings change when they know the response came from AI. This evidence directly tests the draft's claim that emotional intelligence "stays squarely human."
- Research on AI companions, AI therapy chatbots, and evidence of benefits and harms (Chapter 9 may cover the risks; record the boundary).
- The difference between **producing empathetic-sounding language** and **being present, accountable, and in a relationship** with someone.
- Research on in-person care work, teaching, nursing, social work, and other relational jobs, and why presence and trust matter.
- The draft's example of a manager delivering difficult feedback: whether it holds up, and whether stronger examples exist.

# Cognitive offloading, deskilling, and the paradox of supervision

Research the risk that heavy reliance on AI can weaken the very skills people need to supervise AI.

Investigate:

- research on cognitive offloading (for example, relying on search engines, GPS navigation, calculators, and now AI);
- studies of knowledge workers' self-reported critical thinking when using generative AI;
- studies measuring learning or brain activity when people write with and without AI assistance, including their sample sizes, peer-review status, and limitations;
- studies of students with unrestricted AI access versus guided AI access and the effect on later unassisted performance;
- studies of professionals whose performance changed after a period of AI assistance;
- historical research on automation and skill loss (for example, aviation pilots and autopilot);
- the concern that junior workers who never do effort-heavy work may not develop the judgement needed to supervise AI (connect to Chapter 10's entry-level discussion);
- and research-based practices that reduce these risks (for example, attempting a task before asking AI, using AI as a critic rather than an author, deliberate practice, and periodic work without AI).

Distinguish strong evidence from early or small studies, and do not overgeneralise from any single study.

This thread may substantially strengthen the chapter's practical advice: staying relevant may depend not only on having durable skills, but on **deliberately protecting them** while using AI.

# Contrasting viewpoints

Chapter 12 concerns questions where economists, psychologists, educators, employers, and technologists disagree.

Do not manufacture artificial "both sides" symmetry.

Instead:

- identify the main positions;
- explain the reasoning behind each;
- identify what evidence supports each;
- identify where evidence is weak;
- distinguish employer survey intentions from observed hiring and wage data;
- distinguish laboratory tests of AI "skills" from real-world performance;
- distinguish short-term study results from long-term skill development;
- distinguish company claims from independent research;
- identify where two apparently conflicting claims can both be true.

Examples:

> AI-generated responses can be rated as more empathetic than human responses in text-based studies.

and:

> People still value human presence, accountability, and relationships in situations that matter to them.

These are not necessarily contradictory.

Likewise:

> AI can improve a person's immediate performance on a task.

and:

> Relying on AI can weaken the skills needed to judge its output.

may both be true.

Likewise:

> Social and interpersonal skills have grown in labour-market value over recent decades.

and:

> AI may reduce the scarcity of some of those skills in the future.

may both be true.

Capture this nuance.

Useful for:
- controlled evidence;

Useful for:
- what AI can do on a defined task.

- specific model version;
- text-only or single-turn tasks;

Useful for:
- official frameworks;
- policy.

Possible limitation:
- frameworks may reflect consensus or politics rather than evidence.

# Depth

The research can be extensive.

Do not artificially restrict it to the expected length of the eventual Chapter 12.

It is preferable for the chapter-writing AI to receive substantially more high-quality research than it ultimately uses.

Do not pad the package with repetitive information.

Prioritise:

1. accuracy;
2. primary evidence;
3. recent evidence;
4. practical usefulness;
5. strong real-world examples;
6. honest counter-evidence about AI performance on "human" tasks;
7. evidence on protecting skills while using AI;
8. relevance beyond office work;
9. Australian relevance;
10. clear distinction between evidence and opinion;
11. material that gives the eventual writer genuine editorial choices.

# Research philosophy

Do not approach this task trying to prove that:

- human skills will always matter more;
- AI will make human skills obsolete;
- soft skills are the answer;
- soft skills are meaningless;
- AI makes people smarter;
- AI makes people less capable.

Approach these as questions to investigate.

The research package should contain enough evidence for the later writer to say:

> Here is which human skills the evidence suggests matter more alongside AI, and why.

> Here is where AI is also getting good at things that look like these skills.

> Here is what the human part still is.

> Here is how relying on AI can quietly weaken these skills.

> Here is what you can actually do to build and protect them.

That is more valuable than producing a predetermined narrative.
