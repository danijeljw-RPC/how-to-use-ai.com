# 04 — Cognitive Offloading, Deskilling and the Supervision Paradox

## Why this matters to Chapter 12

The chapter should not only tell readers which capabilities to build. It should explain why those capabilities may require **deliberate protection** when AI performs more of the practice that previously developed them.

The central paradox is:

> The more capable the automation becomes at routine work, the less often a person may practise the underlying skill—yet the person may still be expected to recognise the rare occasion when the automation is wrong.

This problem predates generative AI but is newly relevant to writing, analysis, coding, professional judgement and learning.

---

# 1. Cognitive offloading is normal, not inherently harmful

Humans have always externalised cognitive work into writing, maps, calculators, search engines, checklists, navigation systems and other people. Offloading can free capacity for higher-level work.

Therefore the chapter should **not** frame all AI assistance as intellectual decline. The useful question is whether a tool offloads a task that:

- did not need to remain internal; or
- was itself the practice through which the person developed understanding needed later.

A calculator may sensibly offload long arithmetic for an engineer who understands the model. It may be harmful during an exercise whose purpose is to teach number sense. The same tool can have opposite learning effects depending on the goal.

---

# 2. Critical-thinking effort among knowledge workers

A Microsoft Research/Carnegie Mellon study published for CHI 2025 surveyed 319 knowledge workers about 936 first-hand examples of GenAI use. Higher confidence in AI was associated with less self-reported critical-thinking effort, while higher self-confidence was associated with more. Participants described critical thinking shifting toward verification, integration and stewardship.

Source: Microsoft Research / Carnegie Mellon University (CHI 2025), "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking: Self-Reported Reductions in Cognitive Effort and Confidence Effects From a Survey of Knowledge Workers."  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/

### What it supports

- AI use can change where people report investing cognitive effort.
- Trust/confidence calibration matters.
- Verification and integration can become new forms of critical work.

### What it does not support

- objective proof that AI users' critical-thinking ability declined;
- a causal claim that GenAI makes people less intelligent;
- a universal effect across occupations.

The study is self-report and retrospective. The chapter should call it a warning signal, not a settled deskilling result.

---

# 3. Immediate performance can improve while unaided learning suffers

A particularly useful randomized classroom study by Bastani and colleagues involved nearly 1,000 high-school students in Türkiye. Students used different AI configurations during maths practice. An unrestricted GPT-based assistant greatly improved practice performance, but when AI was removed for an exam, the unrestricted group performed worse than the control group. A guardrailed tutoring system produced large practice gains without the same statistically significant unaided-performance penalty.

Source: Bastani et al. (2025), "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*.  
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122

### Why it is unusually valuable for Chapter 12

It demonstrates a simple distinction readers can understand:

- **getting the task done better today** is not the same as
- **becoming better able to do the task tomorrow without assistance**.

It also avoids an anti-AI conclusion: design and constraints mattered. The guardrailed system supported learning better than unrestricted answer access.

### Limitations

- specific subject, age group and implementation;
- not proof that the same effect occurs for adults at work;
- model/tool designs continue to change.

# 4. Professional deskilling signal: endoscopy

A 2025 multicentre observational study in *The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology* examined colonoscopies at four Polish centres before and after clinicians were routinely exposed to AI-assisted polyp detection. When they later performed colonoscopies **without** AI, adenoma detection was lower after the AI-exposure period than before it (28.4% versus 22.4%; absolute difference −6.0 percentage points).

Source: Budzyń et al. (2025), PubMed record.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40816301/

### What it supports

Routine exposure to decision support may be associated with lower unaided performance in a high-skill professional task.

### What it does not support

- definitive causal proof of deskilling;
- a claim that AI-assisted endoscopy is harmful overall;
- generalisation to all professions.

The study was observational. Other research reports no measurable persistent deskilling under different designs/participants, including a 2026 prospective study of inexperienced endoscopists.

Counter-source:  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13552228/

### Editorial implication

Present deskilling as a **real risk with mixed and emerging evidence**, not an inevitable outcome.

**Recheck before publication.**

# 5. "Your Brain on ChatGPT": interesting, but easy to overstate

The MIT Media Lab's 2025 preprint studied essay writing with LLM, search-engine and brain-only conditions using EEG and behavioural measures. The reported results included lower neural-connectivity measures and lower ownership/recall in the LLM condition. The initial sessions involved 54 participants, with 18 participating in a later crossover session.

Source: MIT Media Lab (2025), "Your Brain on ChatGPT."  
https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/

### Why caution is essential

- small sample;
- preprint rather than established replicated finding;
- EEG interpretation is complex;
- narrow essay-writing setting;
- strong media-friendly language such as "cognitive debt" can travel further than the evidence.

Use only as an example of an emerging research question unless peer-reviewed/replicated evidence strengthens before publication.

**Recheck before publication.**

# 6. Classic automation problem: Bainbridge

Bainbridge's 1983 paper identifies a structural problem still relevant to GenAI: designers automate what can be automated, leaving humans to handle what cannot—including unusual failures. Yet automation removes the human from routine control, weakening awareness and practice.

Source: Bainbridge (1983), "Ironies of Automation."  
https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8

### Beginner explanation

If a system handles 99 routine cases, the human may see only the 1 difficult case. The job can become **less frequent but harder**, exactly when the person has had less recent practice.

This maps directly to AI-assisted writing, professional analysis and entry-level work.

# 7. Entry-level work and the apprenticeship problem

One concern in Chapter 10 can be carried forward carefully: senior judgement often develops through years of lower-level production, observation, correction and feedback. If AI eliminates too much of that work, organisations may save effort now while weakening the pipeline that develops future experts.

Current evidence is more developed as an economic/organisational concern than as long-term causal proof. Do not claim a demonstrated economy-wide "apprenticeship collapse."

The customer-support study is useful counter-evidence: AI can also **accelerate exposure to high-performer patterns** for novices. Therefore AI could either weaken or accelerate expertise development depending on workflow and learning design.

Source:  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

# 8. Human final authority does not solve automation bias

The 2024 *Scientific Reports* personnel-selection experiments show that people can fail to reject incorrect AI advice even when they remain the final decision-maker. This is crucial for the chapter's effort-versus-judgement model.

Source:  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60220-5

### Editorial implication

Avoid wording such as:

> AI produces; the human decides.

A more realistic process is:

> AI produces; the human must remain capable and motivated enough to evaluate, challenge and sometimes reject it.

# 9. Practices that can protect capability

The evidence base is not mature enough to prescribe a universal anti-deskilling protocol, but several practices follow reasonably from learning science, automation research and the studies above.

## Attempt first when learning is the goal

If the objective is capability development rather than merely output, make an unaided attempt before viewing an AI solution. This preserves retrieval, generation and error-correction opportunities.

## Use AI as critic, tutor or counterargument—not always as author

Ask the system to identify weaknesses in your work, pose questions, generate test cases or explain errors. The Bastani study suggests guardrailed assistance can support learning better than unrestricted answer access in at least one setting.

## Preserve periodic unaided practice

For skills that may be needed when AI is unavailable or wrong, occasionally perform representative tasks without it. This is analogous to maintaining manual capability in other automated domains.

## Keep decision criteria visible

Write down what makes an answer acceptable **before** asking AI when stakes justify the effort. This reduces the risk that a polished output silently changes the standard by which it is judged.

## Verify selectively by consequence

Not every sentence needs independent verification. Verification effort should increase with consequence, irreversibility and uncertainty.

## Seek feedback from reality

Judgement cannot improve if outcomes never return to the decision-maker. Track whether messages worked, forecasts were accurate, plans succeeded and assumptions held.

## Rotate the role AI plays

Sometimes generator, sometimes critic, sometimes simulator, sometimes checker. Always using AI as the first-answer machine concentrates dependence on one cognitive workflow.

# 10. Practical "supervision paradox" box for the later writer

**Research synthesis, not finished chapter copy:**

| When AI does more of... | The person gets less practice at... | Yet may still need to... |
|---|---|---|
| drafting | structuring an argument | spot a misleading argument |
| diagnosis/classification | recognising routine patterns | catch rare failures |
| navigation | building spatial knowledge | recover when routing fails |
| coding | recalling syntax/implementation patterns | debug subtle failures |
| summarising | reading source material closely | notice what the summary omitted |
| customer responses | handling recurring conversations | manage exceptional or emotionally charged cases |

The important point is not "never offload." It is to know which skills you still need **inside your own head** to supervise the offloaded work safely.
