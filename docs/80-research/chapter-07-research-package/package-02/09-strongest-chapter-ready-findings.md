# Strongest Chapter-Ready Findings

This file prioritises the supplementary research most directly aligned with Chapter 7's reader-state goal.

## 1. Strongest professional use-case pattern

**Transforming reliable existing material** is the clearest cross-role use case:

- notes → report;
- meeting → decisions/actions/open questions;
- support thread → issue summary;
- report → presentation outline;
- process notes → SOP draft;
- spreadsheet requirement → formula/explanation;
- research question → search/source plan.

Why it is strong: the worker already owns or can verify the underlying information, reducing the need for the AI to invent domain facts.

## 2. Strongest productivity evidence

### Customer support field deployment

5,179 support agents; about 14% average productivity improvement, much larger among novice/lower-skilled workers.

**Use:** real workplace multiplier example.  
**Caveat:** do not transfer the percentage to other jobs.

### Professional writing experiment

453 professionals; about 40% less time and 18% higher quality on the tested writing tasks.

**Use:** supports report/drafting value.  
**Caveat:** controlled tasks are cleaner than real organisational workflows.

## 3. Strongest evidence productivity depends on task/implementation

### Jagged frontier

Consultants improved strongly on tasks inside the model's capability frontier and performed worse on a task outside it.

### Experienced developer slowdown

One specialised RCT found experienced open-source developers took about 19% longer with early-2025 AI tools despite believing they were faster.

### Job-post field experiment

Employers drafted faster but produced more generic posts and did not improve job matching; local efficiency did not equal system effectiveness.

**Chapter lesson:** judge the completed workflow, not generation speed.

## 4. Best Chapter 5 skill demonstrated at work

The simplest reusable pattern:

> **Objective + audience + source material + desired structure + constraints + what not to invent.**

This works across reports, support, presentations, documentation and meetings without teaching elaborate prompt syntax.

## 5. Best fully worked example

**Meeting notes → decisions/actions/open questions.**

It can demonstrate:

- raw notes;
- weak generic request;
- structured request;
- explicit no-inference rule;
- plausible subtle errors;
- verification checklist;
- confidentiality/tool-policy question;
- follow-up email/task conversion.

The central semantic distinction should be:

**discussion ≠ proposal ≠ decision ≠ action ≠ unresolved issue.**

## 6. Clearest confidentiality example

The same task — “summarise this client complaint” — changes depending on what is supplied and which system receives it.

- approved managed system + permitted data: potentially ordinary workflow;
- personal/public tool + customer personal/confidential data: may be inappropriate/prohibited.

This keeps the chapter practical rather than fear-based.

## 7. Clearest company-policy example

Australian Government public-genAI guidance explicitly distinguishes public and enterprise tools, information sensitivity, permitted uses, validation and accountability.

**Chapter lesson:** availability is not the same as organisational approval.

## 8. Clearest verification example

Meeting notes say a launch move was **proposed**; AI summary says it was **decided**.

Why strong: no absurd hallucination is required. One ordinary verb silently changes the professional record.

## 9. Strongest subtle AI failure

A spreadsheet formula returns plausible values and has no syntax error, but the business logic omits one category or mishandles a boundary date.

**Lesson:** successful calculation is not proof of correct logic.

## 10. Best Myth vs Reality candidates

1. **Myth:** AI automatically saves time.  
   **Reality:** count prompting, review, verification and rework.

2. **Myth:** professional-looking output is ready to send.  
   **Reality:** fluency can conceal wrong facts, decisions, numbers or commitments.

3. **Myth:** removing a name makes data safe.  
   **Reality:** contextual re-identification and business confidentiality remain possible.

4. **Myth:** a paid AI account automatically makes work information safe.  
   **Reality:** exact account/product, settings, organisation contract and policy matter.

5. **Myth:** no ban means approval.  
   **Reality:** employees should know what the organisation actually permits.

## 11. Strongest capability-not-just-output example

**Spreadsheet explanation:** the model explains why a formula behaves as it does, then the worker tests it against known cases.

This can increase understanding rather than merely outsourcing production.

Other capability examples: explaining unfamiliar terminology, asking missing questions, critiquing a draft, exposing assumptions.

## 12. Strongest example where AI is not the best tool

**Simple deterministic calculation.** If the user needs an exact total already represented in a spreadsheet, the spreadsheet calculation is more direct, reproducible and transparent. AI may explain the formula but need not become an unnecessary intermediary.

## 13. Best compact professional AI literacy tests

These nine candidate questions are well supported by the research and guidance:

1. **Workday test:** Does this save more time/effort than it costs?
2. **Confidentiality test:** Am I allowed to give this information to this system?
3. **Policy test:** Does my organisation permit this tool and this use?
4. **Verification test:** What happens if this is wrong?
5. **Source test:** Where did this claim/number actually come from?
6. **Authority test:** Is AI assisting, or deciding something I must own?
7. **Audience test:** Who will rely on this output?
8. **Expertise test:** Would I recognise a plausible mistake?
9. **Tool-choice test:** Is AI actually better than the existing tool/process?

The **minimum-data test** can sit within confidentiality: does the system need all the information I am about to provide?

## 14. Best core takeaway supported by this supplementary research

A careful, evidence-aligned version of the chapter's intended proposition is:

> AI can multiply professional capability on suitable tasks, particularly when it helps people structure, transform, summarise or interrogate information they can verify. The worker still needs to choose an appropriate tool, protect information, follow organisational rules and take responsibility for what is accepted, sent or acted upon.

This is research synthesis, not mandatory final wording.
