# Supplementary Research Delta, Scope, and Method

## What this second pass adds

The first research package already established the main Chapter 7 terrain: drafting, meeting summaries, customer support, presentations, spreadsheets, research, brainstorming, confidentiality, company policy, verification, productivity evidence, and template observations.

This pass goes deeper where the supplementary instructions identify editorial risk or an opportunity for a stronger practical example.

### 1. Replace the vague idea of “AI makes workers more productive” with a task-and-workflow view

The stronger evidence does not support a universal productivity slogan. It supports a conditional statement:

> AI can make some workers faster or more effective on some tasks when the task, tool, worker, information, and review process are suitable.

This formulation is supported by studies that point in different directions:

- professional writing tasks showed large time and quality improvements in a controlled experiment;
- customer-support deployment showed an average productivity increase, with much larger gains among novice/lower-performing workers;
- consulting tasks improved substantially when tasks were inside the model's capability frontier, but performance deteriorated on a task outside it;
- experienced open-source developers in one 2025 trial took longer with AI even while believing it had sped them up;
- AI-assisted job-post drafting saved employers time locally but produced poorer market-level matching outcomes.

The chapter can therefore teach readers to assess **the completed workflow**, not merely how quickly text appears on screen.

### 2. Make the “workday test” concrete

The proposed question — “Does using AI save more time than it costs?” — is defensible as a practical heuristic rather than a scientific metric.

A useful decomposition is:

1. time spent preparing useful context;
2. generation time;
3. time spent reading the result;
4. correction and editing time;
5. verification time;
6. time spent recovering from errors or omissions;
7. downstream effects on other people or systems.

This prevents a 30-second first draft from being counted as a productivity win if the finished task takes longer or becomes worse.

### 3. Treat meeting summaries as the chapter's strongest fully worked demonstration

Meeting summaries combine several chapter lessons in one example:

- useful context and explicit output structure;
- extraction rather than open-ended invention;
- professional time savings;
- confidentiality and policy questions;
- subtle verification problems;
- distinction between discussion, proposal, decision, action and unresolved issue;
- a natural route from notes to follow-up email or task list.

Academic meeting-summarisation research provides documented failure categories such as omission, irrelevance, structural problems, hallucination, speaker/attribution difficulties and loss of meeting-specific nuance.

### 4. Clarify tool/account distinctions without promising “enterprise = safe”

Current vendor documentation shows why broad statements about “AI tools” are misleading. Training use, retention, administrative access, logging, data residency, integrations and contractual protections are separate dimensions. Product/account type matters.

A simple reader principle is stronger than a product comparison table:

> Know exactly which tool/workspace/account you are using, what data-handling rules apply to it, and what your organisation permits.

### 5. Add capability-versus-dependency nuance

The evidence does not justify either “AI causes deskilling” or “AI teaches workers” as a universal statement.

Research suggests both are plausible depending on workflow:

- customer-support evidence suggests novice workers can absorb patterns used by stronger workers;
- critical-thinking research suggests high confidence in AI may be associated with less critical engagement;
- controlled work can shift human effort from production toward verification and integration;
- newer evidence suggests AI-supported performance improvements do not necessarily persist once assistance is removed unless learning and effort occur.

Chapter 7 only needs the practical implication: prefer workflows that leave the worker able to understand, check and defend the result.

## Source selection approach

Priority order used in this supplementary pass:

1. peer-reviewed papers and primary working papers;
2. government/regulator guidance;
3. original study/project pages;
4. professional-body guidance;
5. current vendor documentation for product-specific behaviour;
6. reputable institutional explanations where they add interpretation.

Company case studies are not treated as independent evidence of effectiveness. Vendor documentation is used for product capabilities and data-handling statements only.

## Important limits

### Evidence does not transfer automatically between occupations

A customer-support study is strong evidence about the studied support environment. It is not proof that lawyers, teachers, accountants, salespeople, analysts and project managers will see the same percentage gain.

### Tool generations matter

A 2023 hallucinated-citation rate or GPT-3.5 result should not be presented as a current 2026 model error rate. Older studies remain useful for demonstrating failure mechanisms, not present-day percentages.

### “Human review” is not a magic fix

Review quality depends on whether the reviewer has time, source access and sufficient expertise to notice a plausible error. This makes “Would I recognise a mistake?” a valuable professional-literacy question.

### Privacy guidance is jurisdiction-specific

The OAIC and Australian Government sources are highly relevant to an Australian-authored general book and provide good principles. The chapter should avoid presenting their legal requirements as universal rules for readers everywhere.

## Suggested evidence language for eventual chapter writing

Prefer language such as:

- “In one controlled experiment…”
- “In a large customer-support deployment…”
- “Results varied substantially by experience level…”
- “A different study found the opposite effect in a specialised setting…”
- “Current vendor documentation says…”
- “Australian privacy guidance recommends…”

Avoid:

- “Studies prove AI makes everyone faster.”
- “Enterprise tools are private.”
- “Public tools train on everything you type.”
- “Human review prevents hallucinations.”
- “AI meeting summaries are accurate.”

- **substantial:** ordinary context-and-specificity demonstrations from Chapter 5;
