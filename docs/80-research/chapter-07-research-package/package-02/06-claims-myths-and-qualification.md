# Claims We Should Not Make Without Qualification

This section stress-tests broad claims requested by the supplementary instructions. It is research guidance, not chapter prose.

## 1. AI makes workers more productive.

**Evidence status:** Supported in several studied tasks/settings, but contradicted or weakened in others.

**Narrower defensible version:** AI can improve productivity on some tasks and for some workers; gains depend on task fit, worker experience, tool quality and review process.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Noy & Zhang writing experiment; Brynjolfsson et al. support agents; HBS/BCG frontier study; METR experienced developers; Wiles/Horton job posts.

## 2. AI saves time.

**Evidence status:** Often true for generation or particular activities, not guaranteed for end-to-end completion.

**Narrower defensible version:** AI can save time when drafting/structuring gains exceed prompting, correction and verification costs.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Noy & Zhang; NBER work-pattern study; METR; Wiles/Horton.

## 3. AI will replace office jobs.

**Evidence status:** Not established by Chapter 7 evidence and outside chapter scope.

**Narrower defensible version:** Current evidence shows task-level augmentation, workflow change and some substitution; job-level effects remain heterogeneous and evolving.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** OECD SME survey reports most AI-using SMEs saw no change in overall staffing need; do not generalise this to the whole labour market.

## 4. AI makes junior employees perform like experts.

**Evidence status:** Too strong.

**Narrower defensible version:** Some studies show larger gains for less-experienced/lower-performing workers, narrowing performance gaps on tested tasks.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Customer support; professional writing; BCG consultant experiment.

## 5. Enterprise AI keeps everything private.

**Evidence status:** False as a blanket statement.

**Narrower defensible version:** Organisation-managed products can offer materially different contractual, training, logging, retention and admin controls; the exact product/configuration must be checked.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** OpenAI, Microsoft, Google, Anthropic current product documentation.

## 6. Public AI tools use everything entered into them for training.

**Evidence status:** False as a blanket statement.

**Narrower defensible version:** Training use depends on provider, product, account type, settings and sometimes feedback choices. Retention and training are separate questions.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Google Gemini consumer controls; commercial vendor docs; current product terms.

## 7. Removing names makes business information safe to upload.

**Evidence status:** Unsupported.

**Narrower defensible version:** De-identification may require addressing contextual identifiers and re-identification risk; information can also remain commercially confidential without identifying a person.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** OAIC privacy/de-identification guidance.

## 8. AI meeting summaries are accurate.

**Evidence status:** Too strong.

**Narrower defensible version:** Meeting summaries can be useful but remain vulnerable to omissions, irrelevance, hallucination, attribution problems and loss of uncertainty; verify operationally important details.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** COLING 2025 meeting-summary error research and related meeting-summarisation studies.

## 9. AI research tools provide reliable citations.

**Evidence status:** Depends heavily on product/retrieval method and cannot be assumed.

**Narrower defensible version:** Treat candidate citations as leads until the source is opened and the specific claim checked.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Scientific Reports 2023 historical citation study; NIST confabulation guidance; newer retrieval products may perform differently.

## 10. AI-generated spreadsheet formulas are safe if they calculate successfully.

**Evidence status:** False.

**Narrower defensible version:** A formula can be syntactically valid and return values while encoding the wrong logic; test known and edge cases and compare consequential results independently.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Microsoft and Google product warnings; general spreadsheet verification practice.

## 11. AI improves brainstorming.

**Evidence status:** Partly supported but incomplete.

**Narrower defensible version:** AI can improve rated individual ideas in some experiments while reducing diversity across a pool of ideas.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Lee & Chung; Doshi & Hauser; Meincke et al. and reply.

## 12. AI reduces creativity.

**Evidence status:** Too broad.

**Narrower defensible version:** Some research finds higher individual creativity but greater similarity across AI-assisted outputs; the effect depends on what 'creativity' means and how it is measured.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Science Advances 2024; Nature Human Behaviour 2024/2025 debate.

## 13. Using AI causes deskilling.

**Evidence status:** Not established universally.

**Narrower defensible version:** AI can create dependency or reduce critical engagement in some workflows, but other evidence suggests learning and skill transfer can occur.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Microsoft critical-thinking study; customer-support learning evidence.

## 14. Using AI improves workers' skills.

**Evidence status:** Not guaranteed.

**Narrower defensible version:** AI can expose workers to useful patterns and explanations, but performance with assistance does not automatically mean durable learning without assistance.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** Customer-support study plus broader learning/critical-thinking evidence.

## 15. Human review solves AI errors.

**Evidence status:** Too strong.

**Narrower defensible version:** Human review reduces risk only when the reviewer has adequate expertise, source access, time and authority to challenge the output.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** NIST risk framing; OAIC human oversight; automation-bias/critical-thinking literature.

## 16. Employees can use AI safely as long as they do not paste confidential information.

**Evidence status:** Incomplete.

**Narrower defensible version:** Workers also need to consider personal information, policy approval, intellectual property, tool/account configuration, accuracy, decision authority and downstream use.

**Useful evidence/counter-evidence:** OAIC; Digital.gov.au; organisational/vendor documentation.

# Myth vs Reality candidates

## Myth: AI at work is mostly for technical workers.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Research and current deployments span writing, support, email, meetings, small business, analysis and many administrative tasks; technical work is only one category.

## Myth: AI will automatically save time.

**Evidence-supported reality:** AI may save generation time while adding prompting, verification or rework. Judge the completed task.

## Myth: If the answer looks professional, it is ready to send.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Fluent presentation does not validate names, numbers, sources, commitments or business rules.

## Myth: If I remove the person's name, the data is safe.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Context can permit re-identification and non-personal business information can still be confidential.

## Myth: A paid account makes work information safe.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Price is not a proxy for employer approval, contract terms, retention, logging or data controls.

## Myth: If the company has not banned it, it is approved.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Organisations may require approved tools and uses; access or silence should not be treated as authorisation.

## Myth: AI can replace professional judgement because it has more information.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Breadth of model knowledge does not transfer accountability, organisational authority or domain-specific responsibility.

## Myth: Using AI means automating an entire job.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Much measured value comes from assisting individual tasks such as drafting, summarising, searching or explaining.

## Myth: AI is only useful for generating text.

**Evidence-supported reality:** Workplace uses also include extraction, classification, explanation, formula assistance, comparison, restructuring and question generation.


# Which myths are strongest for the chapter?

Prioritise myths that directly change reader behaviour:

1. AI automatically saves time.
2. Professional-looking output is ready to send.
3. Removing a name makes information safe.
4. A paid AI account automatically makes work information safe.
5. No ban means approval.
6. AI use means automating the entire job.

The creativity, labour-replacement and deskilling myths are interesting but may pull Chapter 7 toward later chapters unless kept very short.

# Qualification language pattern

For any broad workplace-AI claim, ask:

- **Which workers?**
- **Which task?**
- **Which tool/model and date?**
- **What outcome was measured?**
- **Was the task completed faster, or merely generated faster?**
- **Was quality independently assessed?**
- **Did verification/rework count?**
- **Was the effect self-reported or measured?**
- **Does the evidence come from a lab, real workplace, survey or vendor case study?**
- **What contradictory evidence exists?**

This checklist is useful for the later writing system whenever it encounters a striking percentage.
