# 11 — Additional Findings Outside the Minimum Chapter Plan

## Purpose

These topics are not all explicit in the approved Chapter 8 section list, but the research indicates they could materially improve the chapter or companion website.

They should **not** be allowed to overwhelm the chapter's five-domain overview and three core questions.

---

# 1. The important shift from generation to abundance

The approved plan asks what AI can create. A second-order issue is what happens once creation itself becomes cheap.

Deezer's current data makes this concrete: fully AI-generated music can represent an enormous share of new supply without representing a comparable share of actual listener demand. [S34]

This changes the problem from:

> Can someone make a song/image/article/video?

to:

> Who will find it, trust it and care about it?

Potential consequences:

- discoverability becomes harder;
- platforms strengthen spam/fraud moderation;
- trusted identity/reputation can become more valuable;
- recommendation systems have more power over what is seen;
- creators may compete with large volumes of near-zero-cost material even if audiences prefer human work;
- provenance/disclosure becomes more relevant.

**Recommended placement:** a short paragraph in print; deeper website treatment.

---

# 2. Digital replicas are not merely "copyright"

Voice cloning and generated likenesses deserve their own conceptual category.

Examples include:

- synthetic singers;
- digital actors;
- AI dubbing;
- cloned narrators;
- generated endorsement videos;
- deceased-performer simulation.

The relevant rights can include:

- performer rights;
- contract;
- personality/publicity rights;
- passing off/consumer protection;
- specific digital-replica laws;
- copyright in source recordings;
- copyright in the generated output.

SAG-AFTRA's negotiated replica-consent regime and the UK government's separate treatment of digital replicas show why this cannot be reduced to "who owns the AI file?" [S44] [S14]

**Recommended placement:** one music/video example in print; legal detail online.

---

# 3. Training-data models are becoming part of product positioning

Products increasingly differentiate themselves on:

- licensed datasets;
- public-domain datasets;
- contributor payments;
- opt-in consent;
- rights-reservation support;
- indemnity;
- dataset transparency.

Adobe Firefly is a useful example of a product whose training provenance is explicitly part of its market positioning. [S37] [S38]

Music deals between labels and AI companies show another route: sector-specific licensing after litigation/dispute. [S35] [S36]

This means "Which AI tool?" can matter ethically and commercially even when two tools have similar visible capability.

**Recommended placement:** practical website guidance; one sentence/example in print.

---

# 4. Transparency is splitting into input transparency and output transparency

These are different.

## Input transparency

> What material was used to develop/train the model?

EU GPAI training-content summaries are a current example. [S12] [S13]

## Output transparency

> Was this particular piece generated/altered using AI, and what happened to it?

YouTube disclosure, KDP disclosure and C2PA credentials are examples. [S31] [S32] [S30]

A system can be transparent in one direction and opaque in the other.

**Recommended placement:** website diagram.

---

# 5. Provenance is becoming infrastructure, not a truth machine

C2PA matters because it gives creators/platforms a standard way to attach cryptographically verifiable assertions about media history. [S30]

But its real significance is often misunderstood.

It is useful for:

- stating where a file came from;
- recording participating edits;
- recording AI-use assertions;
- making tampering with those assertions detectable.

It cannot make every absent credential suspicious, because many legitimate files will lack credentials.

It cannot prove the world depicted in a photograph/video is true.

**Recommended placement:** short Myth vs Reality in print; interactive online.

---

# 6. AI can silently become a casting/art-direction system

Bias in generated creative media is not only a fairness issue; it is a **creative control issue**.

If a user asks for "a CEO at work" without specifying demographics, the model fills those blanks. Those choices become part of the user's final work unless the user notices them.

Generated-profession image research shows measurable demographic patterns in tested systems. [S45] [S46] [S47]

A useful reader question:

> What details did the AI supply that I never deliberately chose?

This could apply beyond images:

- the assumed gender in a story;
- accent in synthetic voice;
- family structure;
- cultural reference;
- beauty norms;
- social class markers.

**Recommended placement:** short Watch Out or companion demo.

---

# 7. Accessibility is a benefit that should stand on its own

Generative interfaces can reduce barriers for people who cannot easily use conventional creative tools or who lack expensive equipment/training.

Possible benefits:

- speech-to-creative workflows;
- natural-language image editing;
- drafting assistance;
- translation;
- visual expression without fine-motor drawing;
- rapid prototyping without specialised software knowledge.

The evidence base for long-term creative participation/accessibility outcomes is less mature than the creativity and labour evidence in this package, so the chapter should avoid inflated claims.

Most importantly, accessibility should not be deployed as a debating weapon:

> AI helps disabled users, therefore creator consent doesn't matter.

Those are separate benefits/concerns.

**Recommended placement:** one positive paragraph; deeper examples when strong primary research is available.

---

# 8. "Human-made" may become a market attribute, but evidence is still emerging

As generated supply increases, some creators/markets may differentiate through:

- process disclosure;
- hand-made/live performance;
- signed/physical scarcity;
- no-generative-AI policies;
- provenance;
- named human authorship.

The research does not yet justify saying a broad premium market for "AI-free" media is established.

Treat this as an emerging strategy, not a forecast.

**Recommended placement:** possibly one sentence in abundance/authenticity discussion.

---

# 9. Creative authenticity should be contextual

The word "authentic" has too many meanings to use without definition.

A work may be authentic because:

- a real person performed it;
- it truthfully represents lived experience;
- the creator intentionally directed it;
- the process is disclosed;
- it does not impersonate someone;
- it is documentary evidence of a real event;
- it follows the norms of a community/practice.

A person may happily accept AI-generated fantasy art while objecting strongly to AI-generated documentary footage or a synthetic dead singer. The difference is not technical capability alone; it is audience expectation and context.

**Recommended placement:** reflection section or website essay.

---

# 10. Provider terms can create rights that copyright law does not—and vice versa

A beginner may assume:

> copyright = the provider's ownership rule.

But the provider-user contract can:

- grant permission;
- assign whatever rights the provider has;
- prohibit certain uses;
- impose disclosure obligations;
- restrict training/impersonation;
- disclaim exclusivity.

Statutory copyright can separately determine whether protected authorship exists.

Canva and Suno make this distinction unusually visible. [S41] [S42] [S43]

**Recommended placement:** core printed callout because it is directly actionable.

---

# 11. Platform policies are becoming a form of practical governance

For ordinary creators, the rule that changes behaviour may come from a platform rather than parliament/court.

Examples:

- KDP asks publishers to disclose generated content. [S32]
- YouTube requires disclosure of certain realistic altered/synthetic media. [S31]
- Spotify moderates spam/impersonation and supports granular AI metadata. [S33]
- Deezer labels/excludes fully AI-generated material from some recommendation/monetisation pathways. [S34]

These policies can change faster than law.

**Recommended placement:** print can teach "check the platform"; website maintains current examples.

---

# 12. "AI-generated" is becoming too coarse for provenance and authorship

A finished creative object may involve:

- human source material;
- generated elements;
- stock assets;
- AI editing;
- human compositing;
- AI translation/dubbing;
- human performance;
- AI cleanup.

Calling the entire thing "AI-generated" can obscure meaningful human authorship; calling it "human-created" can hide material synthetic contribution.

This supports the package's continuum:

1. blank-page breaker;
2. variation engine;
3. production assistant;
4. co-generator;
5. AI-led generation;
6. automated content generation.

**Recommended placement:** possible central diagram.

---

# 13. The cost of generation can fall faster than the cost of verification

As output becomes cheaper:

- checking factual claims still takes time;
- checking rights can be difficult;
- reviewing hundreds of variants can consume attention;
- client/stakeholder approval remains human;
- quality-control burden can rise with volume.

This creates a practical reversal:

> AI can remove the cost of making options and increase the cost of deciding among them.

Research on expert design inefficiency provides one direct example of this kind of workflow mismatch. [S21]

**Recommended placement:** strengthens effort-versus-judgement section.

---

# 14. More model capability does not automatically make AI the better tool

Some operations are better served by deterministic software:

- exact vector edit;
- typography;
- known colour transform;
- crop/resize;
- deterministic automation;
- source-controlled text changes;
- conventional search for primary sources;
- professional audio mixing controls.

Generative AI is useful when uncertainty/variation is a feature. It can be wasteful when the desired transformation is exact.

**Recommended placement:** cross-domain practical rule.

---

# 15. The chapter can be balanced without being neutral about evidence quality

Intellectual fairness does not require giving every claim equal weight.

Examples:

- a creator survey is strong evidence that surveyed creators are concerned;
- it is weak evidence for the actual future unemployment rate;
- a company usage statistic is strong evidence for activity on that platform if methodology is credible;
- it is not independent evidence that the company's product is "safe";
- a short experiment is strong evidence for that task/condition;
- it is weak evidence for lifelong creativity.

The later writer should openly make these distinctions.

This is how the chapter can present competing perspectives without becoming vague or falsely symmetrical.
