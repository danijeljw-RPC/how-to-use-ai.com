# 05 — Creative Labour, Substitution, Economics and Market Abundance

## Research framing

"Is AI replacing artists?" is too coarse to research well.

The better unit is:

> **Which tasks, in which markets, for which buyers, at what quality threshold, price and time period?**

A creative occupation can contain simultaneously:

- tasks AI substitutes for;
- tasks AI complements;
- tasks AI changes but does not remove;
- and tasks where AI has little value.

---

# 1. Terms the chapter should distinguish

## Task replacement

A client no longer pays a person for a specific task because AI produces an adequate substitute.

Example: generic product-description variants.

## Occupation replacement

The overall role disappears or employment collapses.

Current evidence for broad creative occupations is much weaker than evidence for task-level shifts.

## Productivity improvement

The same worker produces more output or completes a task faster.

This can raise wages/profits, reduce staffing needs, lower prices or increase total demand. Direction is not automatic.

## Job redesign

The role survives but shifts toward:

- direction;
- selection;
- client interaction;
- verification;
- curation;
- editing;
- rights/provenance;
- higher-complexity work.

## Price pressure

The task remains but clients expect lower prices because alternatives are cheaper.

## Increased competition

More people can enter the market because AI reduces skill/time barriers.

## Market expansion

Lower production cost creates projects that would not otherwise exist.

## New work

AI creates demand for integration, model/workflow design, curation, data/licensing, provenance and specialised human finishing.

These effects can coexist.

# 2. Demirci, Hannane and Zhu — exposed freelance postings

## Source

"The Effects of Generative AI on Online Freelancing" / Management Science research, repository version. [S23]

## Evidence

The study reports about a **21% decrease** in job postings in automation-prone writing/coding categories relative to manual-intensive categories after ChatGPT, and an approximately **17% decrease** in image-creation postings following exposure to generative image tools.

The remaining exposed jobs shifted toward greater complexity and higher pay.

## What it supports

- immediate task-demand substitution is real in at least one large online labour market;
- image generation is not only a hypothetical future effect;
- the surviving work can change composition rather than merely shrink.

## What it does not support

- a 21% decline in all writing jobs;
- a 17% decline in all illustrator employment;
- permanent economy-wide unemployment;
- a conclusion about high-end art, publishing authors, staff designers or physical performance.

## Chapter use

This is a better example than a dramatic anecdote about one company firing an artist because it provides market-level evidence while still requiring careful limits.

---

# 3. Teutloff et al. — aggregate stability hides large reallocations

## Source

"Winners and Losers of Generative AI: Early Evidence of Shifts in Freelancer Demand," *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*. [S24]

## Data

More than three million freelancer job postings, covering the pre/post-ChatGPT period.

## Findings useful to Chapter 8

- aggregate freelancer demand did not simply collapse;
- substitutable writing/translation skill clusters declined substantially relative to counterfactual trends, roughly 20–50% depending on cluster/specification;
- complementary machine-learning work grew;
- chatbot-development demand grew very strongly;
- job composition and skill demand shifted;
- novice-worker patterns also changed.

## Why it matters

This is an empirical example of why two headlines can both be misleading:

> "AI destroyed freelance demand."

and:

> "AI had no effect because total postings stayed stable."

Aggregate totals can hide large shifts between skill clusters.

## Limitations

- online-platform market;
- early period;
- skill clusters/classification methods;
- not all work labelled "creative";
- demand postings are not identical to completed contracts or earnings.

# 4. Hui, Reshef and Zhou — short-term employment and earnings

## Source

"The Short-Term Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence on Employment: Evidence from an Online Labor Market," *Organization Science*. [S25]

## Finding

Freelancers in occupations more exposed to generative AI experienced reductions in employment and earnings in the observed period; the research also considers image-generation exposure.

## Important nuance

Past quality/reputation did not simply immunise workers from exposure in the short term.

This challenges a comfortable claim sometimes made to creators:

> AI will only replace bad work; skilled professionals are safe.

The evidence does not justify that reassurance.

It also does not justify the reverse:

> skill no longer matters.

Exposure and market structure matter alongside skill.

# 5. Creator surveys: what they tell us and what they do not

## Australian authors and illustrators — Macquarie/ASA

A 2024 survey reported by the Australian Society of Authors included 419 author/illustrator respondents. 70% thought it possible or likely generative AI would replace their current income-generating work; 91% viewed uncompensated training as unfair; 82% reported not using generative AI in their work. [S48]

This is valuable evidence of **creator expectations and attitudes**.

It does not show 70% have actually lost work.

## Australian Society of Authors 2025

The ASA's 2025 survey had 928 respondents and was open to members and non-members. It reports:

- 21% using generative AI in creative practice;
- high uncertainty about whether respondents' works had been used in training;
- 87% saying, to their knowledge, neither they nor publishers had granted training permission;
- 89% concerned about style imitation. [S49]

The source also records some respondents suggesting beneficial creative uses.

This is a useful way to avoid portraying creators as uniformly non-users.

Limitation: ASA is an advocacy organisation; respondents self-select and the article uses campaigning language. Use it to report the surveyed creators' views, not to prove legal "theft" or economy-wide effects.

## APRA AMCOS music creators

The APRA AMCOS study reports 4,274 respondents across Australia/New Zealand. 54% agreed AI can assist human creative process, while 82% expressed concern about being able to make a living. [S50]

Again: use the former/latter as survey sentiment. The source's headline revenue-at-risk estimate for 2028 is a **forecast/commissioned model**, not observed loss.

# 6. Entry-level and apprenticeship effects

## Why the concern is credible

Many junior creative tasks are exactly the ones easiest to specify and evaluate:

- basic social graphics;
- simple copy;
- stock-style illustration;
- rough storyboards;
- resizing/adaptation;
- background music;
- simple edits;
- first-pass research/summaries.

If AI substitutes for those paid tasks, fewer people may receive the traditional paid practice that developed expertise.

Teutloff et al. provide some evidence of changing novice demand in the online market. [S24]

## Evidence boundary

There is not yet strong longitudinal evidence that:

1. entry-level creative jobs disappear at scale;
2. this causes fewer people to develop expert skills;
3. senior-skill shortages then emerge.

That three-step causal chain remains largely a concern/hypothesis.

The chapter can say:

> There is early evidence that some lower-complexity online work is more exposed. Whether this damages the long-term apprenticeship pipeline is an important question, but the long-term evidence is not yet mature.

That is much stronger editorially than stating the concern as fact or ignoring it.

# 7. Work that is easiest to substitute

Substitution is more likely where:

- the buyer needs something adequate rather than distinctive;
- the brief is easy to specify;
- quality is easy for a non-expert to judge;
- rights/provenance risk is low or ignored;
- revisions can be handled cheaply;
- no live human performance/relationship is valued;
- volume matters more than uniqueness.

Examples:

- generic background image;
- commodity ad variants;
- basic social copy;
- generic music bed;
- rough concept visual;
- stock-style imagery.

This does **not** imply low skill was historically required. It means the buyer may accept a lower-cost substitute.

---

# 8. Work that can remain human-differentiated

Human demand may remain stronger where buyers value:

- named reputation;
- personal/lived perspective;
- trusted reporting/testimony;
- physical performance;
- relationship/collaboration;
- exact controllability;
- rights certainty;
- cultural authority;
- accountability;
- handcrafted scarcity;
- bespoke understanding of a client;
- established fan communities.

This is not a guarantee of job security; it identifies dimensions on which a human offering can remain differentiated.

---

# 9. Market expansion

Lower creative production cost can create work that never existed before.

Examples:

- a sole trader can make a visual concept instead of buying no visual at all;
- an indie game can prototype more environments before funding;
- a podcaster can create temporary storyboards or localisation drafts;
- a disabled user can create visual material they could not manually execute;
- a small organisation can make internal training media it would never commission externally.

Economically, this is important because not every AI-created output corresponds to a lost commission. Some substitute for **nothing**.

The later writer should therefore avoid:

> Every AI image is one lost illustrator job.

But equally avoid:

> If some new demand exists, displacement is not real.

Both effects can occur.

---

# 10. Output expectations and work intensification

A productivity tool can raise employer/client expectations:

> If AI lets you make ten variants quickly, why not make fifty?

Potential effects:

- faster deadlines;
- more variants requested;
- smaller teams expected to do more;
- reduced budgets;
- creative workers spending more time reviewing/curating machine output;
- less time per individual piece.

Current quantitative evidence specific to creative industries is still patchy, but this is a recurring job-redesign concern and should be identified as such—not asserted as universal measured fact.

# 11. Abundance, spam and discoverability

## The economic shift

When production becomes cheap, scarcity moves.

Old bottleneck:

> Can we make this?

New bottleneck:

> Can anyone find, trust and care about this?

That creates economic value in:

- curation;
- reputation;
- recommendation;
- provenance;
- trusted brands/creators;
- moderation;
- anti-fraud systems;
- audience relationships.

## Deezer: abundance does not equal demand

Deezer reported that fully AI-generated tracks exceeded **50% of daily uploads** at a peak in 2026, around 90,000 per day. Yet it reported only around **1–3% of streams** went to fully AI-generated tracks. [S34]

This directly rebuts:

> If most new uploads are AI, most listening must be AI.

The supply share and demand share can be radically different.

Deezer also reports extensive fraud associated with AI-generated tracks and excludes fully AI-generated music from recommendations/editorial promotion under its current system. [S34]

## Spotify: spam is not identical to AI

Spotify said in September 2025 that it had removed more than **75 million spammy tracks** over the prior 12 months and strengthened protections against mass upload and impersonation. [S33]

Do not label those 75 million tracks "AI-generated"; the company's spam category is broader.

The useful chapter point is:

> Generative AI can amplify existing low-cost content/spam economics, forcing platforms to spend more effort on authenticity, fraud and discovery.

# 12. Human-made as a market signal

It is plausible that provable human craft becomes a differentiator in some markets:

- live performance;
- signed/limited physical art;
- "no generative AI" creative policies;
- provenance-rich photography;
- named authors/artists;
- human narration.

However, the research did not find enough broad evidence to claim a dominant premium "human-made market" has already emerged.

Treat it as an emerging strategy/possibility, not a forecast.

Provenance systems such as C2PA may support process disclosure, but they do not prove human authorship by absence/presence alone. [S30]

---

# 13. Economics of licensing

Training-data licensing can redirect value toward right holders, but creates unresolved economic design questions.

Potential benefits:

- consent/permission;
- compensation;
- clearer rights chains;
- lower litigation risk;
- sector-specific governance.

Potential costs:

- transaction complexity;
- concentrating AI development among companies able to afford large licences;
- difficulty identifying/compensating millions of contributors;
- disputes over valuation;
- fragmented rights;
- international inconsistency.

The UMG/Udio, WMG/Suno and Adobe Stock examples show real licensing/compensation models emerging. [S35] [S36] [S38]

They do not establish that one model will work for books, visual art, film and software equally.

# 14. What the chapter can say with confidence

## Strong

- Some exposed online freelance creative/writing/image tasks have experienced measurable demand/earnings declines after generative AI. [S23] [S24] [S25]
- Complementary AI-related work can grow at the same time. [S24]
- Creator surveys show substantial concern about income, consent and compensation. [S48] [S49] [S50]
- Generative AI can dramatically expand content supply; supply growth need not imply audience demand. [S34]
- Platform spam/impersonation/discoverability have become material operational issues. [S33] [S34]

## Qualified

- Entry-level creative pathways may be particularly vulnerable.
- AI can pressure rates.
- teams may be expected to produce more with fewer people.
- identifiable human creators may become a stronger market signal.

## Avoid as fact

- "AI is replacing artists."
- "AI is creating more creative jobs than it destroys."
- "Creative employment will collapse."
- "Only low-quality creators are affected."
- "High-skill creators are protected."
- "Most music people listen to is AI-generated."
- "Half of music listening is AI" because half of new Deezer uploads were AI.

# 15. Practical decision framework for readers

Before using AI instead of commissioning/doing creative work, ask:

1. **Is the need generic or distinctive?**
2. **Would I otherwise pay someone, do it myself, or not create it at all?**
3. **Do I need exclusive rights?**
4. **Does a person's performance/reputation/point of view matter?**
5. **Can I judge quality well enough to catch failures?**
6. **Do I need precise revisions?**
7. **Will I publish commercially?**
8. **Could the output imitate or appropriate a person/brand?**
9. **What does the provider permit?**
10. **Would disclosure matter to my audience/platform?**

This turns the economic debate into a useful reader decision without telling them what political/ethical conclusion to adopt.
