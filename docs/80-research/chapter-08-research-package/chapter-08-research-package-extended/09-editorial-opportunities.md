# 1. Best candidate diagram — Creative AI is a continuum, not two buckets

## Concept

A vertical or square-ish continuum suitable for a book page:

**Human-led**
1. Blank-page breaker
2. Variation engine
3. Production assistant
4. Co-generator
5. AI-led generation
6. Automated content generation
**AI-led**

For each stage, a small second column:

- human contribution;
- AI contribution;
- main question.

Example:

| Mode | Human role | AI role | Main question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blank-page breaker | defines problem/direction | suggests starts | does suggestion anchor me? |
| Variation engine | provides source/direction | creates alternatives | which variation fits? |
| Production assistant | controls creative plan | performs bounded transformation | did it preserve intent? |
| Co-generator | selects/edits substantial material | supplies expressive material | what did each contribute? |
| AI-led generation | short prompt/selection | supplies most detail | authorship/rights/disclosure? |
| Automated generation | defines system/rules | produces many outputs | abundance, moderation, responsibility? |

## Why it works

Beginners often ask "Was it made by AI?" as if yes/no answers the meaningful question. The continuum redirects attention to contribution and responsibility.

## Where it breaks down

Real workflows can move back and forth; "AI-led" is not a legal category.

# 4. Training versus retrieval versus generation diagram

## Concept

A vertical three-box diagram:

**Training**
Examples → repeated learning → model parameters

↓ later

**Generation**
Prompt + model parameters → new output

Optional branch:

**Retrieval**
Prompt → search/fetch external source → source supplied to model → answer

Small side note:

> Memorisation can occur, so "not a database lookup" does not mean "reproduction is impossible."

## Why it works

Corrects two major myths without mathematics.

## Where it breaks down

Actual modern training stacks are vastly more complex; some systems combine training, fine-tuning, retrieval and tool use.

# 8. Reader exercise — Human-first versus AI-first ideation

## Version A: Human first

1. Pick a small creative problem: a birthday invitation theme, a story premise, a poster concept.
2. Write five genuinely different directions without AI.
3. Then ask AI for five.
4. Group similar ideas.
5. Note which ideas changed your thinking.

## Version B: AI first

On another problem, reverse the order.

## Reflection

- Did the AI expand the space or anchor it?
- Were its ideas more polished but less unusual?
- Did your own ideas become variations of the first suggestion?

## Research basis

Inspired by fixation/diversity findings [S17] [S18], but the reader exercise is not a scientific experiment.

# 9. Reader exercise — "What did AI decide for me?"

Generate or inspect an AI-created scene where the prompt leaves details unspecified.

Checklist:

- age;
- gender;
- skin tone/ethnicity;
- body;
- occupation cues;
- clothing;
- wealth/class cues;
- location;
- family roles;
- visual beauty conventions.

Question:

> Which decisions came from me, and which came from the model's defaults?

Basis: image-bias research [S45] [S46] [S47].

Keep this reflective rather than accusatory.

# 10. Website interactive — Terms are not copyright

## UI concept

A five-card quiz:

> A music generator says your paid plan includes commercial rights. Which statements automatically follow?

Options:
- I may use it commercially under the provider contract.
- Copyright definitely exists.
- My result is exclusive.
- It cannot infringe a third party.
- I can falsely imply a real singer performed it.

Reveal why only the first is supported by the premise.

## Source anchors

Suno [S42] [S43], Canva [S41].

## Value

Turns an abstract legal distinction into a practical decision.

# 14. Side-by-side example — Same goal, different workflows

Goal: "Create a cover concept for a fictional local gardening club."

## Human-only
Sketch/moodboard → select typography → create vector/layout.

## AI-assisted
Human defines audience/message → AI generates visual directions → human selects one → conventional design tool rebuilds precise final layout.

## AI-led
Short prompt → first generated cover used unchanged.

Compare:
- speed;
- control;
- human authorship;
- editability;
- provenance;
- consistency;
- rights questions.

Do not claim one is morally superior; the point is to show why "AI used" is not enough information.

# 15. Side-by-side example — AI as critic versus AI as ghostwriter

Use a short original paragraph created for the book/website.

**Workflow A:** author writes; AI identifies three unclear sentences; author rewrites.

**Workflow B:** author gives premise; AI writes entire paragraph; author accepts.

Ask:

- where is the human expression?
- which workflow is more likely to preserve voice?
- which produces more authorship uncertainty?
- where can factual hallucinations enter?

This is more useful than a generic "AI can write" demo.

# 16. Possible callout — The polished-output trap

> **Watch Out:** A generated result can look finished before it has been checked. Polish is not the same as accuracy, originality, rights clearance, consistency or fitness for your purpose.

Why useful:
- durable across media;
- replaces outdated "AI output obviously looks fake" assumptions;
- connects Chapter 7 verification discipline to creative work.

# 17. Possible callout — AI can change your options before you make a choice

> **Key Idea:** Keeping the final decision human does not mean AI had no effect on judgement. The suggestions you see first can shape what you consider next.

Basis: fixation/similarity research [S17] [S18].

This is an important qualification to the book's effort/judgement framework.

# 18. Possible callout — "Commercial use" has layers

> **Plain English:** A tool can give you permission to sell an output without giving you a copyright monopoly over it. It can also be possible for you to own your human edits while the purely generated parts receive different protection.

Use current provider terms only as examples and keep law jurisdiction-aware. [S07] [S04] [S41] [S43]

# 19. Possible table — AI versus conventional tool

| Task | AI useful when | Conventional/human tool often better when |
|---|---|---|
| writing | exploring variants, reorganising | exact factual sourcing, distinctive final voice |
| image | concept exploration, rough illustration | exact vector/technical drawing, rights-sensitive brand |
| music | demo/arrangement exploration | exact performance/mix, clear performer identity |
| video | previsualisation, bounded generative edit | documentary truth, exact continuity |
| design | fast variants | typography, design systems, deterministic layout |

The table should avoid "AI bad at X forever"; phrase as current workflow fit.

# 20. Possible myth/reality table

## Myth
"AI just copies and pastes."

## Reality
Training normally learns distributed patterns rather than querying a scrapbook, but memorisation/reproduction can happen. [S26]-[S29]

## Myth
"AI never copies."

## Reality
Also false. See extraction research. [S26] [S27]

## Myth
"It's trained on everything."

## Reality
Training sources differ; licensed, public-domain, scraped, private, user and synthetic data all exist. [S13] [S37]

## Myth
"If I can use it commercially, I own the copyright."

## Reality
Contract permission and statutory copyright differ. [S42] [S43]

## Myth
"AI is replacing artists."

## Reality
Some tasks/markets show substitution; effects differ by task and complementary work also grows. [S23] [S24]

## Myth
"AI can't replace real artists."

## Reality
Buyers can substitute adequate AI output for some paid tasks even when it does not replicate the full craft/role.

## Myth
"C2PA detects AI."

## Reality
It records/verifies provenance claims where credentials exist. [S30]

# 21. Reflection prompt options

The plan asks for one reflection; keep it genuinely open.

### Option A — Process
> Think of something creative you care about. Which parts would you happily hand to AI, and which parts would make the work feel less like yours if you delegated them? Why?

### Option B — Rights
> If a model learned from your creative work without asking, would your view change if it could never reproduce your work? What if you were paid? What if you could opt out?

### Option C — Audience
> Where would you personally want AI use disclosed: a poster, a song, a novel, a documentary, a private card? What makes the difference?

### Option D — Skill
> Which creative skills do you use because you value the final result, and which do you value practising for their own sake?

These do not tell the reader which answer to adopt.

# 23. What not to build

Avoid:

- an "AI detector" demo presented as reliable truth;
- named living-artist style imitation as entertainment;
- a legal checker that tells users an output is "safe";
- a chart combining incomparable creator surveys as if they were one representative statistic;
- a "human vs AI" beauty contest with no methodology;
- a benchmark of product quality that will be obsolete before publication;
- a diagram implying every model stores a literal copy of its dataset;
- a binary badge that says "100% human" based only on missing metadata.
