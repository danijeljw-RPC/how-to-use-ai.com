# 02 — Research by Creative Domain

## How to use this file

The approved plan requires five domains: **writing, art/image creation, music/audio, video/filmmaking and design**.

For each domain this file separates:

- genuine current uses;
- where the technology is strong;
- where it remains weak;
- ordinary-reader workflows;
- professional workflow examples;
- substitution versus assistance;
- failures and misconceptions;
- rights/commercial considerations;
- and situations where conventional tools or human practitioners remain the better choice.

This is deliberately not a product catalogue.

---

# 1. Writing

## Current uses

Common useful roles include:

- brainstorming possible angles, scenes, examples or titles;
- creating structural alternatives;
- generating a deliberately rough first draft;
- compressing, expanding or reordering human-written material;
- changing tone for a defined audience;
- producing multiple variants for comparison;
- role-playing an editor, critic or reader;
- identifying repeated ideas or gaps in a long document;
- preparing summaries and synopses;
- generating prompts/questions that help the human continue;
- translation/localisation drafts where a qualified human can review important nuance.

## What it tends to do well

Language models are unusually strong at producing **plausible continuations and variants** quickly. In creative writing, this can be useful when the cost of generating alternatives is the bottleneck.

For a beginner, a high-value workflow is:

1. Write the premise and a few non-negotiable facts.
2. Ask for several *different directions*, not finished prose.
3. Choose none, one or a hybrid.
4. Draft in the writer's own voice.
5. Use AI later as a critic: identify weak motivation, pacing issues or unanswered questions.
6. Accept/reject the criticism rather than automatically accepting a rewrite.

This preserves a wider role for human framing and voice than "prompt → copy output."

## Evidence: assistance can help and homogenise

Doshi & Hauser's short-story experiment is unusually useful for beginners because the findings pull in opposite directions. Participants with access to generative-AI story ideas received higher ratings on average, especially participants who initially measured as less creative; at the same time, AI-assisted stories became more similar to each other. [S17]

Do **not** translate this into "AI makes bad writers good" or "AI makes all writing the same." The task was short, experimental and immediate. The defensible lesson is:

> A common model can improve an individual's local result while also acting as a shared source of ideas that reduces diversity across a group.

That can motivate a practical technique: **human-first ideation** before AI suggestions.

## Failure modes

- confident but invented factual detail inside historical or realistic fiction;
- conventional plot beats and phrasing;
- loss of a distinctive author's cadence when large passages are repeatedly rewritten;
- flattening ambiguity or deliberate oddness because the model optimises toward plausible text;
- continuity mistakes over long works;
- accidental imitation when a user asks for a named living author's voice;
- "editor" feedback that sounds authoritative but is generic or wrong;
- over-reliance on the first plausible suggestion;
- the author becoming reviewer of model prose rather than originator of ideas.

## Substitution versus assistance

Low-stakes generic copy is highly substitutable because buyers may value adequacy, speed and price more than distinctive authorship. Online freelance research finds exposed writing/translation categories declining after mainstream generative AI. [S23] [S24] [S25]

Distinctive long-form authorship is not well measured by those studies. Do not infer from freelance copywriting data that novels or journalism as whole occupations are disappearing.

## Publishing consideration

Amazon KDP currently requires publishers to disclose **AI-generated** text, images or translations, while it distinguishes that from **AI-assisted** work and does not require disclosure of the latter under that policy. [S32] This is a strong real-world demonstration that platforms already need a continuum more nuanced than "AI used / not used."

## When conventional or human methods may be better

- factual research requiring primary sources;
- legal or medical claims;
- maintaining a very specific established authorial voice;
- sensitive interviews and lived-experience narrative;
- final literary line editing where every word matters;
- work where provenance and human authorship are part of the product's value.

# 2. Art and Image Creation

## Current uses

- concept art and thumbnails;
- moodboards;
- rough visual directions before commissioning;
- illustration;
- background/texture generation;
- image expansion/outpainting;
- object removal/replacement;
- photorealistic mockups;
- visualisation of ideas that do not yet exist;
- fast variants of composition, lighting or colour;
- reference exploration;
- compositing elements into a human-controlled design.

## Strengths

The largest practical shift is **option cost**. Someone who cannot draw can produce a visual representation of an idea; a trained artist can create more concept directions before committing to a labour-intensive execution.

For nonprofessionals this can be genuinely enabling. A person can visualise:

- a room renovation concept;
- a fictional character;
- a book-cover direction;
- a storyboard frame;
- a party invitation;
- a small-business visual concept.

The chapter should still distinguish "visualising what I mean" from "commission-ready professional art."

## Current limitations that matter more than old 'bad hands' jokes

By 2026 it is weak to frame image generation primarily around visibly malformed output. Models have improved quickly. More durable limitations include:

- exact, repeatable identity across many scenes;
- precise composition constraints;
- reliable small text/typography;
- editability as vectors/layers/components;
- rights and provenance;
- accidental resemblance or memorisation;
- representational bias;
- maintaining a coherent original style across a long project;
- precise factual/historical representation;
- brand governance;
- consistency under client revision.

## Memorisation and copying nuance

Research has demonstrated that diffusion models can reproduce some training examples and that data duplication/text conditioning influence replication risk. [S27] [S29]

This does **not** justify saying every generated image is a collage. It does justify rejecting "the model never copies" as an absolute.

## Bias and hidden creative defaults

Generated imagery can introduce representational decisions the user never consciously made. Studies auditing professional imagery have found gender/skin-tone and racial representation biases. [S45] [S46] [S47]

The useful beginner lesson is not "AI images are biased therefore never use them." It is:

> When you ask a model to fill in unspecified details, it has to choose something. Those defaults can encode patterns in the model and training data. Inspect who is being represented, not only whether the image looks attractive.

## Alternative training/provenance models

Adobe says Firefly's generative models use licensed and public-domain content rather than indiscriminate customer content/general web mining. Its Stock contributor FAQ also describes contributor bonus payments connected to eligible content considered for training. [S37] [S38]

This is useful not as proof that Firefly is "ethically clean," but as evidence that **training-data business models are not all the same**. The current contributor arrangement also demonstrates trade-offs: Adobe states there is no individual Stock opt-out from Firefly training under the relevant contributor agreement.

## Style imitation

A prompt such as "in the style of [artist]" can raise different questions:

- an artist's *style* may not itself map neatly to copyright protection;
- a particular output can still reproduce protected expressive material;
- branding, attribution or passing-off questions can arise if an output is presented as genuine;
- a living creator may object ethically even where copyright does not provide a direct style right;
- provider terms may separately prohibit some identity/style uses.

The chapter should not teach "style is not copyrightable, so it is fine." Legal category and ethical acceptability are different.

## When conventional tools/humans are better

- exact vector logos and identity systems;
- accessible, production-ready typography;
- diagrams that must be factually precise;
- pixel-accurate edits;
- scientific/technical illustration;
- legally sensitive trademarks;
- documentary photography where evidentiary authenticity matters;
- distinctive commissioned art where the relationship with the artist is part of the value.

# 3. Music and Audio

## Current uses

- generating complete synthetic songs;
- rough demos and backing arrangements;
- chord/progression exploration;
- instrumental beds;
- variations in genre/instrumentation;
- sound effects and textures;
- stem-like transformations;
- synthetic singing;
- voice cloning;
- dubbing/localisation;
- restoration/cleanup and production assistance.

## Why music is a particularly useful Chapter 8 case

Music puts several Chapter 8 issues in one place:

- creative usefulness;
- training-data rights;
- performer identity;
- composition versus sound-recording rights;
- synthetic voices;
- platform flooding and streaming fraud;
- commercial-use terms;
- licensed training models;
- audience disclosure.

It therefore provides better evidence than a generic "AI can make a backing track" example.

## Commercial rights are not copyright

Suno's current help documentation is an unusually clean teaching source.

Its documentation distinguishes free/basic versus paid-plan rights and says paid subscribers receive ownership/commercial-use rights to qualifying songs under its terms. It separately warns that this does **not guarantee copyright protection**. [S42] [S43]

That enables a beginner-friendly five-question checklist:

1. Does the service permit commercial use?
2. As between me and the service, who claims/receives rights?
3. Does my country's copyright law protect my contribution/output?
4. Is the output exclusive, or could someone else receive similar material?
5. Could the output still infringe or appropriate someone else's rights?

## Licensed training and consent models are emerging

The 2025 UMG–Udio and WMG–Suno agreements are important because they demonstrate a possible market response to training disputes: licensed/authorised repertoires, compensation arrangements and, in the WMG/Suno announcement, artist controls over name/image/likeness/voice and compositions. [S35] [S36]

These are not proof that the licensing debate is solved. They are commercial deals by interested parties, and promised 2026 products/arrangements need current verification at publication.

## Platform abundance: making is no longer the only bottleneck

Deezer reported in July 2026 that fully AI-generated tracks exceeded half of daily uploads at a peak of around 90,000 per day, while accounting for only roughly 1–3% of streams. It also reported that a large proportion of streams on those AI-generated tracks were fraudulent and that it demonetised those streams. [S34]

This is one of the chapter's strongest examples because it demonstrates:

> Cheap production can create abundance without equivalent audience demand.

The new scarce resource may be attention, trust, curation and discoverability.

Spotify separately says it removed more than 75 million "spammy" tracks over the prior 12 months while strengthening policies against mass upload, deception and impersonation. [S33] Important: do **not** rewrite that as "75 million AI songs." Spotify's category is spam, not AI.

## Creator perspective in Australia/New Zealand

APRA AMCOS's research records both use and concern: 54% of surveyed respondents agreed AI can assist the human creative process, while large majorities expressed livelihood/copyright concerns. [S50]

The useful lesson is that "creator" and "AI user" are not mutually exclusive categories.

Its revenue-at-risk numbers are **forecasts**, not observed historical losses. If used, label them as commissioned/modelled scenarios.

## Voice and likeness

A cloned singer's voice is not merely a copyright-output question. Performer rights, consent, contracts and personality/publicity/digital-replica rules can apply differently.

SAG-AFTRA's 2026 interactive digital-replica guidance provides a concrete negotiated example requiring separate, specific written consent in covered situations. [S44]

## When conventional/human work may be better

- a performance where the human performer is the point;
- exact composition/orchestration under professional notation constraints;
- mastering/mixing decisions requiring controlled, deterministic audio engineering;
- music relying on cultural/community context;
- projects needing unambiguous rights chains;
- a distinctive commissioned score;
- voice work where informed performer consent is central.

# 4. Video and Filmmaking

## Current uses

- script/shot exploration;
- storyboard and previsualisation;
- concept frames;
- generated short shots;
- plate/background generation;
- rotoscoping/masking assistance;
- cleanup/object removal;
- extending a shot to solve an edit;
- effects;
- synthetic presenters/actors;
- dubbing and translated speech;
- lip synchronisation;
- voice cleanup;
- captioning and metadata assistance.

## Production assistant versus content generator

This domain makes the collaborator/substitute continuum easy to show.

**Production-assistant example:** Adobe Premiere's Generative Extend can create additional frames/audio to lengthen a clip. [S39] The editor still chose the shot, edit point and purpose; AI supplies a bounded transformation.

**AI-led-generation example:** a user supplies a short description and accepts a generated shot with most expressive details supplied by the model.

Both involve AI, but authorship, control, risk and disclosure questions differ substantially.

## Strengths

- speed in previsualisation;
- allowing small teams to test an idea before committing production money;
- filling narrowly defined gaps in post-production;
- localisation/dubbing;
- generating shots that would be costly/impossible to capture;
- prototyping.

## Weaknesses and failure modes

- temporal identity/continuity;
- physically or narratively inconsistent motion;
- hard-to-direct details;
- shot-to-shot continuity across longer sequences;
- difficulty producing exact revisions rather than a newly sampled alternative;
- synthetic performers creating consent/identity questions;
- documentary ambiguity;
- rights provenance for source/reference material;
- temptation to use generated spectacle where a simpler edit/shot would communicate better.

## Disclosure and audience expectation

YouTube's current policy distinguishes realistic synthetic/altered content that viewers could mistake for reality from minor assistance. Examples requiring disclosure include cloning another person's voice and synthetically generating music; examples such as using AI to help make an outline generally do not trigger the same requirement. [S31]

This is useful because it connects disclosure to **audience deception/materiality**, not simply to whether any AI touched the workflow.

## Digital replicas

A generated "actor" or cloned voice changes the legal and ethical problem. The relevant issue may be consent to reproduce a person's voice/likeness/performance rather than copyright in the final video alone. The UK government's 2026 report treats digital replicas separately from ordinary training/output questions, another sign that one copyright bucket is insufficient. [S14]

## When conventional methods may be better

- eyewitness/documentary footage;
- exact product demonstrations;
- interviews/testimony;
- performances where the real human event matters;
- shots requiring deterministic continuity;
- safety-critical instructional footage;
- simple conventional edits already faster in an NLE;
- content whose trust depends on demonstrating what actually happened.

# 5. Design

## Current uses

- layout directions;
- presentation/advertisement variants;
- palette exploration;
- style/mood exploration;
- template suggestions;
- resizing/adaptation across formats;
- rapid mockups;
- background/asset generation;
- copy variants in context;
- early brand exploration;
- image replacement and compositing.

Canva's Magic Design is a mainstream example of rapid layout/design generation. [S40]

## What AI changes

The value is not merely "design without a designer." It changes the economics of **variation**.

A designer can ask for ten rough directions and spend time comparing them. A non-designer can get something usable for a low-stakes invitation or internal slide. A marketing team can generate many adaptations from a direction.

But abundance creates a new problem: someone has to determine which variant actually fits the brief.

## Empirical caution: more generated options can narrow ideation

Wadinambiarachchi et al.'s visual-design experiment found generative-AI support increased fixation and reduced idea quantity/variety/originality. [S18]

That makes a useful practical design rule:

> If novelty matters, establish several independent directions before looking at AI-generated examples. Use AI to expand a space you have already opened, not always to define the space for you.

## Expertise and stage

Hou et al.'s work is especially useful because it breaks the slogan "AI makes designers faster." Benefits differed by stage and expertise. Lower-expertise participants could gain in implementation; experienced participants did not uniformly improve and in one condition took much longer without quality gain. [S21]

The lesson is not "experts should avoid AI." It is:

> Measure AI against the actual bottleneck. A tool that accelerates ideation may add friction in precision implementation, and vice versa.

## Rights and component licences

Canva's current AI terms say, as between Canva and the user and to the extent permitted by law, users retain/receive rights in inputs and outputs, while warning that outputs may not be unique and that users remain responsible for third-party rights. Canva designs can also incorporate separately licensed library content. [S41]

That is a practical reminder that a "design" can contain multiple rights layers:

- user's original material;
- generated material;
- stock/template components;
- fonts;
- third-party brands/logos;
- photographs of people;
- music/video assets.

"AI made it" does not erase those layers.

## When conventional tools or a professional are better

- exact vector identity/logo work;
- typography and typesetting;
- print production specs;
- accessible design systems;
- UI component systems;
- deterministic grid/layout control;
- regulated packaging;
- brand/trademark work;
- data visualisation requiring factual precision;
- high-stakes public communications.

# Cross-domain patterns

## Pattern 1: AI is strongest where many plausible answers are acceptable

Brainstorming, visual directions, alternate wording, mood, rough arrangements and concept shots benefit because there is no single exact output.

## Pattern 2: AI becomes frustrating when the target is exact

"Make something interesting" is easier than "change only this one kerning value, preserve every other property and give me the editable vector source." Conventional tools dominate many exact transformations.

## Pattern 3: judgement occurs before, during and after generation

The human can contribute by framing the problem, choosing references, creating source material, selecting outputs, editing, compositing, sequencing, performing and deciding whether to publish.

## Pattern 4: higher polish increases the need for judgement

A crude result announces its limitations. A polished result can conceal:

- factual error;
- stereotype;
- rights ambiguity;
- inconsistent provenance;
- subtle mismatch to brand/audience;
- genericness.

## Pattern 5: automation level changes the ethical/economic question

Generating one concept for personal use is different from automatically publishing 50,000 songs, images or articles. The technology may be similar; the effect on attention, fraud, discoverability and labour is not. [S33] [S34]

## Pattern 6: "AI-assisted" needs granularity

A useful continuum:

1. **Blank-page breaker** — AI proposes starting points.
2. **Variation engine** — human source/direction, AI creates alternatives.
3. **Production assistant** — AI performs bounded transformations.
4. **Co-generator** — AI supplies substantial expressive material that a human selects/edits.
5. **AI-led generation** — short human instruction; model supplies most expressive detail.
6. **Automated content generation** — many outputs produced with little case-by-case human direction.

This continuum is an editorial model, not a legal taxonomy. Different laws/platforms may define "generated" or "assisted" differently.
