# 03 — Copyright, Training Data, Authorship, Licensing and Provenance

## Scope and warning

This file exists to help a beginner-facing chapter avoid confident but incorrect legal slogans. It is **research, not legal advice**.

Copyright and AI questions depend on:

- jurisdiction;
- what material was copied;
- how it was obtained;
- what the system did with it;
- whether an exception applies;
- what the output contains;
- how much human authorship is present;
- contractual terms;
- and sometimes rights outside copyright entirely.

The final chapter should explain this at a conceptual level and send current/time-sensitive detail to the companion website.

---

# 1. Training data in plain English

## Dataset

A **dataset** is a collection of material used in developing or evaluating a model. Depending on the system it may include text, images, audio, video, code, metadata or other data.

"Training data" should not be described as one homogeneous pile scraped from the open internet. Current sources can include:

- material collected from publicly accessible websites;
- licensed collections;
- public-domain works;
- proprietary/private datasets;
- content submitted by users, where terms permit;
- synthetic data generated by other systems;
- hand-labelled examples;
- curated benchmark or preference data.

The European Commission's current training-content template is useful because it explicitly recognises categories including scraped online sources, private datasets, user data and synthetic data. [S13]

## Web scraping

**Web scraping** is automated retrieval of information from websites. Scraping is a technical collection method, not a legal conclusion. Whether a particular scrape is permitted can depend on copyright, contract, access controls, privacy/data-protection rules and the facts.

## Model training

A beginner-safe description:

> During training, the system repeatedly processes examples and adjusts a large set of numerical parameters so that its predictions better fit patterns in the training material.

Avoid saying the model "stores every work" as if it were a conventional media library. Also avoid saying it "doesn't store anything from training" in an absolute sense.

## Parameters / weights

Parameters are numerical values learned during training. They encode distributed statistical relationships. They are not normally organised as a folder containing one source work per file.

This matters because ordinary generation is not usually "search the training set for a matching paragraph/image and paste it."

## Memorisation

**Memorisation** is the important exception to simplistic explanations. Research has demonstrated that some trained examples can be reproduced or extracted, including verbatim text and training images. [S26] [S27]

Duplication is one factor. Kandpal et al. found deduplication materially reduced memorised emissions in studied language-model settings. [S28]

Therefore:

- "models are just databases" — misleading;
- "models never reproduce training data" — also misleading.

## Retrieval

**Retrieval** is different from training. In retrieval-augmented systems, the application searches or fetches an external source at response time and gives relevant material to the model.

A simple table:

| Mechanism | When source material is used | Beginner analogy | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training | During model development | adjusting a pattern generator from many examples | source copying/licensing; memorisation |
| Generation | When the model creates an output from learned parameters | sampling a new continuation from learned patterns | similarity, errors, stereotypes |
| Retrieval | At response time from an external source | looking something up before answering | source rights, attribution, access, quotation |

The analogy column is explanatory only, not legal.

# 2. What "AI stealing" can mean

The phrase "AI stealing" compresses distinct propositions.

## A. Dataset-copying claim

A rights holder may object that making copies of works for dataset construction or training requires permission.

Legal answer varies by jurisdiction and exception.

## B. Source-provenance claim

Even if a later analytical use might qualify for an exception, how the developer obtained the copy can be a separate issue. US copyright analysis and litigation make source provenance increasingly important. [S08]

Do not collapse:

> "This use might be fair use"

into:

> "Any way of obtaining the works is therefore lawful."

## C. Consent claim

Some creator objections are not reducible to infringement. A creator may argue that a commercial system should not use their work without explicit permission even if law permits the use.

That is a normative/ethical position.

## D. Compensation claim

A creator or collecting society may accept training in principle but argue for licensing or remuneration.

This can produce different policy preferences:

- individual licences;
- collective licences;
- blanket licences;
- statutory remuneration;
- revenue sharing;
- opt-in;
- opt-out/rights reservation.

## E. Memorisation/reproduction claim

A model may generate protected expression that is identical or substantially similar to a source. That is a different issue from whether training itself was permitted.

Research shows memorisation can occur. [S26] [S27] [S29] Whether a particular output infringes remains a legal/factual question.

## F. Style imitation claim

A creator may object that the system can mimic a recognisable style even if the output does not reproduce a particular protected work.

Style, copyright, passing off, trademark, contract and ethics are not interchangeable categories.

## G. Voice/likeness claim

Cloning a performer can implicate consent, performer rights, contractual rights, personality/publicity rights or digital-replica rules. Ordinary copyright in the generated file is only part of the issue. [S14] [S44]

## H. Economic appropriation claim

A creator may mean:

> My work contributed to a system now competing with my labour, without my permission or payment.

This is an economic/ethical argument. Copyright may or may not supply the remedy the creator wants.

# 3. Four-jurisdiction snapshot

## Australia

### Current position relevant to Chapter 8

Australia's Copyright Act provides specific exceptions including fair dealing rather than a broad US-style fair-use provision. [S03]

In October 2025 the Australian government explicitly said it was **not considering a text-and-data-mining exception**. The Copyright and Artificial Intelligence Reference Group's current priority areas include:

- fair/legal avenues and licensing arrangements for copyright material used in AI;
- certainty about copyright law and AI-generated material;
- cheaper enforcement routes for output-related infringement. [S01] [S02]

This is a particularly important correction for an Australian book because it prevents importing US or EU assumptions.

### Human authorship / generated output

Australian copyright traditionally depends on human authorship/originality. Arts Law's current explanation states that purely AI-generated material without sufficient human authorship may not attract copyright, while human creative contributions in an AI-assisted work can still be protected. The threshold created by prompting/iteration is not safely summarised as a universal rule. [S04]

The academic literature continues to debate how Australian originality/authorship doctrine should treat modern generative workflows. [S05]

### Beginner wording

Safe:

> In Australia, whether AI training or an AI-assisted output is protected depends on ordinary copyright rules and the particular facts. Australia has not adopted the broad AI/TDM exception currently being debated in some other jurisdictions.

Avoid:

> AI training is illegal in Australia.

Why: the absence of a general TDM exception does not decide every possible training act, licence, public-domain input or exception.

Avoid:

> AI art has no copyright in Australia.

Why: human contributions, selection, arrangement, editing and source material can matter.

### Recheck before publication

Yes. CAIRG policy work is active. [S01]

## United States

### Fair use is fact-specific

The US Copyright Office's Part 3 report is the strongest synthesis for beginners because it refuses a blanket answer.

It explains that generative-AI training can involve acts within copyright owners' exclusive rights, while fair-use analysis depends on factors including:

- purpose and character;
- the type of source work;
- amount/substantiality;
- market effect;
- source provenance;
- the relationship between training purpose and expressive substitution;
- safeguards against infringing outputs. [S08]

The Office treats some research/analysis uses as more plausibly transformative while warning that commercial use of vast bodies of copyrighted expressive works to create competing expressive material—especially where sources were unlawfully accessed—can present a much harder fair-use case. [S08]

### Current court warning: Thomson Reuters v ROSS

On 29 September 2026 the Third Circuit affirmed Thomson Reuters' win over ROSS Intelligence in a case involving Westlaw headnotes used indirectly to build a competing legal-research system. [S09]

At the research cut-off the appellate reasoning was still sealed pending redactions. [S10]

This should **not** be presented as:

> The US has ruled AI training is not fair use.

Reasons:

- the facts concern legal headnotes and a competing legal search product;
- the decision is one appellate case, not every generative model;
- the opinion's detailed reasoning was not public at the cut-off.

It is nevertheless important because it disproves the opposite slogan:

> US courts have established that AI training is fair use.

### Copyrightability of outputs

The US Copyright Office's Part 2 says AI-assisted work is not automatically excluded from copyright. Protection can cover sufficient human-authored expression, including creative selection/arrangement or modification. Mere prompting, by itself, generally does not establish authorship of the expressive elements supplied by the model under the Office's analysis. [S07]

### Beginner wording

> US copyright does not have a special blanket rule that all AI training is lawful or unlawful. Fair use is evaluated on the facts, and human authorship remains central when deciding what parts of AI-assisted output can be copyrighted.

### Recheck before publication

Urgent. ROSS appellate reasoning, other generative-AI cases and USCO Part 3 final publication may change the landscape. [S06] [S09] [S10]

## European Union

### Text and data mining

The DSM Copyright Directive creates two important TDM regimes. [S11]

- **Article 3:** mandatory exception for research organisations and cultural-heritage institutions carrying out TDM for scientific research where they have lawful access, subject to the directive's conditions.
- **Article 4:** broader TDM exception for lawfully accessible works, but the exception does not apply where rights have been expressly reserved; for content publicly available online the reservation should be machine-readable.

This is materially different from both Australia and US fair use.

### AI Act transparency/copyright duties

The AI Act adds a different layer: GPAI providers have obligations including:

- a policy to comply with EU copyright and related-rights law;
- measures to identify/respect rights reservations;
- a sufficiently detailed public training-content summary;
- technical/documentation obligations. [S12]

The Commission's current mandatory summary template covers broad source categories and is intended to help right holders understand what content types/sources were used. Enforcement timelines and pre-existing-model transition periods are staged. [S13]

Do not say:

> The EU requires every training file to be publicly listed.

The summary regime is not a public item-by-item dataset dump.

### Beginner wording

> The EU has explicit text-and-data-mining rules, including a broader exception that can be affected by a rights holder's reservation. Separately, the AI Act requires general-purpose model providers to maintain copyright policies and publish structured summaries of training content.

### Recheck before publication

Yes. Court interpretation, rights-reservation standards and AI Act enforcement are developing.

## United Kingdom

### Existing TDM rule

The current UK report states that the existing data-mining exception applies to **non-commercial research only**. [S14]

### 2026 policy change

The government previously consulted on a broader exception with an opt-out/right-reservation mechanism. In March 2026 it stated that this approach was **no longer its preferred way forward**, citing strong stakeholder disagreement and gaps in economic evidence. It plans further evidence gathering and consideration of alternatives. [S14] [S15]

This is a good example of why research packs must date policy claims.

### Computer-generated works: a global exception to easy slogans

The UK currently has a specific statutory category for certain literary, dramatic, musical and artistic works "generated by a computer in circumstances such that there is no human author." The current government report describes a 50-year protection and a deemed-author rule based on the person making the arrangements necessary for creation. [S14]

The same March 2026 report proposes removing that specific protection because the government found minimal evidence of its economic value, while preserving copyright for qualifying human-authored AI-assisted work and noting separate entrepreneurial rights such as sound-recording/film rights. [S14]

Therefore the statement—

> Purely AI-generated work can never be copyrighted.

—is not globally accurate as a description of current statutory law.

### Recheck before publication

Yes. The proposed removal is not the same thing as enacted removal.

# 4. Copyright, ethics and economics are separate layers

A strong Chapter 8 table could distinguish:

| Question | Example | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Was a protected copy made? | dataset contains an author's book | copyright/legal |
| Was the copy permitted by licence/exception? | licence, TDM exception, fair use | copyright/legal |
| Did the creator consent? | creator never opted in | ethical/contractual/policy |
| Was the creator compensated? | no training licence/payment | economic/ethical |
| Does output reproduce protected expression? | near-verbatim paragraph/image | copyright/legal |
| Does output imitate style? | recognisable aesthetic without direct copy | ethical; sometimes other law |
| Does output clone identity? | synthetic singer/actor | performer/personality/contract/etc. |
| Does the system compete with creator's market? | commission replaced | economic; can also enter fair-use analysis |
| Is disclosure expected? | synthetic realistic video | platform/audience norm/regulation |

The writer should resist using one answer as a proxy for all rows.

# 5. Authorship and originality in mixed workflows

## Binary labels are too weak

"Human-made" and "AI-made" hide the actual process.

Consider:

- human writes a novel and uses AI only to identify repeated words;
- human writes an image prompt and accepts the first generated image unchanged;
- human photographs a scene, generates replacement sky elements, composites them manually and paints over them;
- human writes lyrics/melody, AI proposes an arrangement, human performers record the final version;
- human automatically publishes thousands of generated images from a prompt list.

All are "AI used," but the human expressive contribution differs greatly.

## Contribution types that can matter

Depending on jurisdiction and facts:

- original human source material;
- selection and arrangement;
- compositing;
- editing and transformation;
- hand-drawn or written additions;
- sequencing;
- iterative direction;
- performance;
- choosing among outputs.

The final book should not promise that a particular amount of prompting or editing crosses a legal threshold.

## Practical record-keeping

For commercial/high-value work, a sensible non-legal practical measure is to keep:

- human drafts/sketches;
- source files;
- prompt/history where available;
- model/tool/version and date;
- licences for stock/reference assets;
- edit/compositing history;
- disclosure/provenance metadata.

This can help explain the creative process and rights chain later.

# 6. Five distinctions beginners need

## 1. Provider contractual ownership

A service can say that **as between the service and the user**, the user owns or is assigned whatever rights the provider has in an output.

Canva's current AI terms contain this sort of language, qualified by law and third-party/licensed content. [S41]

## 2. Permission to use commercially

A provider can permit commercial use under its contract.

Suno gives qualifying paid-plan outputs commercial-use rights under its terms. [S42]

## 3. Statutory copyright protection

Whether copyright actually exists is determined by applicable law, not merely provider wording.

Suno explicitly warns that commercial rights/ownership under its terms do not guarantee copyright protection. [S43]

## 4. Exclusivity

Even if the user has rights, the generated result may not be unique. Canva's AI terms warn that other users may receive similar or identical outputs. [S41]

## 5. Non-infringement / third-party rights

A provider giving permission cannot magically eliminate someone else's copyright, trademark, performer, publicity/personality or contractual rights. Users often remain responsible for third-party claims.

### Beginner summary

> "I can use it" is not the same as "I own a copyright monopoly over it," and neither means "nobody else has a claim."

This is one of the highest-value practical lessons in the research package.

# 7. Style imitation, voice and identity

## Style

A creative style is generally a higher-level set of features rather than one particular protected expression. Copyright often does not grant a simple monopoly over "style" as such.

But the analysis does not stop there:

- a specific output can copy protected elements;
- the prompt/reference can be evidence of deliberate imitation;
- a misleading attribution may raise passing-off/trademark/consumer issues;
- provider terms can impose restrictions beyond copyright;
- the creator can have a strong ethical objection even without a direct copyright cause of action.

Do not teach "style isn't copyright, therefore anything goes."

## Voice and likeness

Synthetic voices and digital replicas are more obviously personal.

SAG-AFTRA's 2026 contractual guidance shows one practical response: negotiated consent requirements for covered interactive digital replicas. [S44]

The UK government's 2026 report treats digital replicas in a separate section and notes the fragmented mix of legal tools rather than a single universal image/voice right. [S14]

The US Copyright Office's AI study similarly treated digital replicas as its own Part 1 topic. [S06]

The conceptual lesson:

> Copyright protects creative works; a person's identity and performance can involve different rights.

# 8. Training-data licensing models

There is no single industry model.

## Open web / exception-based models

Some developers argue training on lawfully accessible works is or should be permitted under copyright exceptions/fair use, especially where the model learns general patterns and does not reproduce works.

Creative Commons and EFF articulate versions of this argument. [S53] [S54] [S55]

## Licensed/public-domain models

Adobe describes Firefly as trained on licensed/public-domain material rather than general web scraping/customer content. [S37]

The Adobe Stock contributor program includes training-related contributor bonus arrangements. [S38]

## Sector licensing deals

UMG/Udio and WMG/Suno announced licensed/authorised music-model arrangements following disputes/litigation. [S35] [S36]

## Collective licensing

Creator organisations and collecting societies often favour collective or remunerated licensing where individual negotiation at training scale would be impractical. [S50]

## Opt-in / opt-out / rights reservation

These mechanisms allocate the default differently:

- **opt-in:** no training unless permission is granted;
- **opt-out:** training allowed unless rights holder objects;
- **rights reservation:** legal/technical mechanism by which a rights holder reserves a use;
- **collective licence:** a body licenses repertoire on behalf of a class of rights holders.

They also create practical questions:

- Can machine-readable reservations work across the web?
- Who authenticates a person claiming rights?
- What about old copies/datasets?
- What about models trained overseas?
- What happens to downstream models?
- Is compensation practical for billions of items?
- Do small/open developers get excluded by licensing cost?
- Can creators meaningfully exercise choice?

The UK 2026 report is useful precisely because it records uncertainty rather than claiming one mechanism is solved. [S14] [S15]

# 9. Provenance and Content Credentials

## What C2PA does

C2PA defines a technical framework for attaching cryptographically bound **provenance assertions** to digital content. [S30]

A credential can record claims such as:

- what tool created/edited content;
- what operations occurred;
- whether generative AI was involved;
- identity/credential information where supported.

## What it does not do

It does not universally answer:

- "Was this image generated by AI?"
- "Is this photo true?"
- "Was no AI used?"
- "Who morally authored this?"
- "Does this content infringe copyright?"

Reasons include:

- not every tool writes C2PA;
- credentials can be absent;
- provenance chains can start after earlier unrecorded steps;
- export/screenshot/platform processing can strip or break metadata;
- a perfectly authentic provenance record can describe content that is itself staged or misleading.

### Good beginner phrase

> Provenance is closer to a signed chain of editing claims than a magic lie detector.

Mark this as an analogy, not the formal definition.

# 10. Platform disclosure is becoming workflow-specific

## YouTube

YouTube's current altered/synthetic-content policy focuses on **meaningful realistic alteration** and audience expectations, rather than requiring an "AI" label for every assistive use. Synthetic music and another person's cloned voice are examples that can require disclosure; outline/script assistance is generally different. [S31]

## Amazon KDP

KDP distinguishes AI-generated from AI-assisted content and currently requires disclosure of generated text/images/translations. [S32]

## Spotify

Spotify is working toward more granular industry metadata/disclosure rather than treating all AI use as one binary category. [S33]

This reinforces the editorial continuum in `02-creative-domains.md`.

# 11. Misconceptions — legal edition

## "If it was publicly accessible online, anyone can train on it."

Incomplete. Public access does not by itself erase copyright. Exceptions, fair use/fair dealing, licences, rights reservation and jurisdiction still matter. [S03] [S08] [S11]

## "If training is fair use, the source can be pirated."

Too broad. Acquisition/provenance can be analysed separately from training purpose. [S08]

## "If training isn't copyright infringement, it is ethically fine."

Legal permission and ethical consent/compensation are different questions.

## "If something feels unethical, it must be illegal."

Also false. Ethical norms can exceed current law.

## "AI style imitation is automatically copyright infringement."

Too broad. Style and protected expression are not identical.

## "Style imitation is legally harmless."

Also too broad. A particular output, source/reference use, attribution, branding or identity could create other legal issues.

## "The tool owns it, so I don't."

Provider terms vary, and statutory rights vary.

## "The tool says I own it, so I have exclusive copyright."

Not necessarily. [S41] [S43]

## "Commercial use means safe."

No. It is one permission layer. [S42] [S43]

## "AI-generated works cannot be copyrighted anywhere."

False as a global claim; current UK law alone makes that statement unsafe, even while reform is proposed. [S14]
