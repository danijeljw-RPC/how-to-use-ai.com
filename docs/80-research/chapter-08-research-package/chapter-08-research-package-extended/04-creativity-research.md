# 04 — Creativity Research: What the Evidence Actually Supports

## Why this file matters

The current Chapter 8 draft correctly avoids claiming a final answer to "Does AI kill creativity?" but is too light to help readers understand what evidence exists.

The research literature is now good enough to say more than "it depends" while still avoiding a false universal conclusion.

The most useful distinction is:

> **Individual output quality and collective creative diversity are different outcomes.**

A tool can improve one while worsening the other.

---

# 1. What does "creativity" mean?

## Standard research definition

Runco and Jaeger summarise a widely used view: a creative product generally requires **originality** plus **effectiveness/appropriateness/usefulness**. [S16]

This is useful because a random output can be novel without being useful, and a competent but conventional output can be useful without being creative.

## Why artistic creativity is broader

For Chapter 8, creativity may also involve:

- intention;
- personal voice;
- expression;
- surprise;
- interpretation;
- craft;
- emotional communication;
- lived experience;
- cultural meaning;
- relationship to a tradition/community;
- audience reception.

A laboratory rating of "creativity" cannot settle whether a novel, painting or performance has authentic meaning.

## Four different questions often collapsed into "Can AI be creative?"

1. Can the model generate something statistically novel?
2. Can people rate the output as original/useful/beautiful?
3. Can AI help a human produce a more highly rated result?
4. Does the model itself possess intention, experience or a creative point of view?

Experimental research can address the first three more directly than the fourth.

The chapter does not need to decide the philosophical fourth question.

# 2. Doshi & Hauser 2024 — individual uplift, collective similarity

## Source

Anil R. Doshi and Oliver P. Hauser, "Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content," *Science Advances* (2024). [S17]

## Research question

Does access to generative-AI ideas change the creativity/quality of short stories, and what happens to similarity across writers?

## Participants/task

The study involved 293 people writing short stories. Conditions varied access to generative-AI ideas, including no AI idea, access to one idea and access to multiple ideas.

Stories were evaluated by other participants/readers on dimensions including creativity/quality-related measures.

## Main finding

AI idea access improved average ratings of the stories. The gains were especially notable among participants who were initially lower on measured creativity.

At the same time, AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other.

## Why it matters

This directly supports both propositions:

> AI can help an individual produce a better-rated result in a short creative task.

and:

> A group drawing suggestions from a similar generative system can become less diverse.

They are not contradictory because one measures **local quality** and the other **distributional diversity**.

## What it does not establish

- It does not show professional novelists write better with AI.
- It does not measure months/years of skill development.
- It does not prove all model-assisted writing converges.
- It does not prove the participants' personal creative voice was permanently harmed.
- It does not establish what happens when writers use AI as critic/editor rather than idea generator.
- It does not show readers would react the same way if they knew AI was used.

## Chapter opportunity

This should probably be the chapter's flagship creativity study because it gives readers a genuinely interesting, non-ideological result.

A simple figure could show:

**Individual axis:** average output rating ↑  
**Group axis:** similarity ↑ / diversity ↓

# 3. Wadinambiarachchi et al. 2024 — fixation in visual ideation

## Source

"The Effects of Generative AI on Design Fixation and Divergent Thinking," CHI 2024. [S18]

## Research question

How does exposure/use of generative AI affect visual design ideation, fixation and divergent thinking?

## Participants/task

60 participants completed a visual ideation task under conditions involving generative-AI image support.

## Findings

The study found evidence that AI support could:

- increase fixation on initial examples;
- reduce the number of ideas generated;
- reduce idea variety;
- reduce originality.

Prompting/interaction strategy mattered.

## Why it matters

"AI gives you more ideas" sounds self-evident because the model can generate many images. This study shows the opposite can happen at the **human ideation level**: seeing machine suggestions can anchor the person and narrow their exploration.

The model's output count is not the same thing as the person's divergent thinking.

## Practical implication

For creative work where novelty matters:

1. Generate several human directions before opening the model.
2. Write down the non-obvious alternatives.
3. Use AI to branch each direction.
4. Compare across branches.
5. Deliberately ask for counter-directions, not merely "more like this."

This is an editorial/practical inference, not a tested universal remedy.

## Limitations

- small sample;
- one class of design task;
- short-term;
- system/prompt dependent;
- not a measure of final commercial design quality.

# 4. Meincke et al. and Lee/Chung — why interpretation matters

## Meincke et al. 2025

Meincke and colleagues argue that generative AI can increase individual performance while reducing collective idea diversity, and that this distinction matters for innovation/learning. [S19]

Their analysis is valuable because it forces researchers/readers to specify the level of analysis:

- person;
- team;
- organisation;
- market/culture.

## Lee and Chung reply

Lee and Chung accept that diversity questions matter but caution against overextending the finding from a setting where people may submit one selected idea rather than expose their full brainstorming set. [S20]

## Chapter lesson

Do not present the homogenisation thesis as universally settled.

A useful formulation:

> Several experiments now show that shared generative systems can create anchoring or similarity effects. How large this becomes in real professional work depends on the task, prompting, model diversity, human expertise and whether people generate independent ideas before consulting AI.

# 5. Hou et al. — expertise and workflow stage

## Source

Hou et al., Information Systems Research, online publication 2025. [S21]

## Why the study is valuable

Many AI-productivity claims treat "design" as one activity. This work separates **ideation** and **implementation** and considers user expertise.

## Findings useful to the chapter

Across two design studies:

- generative AI could help ideation;
- benefits were not uniform across workflow stages;
- less-experienced participants could gain in implementation;
- experienced users did not necessarily receive the same quality benefit;
- in one implementation condition, higher-expertise users took roughly 57% longer without a quality improvement.

## Interpretation

This undermines two simplistic claims:

> AI helps beginners but experts don't need it.

and:

> AI always saves experts time.

The actual effect depends on where AI is inserted and what the expert is trying to control.

An expert may lose time correcting nondeterministic output that a conventional tool would implement exactly.

## Connection to effort vs judgement

This is direct evidence that "effort-heavy = automate it" is insufficient. What looks like effort from outside may encode expert micro-judgements or may be faster with deterministic tools.

# 6. Cognitive mechanisms worth explaining

## Anchoring

The first suggestion or example influences later judgement. AI can create an anchor by supplying a plausible first answer.

This is especially relevant when a user asks:

> Give me the best idea.

A safer creative prompt may request deliberately different families of ideas, or the person may ideate first.

## Fixation

A stronger creative concept than ordinary anchoring: exposure to an example can make it harder to escape its structure/features. [S18]

## Cognitive offloading

People routinely use tools to move work out of working memory: notes, calculators, spellcheck, search and reference materials.

AI can offload:

- drafting;
- recall;
- option generation;
- summarisation;
- transformation.

Offloading is not automatically harmful. The unresolved question is **which mental operations a person wants to continue practising**.

## Automation bias / deference

A polished suggestion can feel more authoritative than it deserves.

In creativity, this may show up as:

- accepting a conventional story ending;
- assuming the model's visual direction is "professional";
- treating a generated chord progression as the natural choice;
- failing to search outside AI suggestions.

## Metacognition

Good creative AI use requires knowing:

- what you are trying to do;
- what you personally care about;
- what the tool is weak at;
- when you are accepting a suggestion because it is good versus because it is easy.

Long-term metacognitive effects need more research.

# 7. Individual versus group-level creativity

This distinction deserves explicit treatment.

## Individual level

Questions:

- Does the person's output receive higher ratings?
- Do they produce more ideas?
- Do they work faster?
- Do less-skilled participants close some of the gap?
- Do experts gain anything?

Evidence: sometimes yes, sometimes mixed. [S17] [S21]

## Group/collective level

Questions:

- Are everyone's outputs more similar?
- Does a shared model create aesthetic convergence?
- Are unusual ideas underrepresented?
- Does a market flood with near-substitutable material?

Evidence: experimental similarity/fixation findings support concern, but economy-wide cultural homogenisation is harder to measure. [S17] [S18] [S19]

## Cultural level

Claims such as "AI will make culture boring" are broader than current experimental evidence.

Possible mechanisms exist:

- shared training data;
- shared models;
- similar prompt idioms;
- recommender incentives;
- low marginal production cost;
- generated content feeding future training data;
- preference optimisation toward average audience response.

But these mechanisms do not by themselves establish a measured long-term cultural outcome.

The chapter should label this as an emerging concern rather than a proven future.

# 8. Creativity and skill level

## Evidence for levelling

Doshi & Hauser found stronger immediate gains among less-creative participants. [S17]

Hou et al. found lower-expertise participants could benefit in design implementation where high-expertise participants did not see the same gains. [S21]

This supports a real positive interpretation:

> Generative AI can lower the skill threshold needed to produce a competent-looking result in some tasks.

That can broaden participation.

## But levelling has two meanings

**Positive:** people who previously could not express an idea visually/musically/textually can now do so.

**Economic:** if adequate low-end output becomes cheap, paid work at that tier can lose demand.

The same capability can be empowering for one person and economically threatening for another.

That is not a contradiction.

# 9. Long-term skill development: evidence gap

The current evidence base is dominated by:

- short experiments;
- bounded tasks;
- immediate quality ratings;
- early labour-market effects;
- surveys.

It is not yet strong enough to conclude:

- AI use causes permanent loss of writing ability;
- AI use prevents people becoming artists/designers;
- AI use creates better professionals over years;
- habitual AI brainstorming permanently narrows originality.

The chapter can analogise to over-reliance cautiously, but should mark long-term causal claims as unresolved.

A useful question for the reader:

> Which part of this skill do I want to keep practising myself?

Examples:

- a novice writer may want to draft scenes rather than always request them;
- a designer may want to sketch independent directions before generation;
- a musician may use AI for backing arrangements while continuing to compose melodies;
- a filmmaker may generate storyboard alternatives while learning shot grammar.

# 10. Human creativity versus machine output

The book does not need to answer whether AI is "really creative."

It can distinguish:

## Novel combination

A model can generate combinations not previously encountered verbatim.

## High creativity score

Human raters can judge an output as novel/useful/appealing.

## Intentional expression

A person may create in order to communicate a lived experience, belief or emotional point of view.

## Craft/performance

A person's physical or intellectual skill may itself be valued irrespective of whether a machine could create a similar-looking product.

## Cultural authorship

Communities decide what counts as meaningful art through institutions, traditions, audiences and relationships—not only output statistics.

This lets readers understand why two people can agree that a generated image looks impressive but disagree about whether the machine is "creative."

# 11. Accessibility and participation

Generative systems can lower practical barriers for people who:

- cannot draw manually;
- have motor impairments;
- struggle with conventional writing interfaces;
- lack production equipment;
- lack formal training;
- communicate in a second language;
- need speech/voice alternatives.

This is a genuine benefit and should be researched/covered on its own terms.

However, strong large-sample empirical evidence specifically linking generative AI to sustained creative participation by disabled users is still limited compared with the general creativity literature.

Do not use accessibility rhetorically as:

> creator concerns do not matter because AI helps disabled users.

Both concerns can be true.

A careful wording:

> Generative interfaces can open creative activities to people for whom conventional tools are inaccessible or prohibitively difficult. That benefit does not settle separate questions about training consent, compensation or professional displacement.

# 12. Bias as a creative influence

Generated systems do not merely render the user's fully specified intent. They fill unspecified details.

Research auditing generated professional images has found demographic imbalances and stereotyped representation. [S45] [S46] [S47]

For a creator, bias is therefore not only an abstract fairness problem. It can affect:

- who appears as the doctor, CEO, nurse or engineer;
- skin tone and gender presentation;
- age/body defaults;
- cultural clothing/setting;
- beauty conventions;
- family composition;
- geographical visual shorthand.

Practical creative check:

> What did the model decide that I never explicitly asked it to decide?

That question is more useful for beginners than a generic warning that "AI can be biased."

---

# 13. Does audience knowledge of AI involvement change evaluation?

This is an active research area, but results depend heavily on domain, disclosure wording and participant attitudes.

The chapter should avoid an unsupported universal:

> People value art less when told AI was used.

or:

> Audiences don't care if the result is good.

A more durable point is that **authenticity expectations vary by context**.

An AI-generated texture in a game may attract little attention. A synthetic singer presented as a real performance, a memoir passage represented as lived testimony, or documentary footage represented as real can affect trust much more strongly.

Platform policies such as YouTube's disclosure rule reflect this materiality distinction. [S31]

# 14. Practical workflow recommendations supported by the evidence

These are editorial/practical inferences, not universal experimental laws.

## For divergent ideation

- ideate independently first;
- create multiple problem framings;
- ask AI for structurally different directions;
- avoid choosing the first polished option;
- compare model suggestions with non-AI references;
- deliberately preserve unusual ideas.

Rationale: fixation/similarity evidence. [S17] [S18]

## For convergent refinement

AI may be more useful once the creator has criteria:

- audience;
- constraints;
- message;
- style rules;
- technical requirements.

Then the model can help generate/refine options against a human-defined target.

## For expertise preservation

Choose the operations you still want to practise. Do not outsource all early skill-building merely because the model can produce a passable result.

## For professional work

Measure whether AI actually saves time in the target stage. Do not assume "AI-assisted" means "faster." [S21]

# 15. Claims the later writer can and cannot make

## Strongly defensible

- Generative AI can improve immediate performance in some bounded creative tasks. [S17] [S21]
- AI suggestions can create fixation or reduce diversity in some experimental conditions. [S17] [S18] [S19]
- Effects differ by expertise and workflow stage. [S21]
- Creativity research and public debate often measure different things.
- There is not yet strong long-term evidence that AI inevitably kills or improves human creativity.

## Needs qualification

- "AI makes people more creative."
- "AI makes everyone sound/look the same."
- "AI destroys originality."
- "AI democratises creativity."
- "AI only helps beginners."
- "Experts get no value from AI."

## Avoid

- "Science proves AI kills creativity."
- "Science proves AI is a creative partner."
- "People who use AI are not creative."
- "If a result scores as creative, the model has human-like creativity."
