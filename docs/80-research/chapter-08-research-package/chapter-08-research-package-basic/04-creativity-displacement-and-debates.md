# Creativity, Displacement and Contrasting Viewpoints

# 1. What does “creativity” mean?

Researchers commonly operationalise creativity with constructs such as:

- novelty
- originality
- usefulness/appropriateness
- divergent thinking
- convergent thinking

Artists may use the term differently, including:
- expression
- intention
- emotional truth
- cultural meaning
- personal voice
- craft
- risk
- interpretation

A system can score well on a laboratory creativity test without establishing that it has:
- intentions
- lived experience
- values
- personal stakes
- self-defined goals

This distinction prevents an unnecessary metaphysical argument.

# 2. Evidence that AI can support creative performance

## Lee & Chung — Nature Human Behaviour (2024)

**Study:** Five experiments on everyday/innovation creative tasks.

**Finding:** ChatGPT use increased average creativity relative to no technology and conventional web search.

**Use in chapter:** supports the claim that AI can be a useful ideation partner.

**Caution:** experimental task performance is not equivalent to a professional creative career.

## Doshi & Hauser — Science Advances (2024)

**Participants:** 293 writers in an online microfiction task.

**Findings:**
- AI story ideas increased average novelty/usefulness ratings
- less-creative participants received larger gains
- AI-assisted stories became more similar to one another
- the study did not find evidence that AI pushed highly creative participants beyond their existing upper range

**Use:** exceptionally strong evidence for a balanced chapter.

### Particularly useful numerical results reported in the paper

For lower-DAT writers:
- one AI idea improved novelty by 6.3%
- access to five AI ideas improved novelty by 10.7%
- usefulness gains were 5.5% and 11.5% respectively
- in the five-idea condition, “well written” ratings increased by up to 26.6%
- enjoyment increased by up to 22.6%

These percentages apply to this specific experiment and should never be generalised into “AI makes people X% more creative.”

## Meincke, Nave & Terwiesch — Nature Human Behaviour (2025)

The authors reanalysed/extended the issue of AI-assisted brainstorming and emphasised reduced diversity across generated idea pools.

**Use:** reinforces the individual-benefit / collective-diversity trade-off.

# 3. Evidence/arguments that AI can narrow creativity

## Homogenisation

If many users:
- use the same models
- use similar prompts
- accept early outputs
- optimise for statistically likely answers

then collective work can converge.

This is now supported by multiple empirical studies.

## Fixation

A 2025 study in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* proposed a dual mechanism:
- AI inspiration can help
- AI suggestions can also produce creative fixation, especially in more complex tasks

The authors argue that unconstrained LLM collaboration can anchor users.

The long-term claim that this will “erode creativity” remains less established than the short-term fixation finding.

## 2026 work on metacognition

A 2026 *Nature Reviews Psychology* commentary argues that intellectual humility and metacognitive flexibility may help counter AI-driven homogenisation.

Useful practical translation:

> Treat the first AI answer as one candidate, not as the shape of the problem.

# 4. Does AI “understand” what it creates?

This is not necessary to resolve for Chapter 8.

Possible positions:

## Functionalist / output-centred
If a system consistently produces novel, useful or aesthetically strong work, calling that behaviour creative can be pragmatically meaningful.

## Human-centred / intentional
Creativity entails intention, experience, agency or meaning; therefore model output alone should not be equated with human creativity.

## Process-centred
Creativity can emerge from a human-machine system even if neither component alone explains the final result.

### Recommended editorial handling

Define what the chapter means by creative work rather than deciding whether machines “really” possess creativity.

# 5. Replacement debate

## Position A — AI will substitute creative labour

Evidence/arguments:
- cost of basic outputs drops sharply
- clients may accept “good enough”
- stock-like and commodity work is especially exposed
- automated generation can scale beyond human production
- creator groups report lost commissions
- synthetic content can compete for attention and platform revenue

## Position B — AI will mainly augment creators

Evidence/arguments:
- tools reduce routine work
- creators can iterate faster
- new media/forms become possible
- clients still require judgement, context and accountability
- high-value creative work depends on relationships, reputation, direction and trust
- historical creative technologies often changed jobs rather than eliminating creativity

## Position C — Both occur simultaneously

This is the most evidence-compatible framing.

Task-level substitution can coexist with:
- new creative roles
- higher output
- lower prices
- increased participation
- premium value for distinctive human work

No chapter-level claim should imply all creative professions move together.

# 6. Creator income evidence

## Society of Authors and partner organisations (2026)

A January 2026 report claimed substantial impacts from GenAI across more than 10,000 creator responses.

Examples publicised:
- 32% of illustrators reported lost/cancelled commissions due to GenAI
- 43% of literary translators and 37% of SoA illustrators reported earnings declines attributed to GenAI

The report also published broader high percentages about scraping and sustainability.

### Caveat

This is advocacy/stakeholder research, not a representative census of all creative workers. It is highly valuable as evidence of creator experience and concern, but sampling and question design should be examined before using headline numbers.

## CISAC / PMP Strategy (2024)

CISAC commissioned PMP Strategy to model economic effects in music and audiovisual industries.

Projected for 2028:
- 24% of music creator revenues at risk
- 21% of audiovisual creator revenues at risk
- €22bn cumulative creator revenue losses over five years in its scenario

### Caveat

These are forecasts based on model assumptions, commissioned by an organisation representing creator societies. Treat as scenario analysis, not observed fact.

# 7. A useful hard-data market example: Deezer

Deezer's own operational data show rapid growth in fully AI-generated music delivery:

- Jan 2025: approximately 10% at launch of its detection system
- Apr 2025: >18% / 20,000 tracks per day
- Sep 2025: >28% / 30,000 per day
- Jan 2026: ~39% / 60,000 per day
- Apr 2026: ~44% / 75,000 per day
- June peak, reported July 2026: >50% / around 90,000 per day

Yet Deezer reports AI-generated music at only 1–3% of listening.

## Interpretation

This supports:
- supply explosion
- platform moderation/discovery challenges
- fraud incentives

It does not prove:
- consumer preference for AI music
- creator replacement at the same percentage
- equivalent data across Spotify/Apple/YouTube

# 8. Training-data debate

## Creator/rightsholder framing

Core concerns:
- consent
- compensation
- transparency
- reproduction during training
- replacement effects
- style/identity appropriation
- bargaining power

## AI developer/open research framing

Core concerns:
- training may be transformative
- broad licensing can be infeasible for web-scale corpora
- mandatory per-work licensing could entrench incumbents
- text/data mining supports research and innovation
- models learn statistical patterns rather than simply serving copies

## Policy middle grounds

- opt-out / rights reservation
- collective licensing
- blanket licences
- dataset transparency
- provenance requirements
- model-specific licensing
- revenue-sharing
- sector agreements

Music is already providing examples of negotiated licensing.

# 9. Authenticity debate

## “AI makes work fake”

Too broad.

A heavily edited digital photograph remains creative work. A film uses CGI. A musician uses synthesisers. Creative practice has long involved tools that transform material.

## “AI is just another tool”

Also too broad.

A tool that generates substantial expressive content from a short instruction may take on a different role from a brush, camera aperture or word processor.

### Better question

> Which expressive decisions did the person make, which did the system determine, and was that process represented honestly to the audience?

# 10. Labour-value debate

AI exposes a tension between two ways of valuing creative work:

## Output value
If a customer only needs an acceptable image, they may not care whether it took one minute or ten hours.

## Process/human value
Audiences can value:
- artist identity
- performance
- craft
- scarcity
- history
- emotional connection
- evidence of effort
- social context

Both exist in creative markets.

A handmade chair and a factory chair can perform the same function while carrying different meanings and prices. That analogy may work better than treating creative work as purely information.

# 11. “Is AI stealing?” — possible balanced presentation

### Case for saying “yes” in ordinary moral language
Creators may reasonably see uncompensated use of their work to build commercial systems as taking value without consent, even if a court has not labelled it theft.

### Case against the word
“Theft” has a specific legal connotation and can obscure distinctions between:
- infringement
- fair use
- text/data mining
- imitation
- licensing
- substitution

### Chapter approach
Explain why people use the word, then switch to precise questions:
- Was protected work used?
- Was permission required?
- Was permission obtained?
- Was the creator compensated?
- Can the system reproduce protected expression?
- Does an output infringe?
- What does local law say?

That respects the emotional reality without turning rhetoric into a legal conclusion.

# 12. “Does AI kill creativity?” — evidence-based answer frame

The strongest available answer is conditional:

- It can expand access to creative production.
- It can improve individual performance on constrained creative tasks.
- It can help overcome blank-page friction.
- It can increase iteration.
- It can also anchor users on common ideas.
- It can reduce variety across a group.
- Long-term effects on individual skill development remain insufficiently established.
- The outcome depends heavily on workflow and incentives.

This directly supports the chapter's intended balanced tone.
