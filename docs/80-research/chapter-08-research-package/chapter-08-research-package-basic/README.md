## Authoritative scope

This package was prepared against the approved Chapter 8 plan. The chapter is intended to close Part 2 by examining AI in writing, art, music, video and design, then addressing three contested questions:

1. Is AI stealing?
2. Is AI replacing artists?
3. Does AI kill creativity?

The approved through-line is the **effort vs judgement** distinction introduced earlier in the book. This research expands the evidence available to the eventual chapter writer without changing that plan.

## Important cautions for the chapter-writing AI

- Do not turn this package into legal advice.
- Do not state that the legality of AI training is globally settled. It is not.
- Separate **training-data legality**, **copyrightability of an AI-assisted output**, **ownership under a tool's contract**, and **permission to use an output commercially**. These are different questions.
- Do not treat the US position as universal. Australia, the EU, the UK and the US are taking materially different approaches.
- Distinguish research findings from advocacy estimates and stakeholder surveys.
- Treat fast-moving product terms, lawsuits, regulatory rules and platform policies as time-sensitive.
- Avoid claims that AI “has creativity” or “cannot be creative” unless the term *creativity* is first defined. Much of the disagreement is definitional.
- Do not overgeneralise laboratory studies of short stories or brainstorming to professional novelists, artists, musicians or filmmakers.
- Preserve the chapter's non-hype position: AI can be genuinely useful in creative workflows while also creating real legal, economic, cultural and ethical problems.

## High-value findings in one page

### 1. AI assistance can improve individual creative output while narrowing collective variety

A 2024 *Science Advances* experiment with 293 writers found that access to generative-AI story ideas improved ratings of novelty and usefulness, with the largest gains among participants who scored lower on a prior creativity measure. At the same time, AI-assisted stories became more similar to each other.

A 2024 *Nature Human Behaviour* study likewise found gains in creative problem-solving with ChatGPT. A 2025 follow-up analysis stressed a trade-off: better average individual ideas can coexist with reduced diversity across the pool of ideas.

This is unusually useful for Chapter 8 because it avoids both “AI makes everyone more creative” and “AI destroys creativity.”

### 2. “Ownership” is not one question

For a beginner, at least four separate issues matter:

- **Did the AI company have the right to use training material?**
- **Is the generated output itself eligible for copyright?**
- **What rights does the tool contract give the user?**
- **Could the output still infringe someone else's rights or create another legal problem?**

These are frequently conflated.

### 3. Human contribution matters strongly in US copyrightability

The US Copyright Office's January 2025 report concluded that AI-assisted works can receive protection for sufficiently human-authored expressive elements, but mere prompting is not by itself enough to make a person the author of the generated expression. Selection, arrangement, modification and perceptible human-authored material can matter.

That is a US position, not a universal rule.

### 4. Training-data policy is diverging internationally

- **EU:** general-purpose AI providers have copyright-policy and training-content transparency obligations under the AI Act. The public training-content summary requirement applies to covered new models from 2 August 2025.
- **Australia:** the Attorney-General's Department stated in 2025 that the government was not considering a text-and-data-mining exception and was examining licensing, copyright status of AI-generated material and lower-cost enforcement.
- **UK:** the issue remains politically contested. A House of Lords committee report in March 2026 strongly defended creator rights and criticised the direction of earlier reform proposals.
- **US:** courts, legislation and Copyright Office analysis continue to develop the boundaries. The Copyright Office's Part 3 training report remains a major policy reference.

### 5. Licensing markets are emerging rather than the industry simply waiting for courts

Music provides a concrete example. Universal Music Group and Udio settled litigation in October 2025 and announced a licensed AI music platform trained on authorised music. Other licensing arrangements followed across the industry.

This matters because the real future may not be simply “training is lawful” versus “training is theft”; negotiated licensing systems are becoming a third path.

### 6. Creative labour effects are uneven

There is credible evidence of pressure on some low-cost, high-volume creative tasks, but economy-wide claims such as “AI has replaced artists” are too broad.

Stakeholder groups report lost commissions and falling earnings, but their surveys often represent affected members rather than a random sample of the entire creative workforce. Industry-commissioned forecasts such as the CISAC/PMP study are useful evidence of concern and plausible scenarios, but should not be written as observed future fact.

### 7. AI-generated content volume is already changing creative markets

Deezer reported in July 2026 that approximately 90,000 fully AI-generated tracks per day were being delivered to the service, exceeding 50% of new uploads at peak in June. However, fully AI-generated music represented only around 1–3% of listening, according to Deezer.

That contrast is editorially useful: **generation volume is not the same as audience demand.**

### 8. Provenance is becoming part of creative workflow

C2PA Content Credentials provide cryptographically signed provenance information about a file's history. Adobe automatically applies Content Credentials to fully Firefly-generated images, and OpenAI uses C2PA plus invisible watermarking on current image-generation systems.

Provenance is not proof that an image is “true,” and metadata can be removed or lost. It is better described as a verifiable record of declared origin/history.
