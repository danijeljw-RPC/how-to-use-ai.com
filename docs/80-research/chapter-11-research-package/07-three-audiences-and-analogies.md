# 07 — Three Audiences and the Author's Analogies

## Purpose

DJ's notes identify three audiences that encounter different AI hype: executives/managers, students, and seniors/older adults. The evidence supports tailoring examples to those audiences, but the research suggests **one common evaluation method with audience-specific examples** is stronger than three separate mini-chapters.

That preserves the chapter's general skill while letting different readers recognise their own version of hype.

---

# Part A — Corporate executives and managers

## 1. The hype they encounter

Common forms:

- “deploy AI now or be left behind”;
- “automate an entire department”;
- “agents will run end-to-end operations”;
- enterprise pilots presented as production transformation;
- vendor benchmark gains presented as customer ROI;
- a large model capability presented as if data/process/integration work disappears.

The evidence supports real productivity gains in some tasks **and** substantial organisational friction.

## 2. Enterprise adoption is high; enterprise-wide financial impact is not universal

Stanford AI Index 2026 reports widespread organisational AI use, but adoption is not the same as scaled value.  
https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy

McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey reported that more than 80% of respondents did not yet see a tangible enterprise-level EBIT effect from generative AI, while workflow redesign was among the factors most strongly associated with reported financial impact. **[Consulting survey; self-report/correlation, not causal proof]**  
https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-how-organizations-are-rewiring-to-capture-value

A later 2025 McKinsey survey found scaling remained uneven and only a minority of organisations reported substantial enterprise-level financial effect. **[Consulting survey; recheck]**  
https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025

### Editorial meaning

Avoid “AI pilots mostly fail” as a universal claim. Stronger:

> “Using AI somewhere is now common. Redesigning a workflow so that AI produces measurable organisation-wide value is much harder.”

## 3. Controlled workplace evidence: benefits are heterogeneous

### Customer support

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied 5,179 customer-support agents and found access to a generative-AI assistant increased productivity by about 14% on average, with much larger gains for newer/lower-skilled workers and smaller effects for experienced/high performers. **[NBER / later academic publication]**  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

**Use:** AI can genuinely augment work; impact differs by worker/task.

### P&G product-development teams

A field experiment involving 776 professionals found individuals using AI could reach performance comparable to some human teams on the studied tasks, and AI changed how expertise was distributed across tasks. **[NBER working paper]**  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w33641

**Use:** team structure and workflow can matter as much as the tool itself.

### Experienced open-source developers

METR's July 2025 randomised study followed 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 real tasks in repositories they knew. With early-2025 AI tools, participants took 19% longer while expecting AI to make them faster and believing afterward that it had. **[Independent RCT/preprint-style report]**  
https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/

**Critical limits:** small, highly experienced sample; familiar codebases; early-2025 tools; developers were constrained by study design. METR later noted growing selection/timing complications in follow-up work.  
https://metr.substack.com/p/2026-02-24-uplift-update

**Use:** perception of productivity can differ from measured productivity; do not generalise to all programming.

## 4. “Task automation, not job automation” — too absolute

The author's note is useful as a corrective to “replace entire departments,” but it should not become a factual rule.

A job is a bundle of tasks. AI can:

- automate individual tasks;
- reduce time on a task;
- change the skill mix;
- increase output expectations;
- change headcount needed for a workflow;
- create new tasks/review roles;
- in some cases eliminate or consolidate roles.

The safest Chapter 11 formulation is:

> “AI capability is usually measured at the level of tasks. Translating that into job or department replacement requires assumptions about all the other tasks, workflow, demand, accountability and organisational change.”

Detailed employment evidence belongs in Chapter 10.

## 5. Change management and workflow redesign

Historical and current evidence both support the idea that complementary organisational changes matter.

### Factory electrification — better analogy than horses to cars

Paul David's classic economic history argues that the productivity gains of electrification were delayed because factories initially replaced steam power with electric motors without redesigning plant layout and production. Larger gains came as organisations reconfigured around the technology. **[Economic history]**  
https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v80y1990i2p355-61.html

Modern empirical work continues to examine how electrification interacted with factory reorganisation and productivity.  
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0014498325000014

### Why the analogy works

- technology can be genuinely better without instant productivity gains;
- old workflows can suppress benefits;
- complementary skills/process redesign matter;
- “installing the technology” and “transforming the organisation” are separate events.

### Where it breaks

- software diffuses much faster than 20th-century industrial power systems;
- AI can change cognitive tasks directly rather than factory layout;
- some AI tools require much less capital reconfiguration;
- historical analogy cannot predict the size/timing of future AI gains.

## 6. Horses-to-cars analogy — why it backfires

US agricultural historical data show work horses and mules declined dramatically as tractors/motor vehicles/mechanisation spread. USDA historical material documents a fall from roughly 22 million work horses/mules near the early-20th-century peak to under 8 million by 1950.  
USDA agricultural census archive:  
https://www.nass.usda.gov/AgCensus/archive/census_parts/1950-agriculture-1950-a-graphic-summary/index.html

### Problem for the intended message

The analogy is often used in the **opposite** direction: horses were the productive input that machines displaced. An anxious reader can immediately respond, “Yes—exactly. What happened to the horses?”

The note's line that companies that bought cars but did not train drivers “went bankrupt” also appears rhetorical rather than a sourced historical generalisation and should not be used as fact.

### Recommendation

Replace with electrification/workflow redesign or use a deliberately limited analogy such as:

> “Buying an MRI does not make a hospital better at diagnosis without trained staff, workflow, maintenance and interpretation.”

That example is less historically sweeping and keeps humans as operators rather than horses as displaced inputs.

## 7. Executive/board Australian guidance

The Australian Institute of Company Directors' 2026 *Director's Guide to AI Governance* is useful for the professional audience because it frames AI as a governance, risk, strategy and capability issue rather than a procurement race. **[Professional governance body]**  
https://www.aicd.com.au/news-media/research-and-reports/a-directors-guide-to-ai-governance.html

ASIC REP 798 is useful regulatory evidence of adoption/governance gaps:  
https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/find-a-document/reports/rep-798-beware-the-gap-governance-arrangements-in-the-face-of-ai-innovation

### Reserve for Chapter 7 / Book 3

Do not turn Chapter 11 into an enterprise AI implementation chapter. Keep only enough to teach executives how to evaluate vendor/project claims.

# Part B — Students

## 8. The two hype extremes

Students can encounter both:

- “AI can do the assignment/exam/course for me”; and
- “my degree will be obsolete before I graduate.”

Chapter 11 should neither moralise nor promise safety. It can teach the distinction between **assisted task performance**, **learning**, and **future labour-market value**.

## 9. Exam performance: impressive, but narrower than job competence

LLMs have achieved high performance on professional/school examinations. The important research lesson is that headline percentiles and comparisons need inspection.

Eric Martínez's analysis of GPT-4 and the bar exam showed the famous “90th percentile” claim was highly sensitive to the comparator population and score conversion. **[Peer-reviewed analysis]**  
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10506-024-09396-9

### Beginner takeaway

Passing an exam can show knowledge/reasoning relevant to a job. It does not show the system can:

- interview a client;
- discover missing facts;
- carry legal responsibility;
- operate over months;
- negotiate;
- comply with professional duties;
- recognise when not to act.

“Passed the exam” is a real result. “Can do the profession” is a much larger inference.

## 10. Performance while assisted is not the same as learning

The PNAS high-school mathematics field experiment is exceptionally useful. Students with generic GPT help performed much better on supported practice but worse on later unassisted assessment than controls; a constrained tutoring system preserved much more learning. **[Peer-reviewed field experiment]**  
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122

### Chapter 11 teaching point

When a claim says “AI improved student performance,” ask:

- performance **while AI was present**, or learning retained afterward?
- answer quality, speed, confidence, grades, or conceptual understanding?
- generic chatbot or purpose-built tutor?

This is a clean demonstration of “what exactly was measured?”

## 11. Cognitive offloading

Humans routinely offload memory/calculation/navigation to tools. This is not automatically harmful; writing, calculators and maps are all forms of cognitive offloading. The concern is whether the offloaded process is one the learner still needs to internalise.

For students, a useful distinction is:

- **tool as scaffold:** supports practice, feedback, explanation and metacognition;
- **tool as substitute:** produces the finished cognitive work without the learner practising it.

The evidence varies by task and pedagogy. Avoid a universal claim that AI “makes students stupid.”

## 12. AI-writing detectors: false confidence is itself hype

A 2023 *Patterns* paper tested seven GPT detectors against essays written by non-native and native English writers. It reported high false-positive rates for the TOEFL/non-native sample, showing that detector confidence could disproportionately misclassify such writing. **[Peer-reviewed; models/detectors from an earlier generation]**  
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666389923001307

### Limitations

- detector technology changes rapidly;
- the sample/task is specific;
- results should not be turned into “all AI detectors are useless.”

### Chapter 11 use

A product that returns a numerical “AI probability” can look scientific even when validation is weak. This is a strong student-facing example of confidence theatre.

## 13. Australian education policy

### Schools

The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools was first agreed in 2023 and reviewed/updated, with a 2025 version available through the Australian Government Department of Education. **[Government framework]**  
https://www.education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools

### Higher education

TEQSA's generative-AI resources emphasise assessment reform, learning assurance, evaluative judgement, critical thinking and ethical use rather than a simple ban-or-embrace binary. **[Australian regulator]**  
https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/higher-education-good-practice-hub/gen-ai-knowledge-hub/gen-ai-teqsa-resources

### Editorial implication

“Knowing how to ask the right questions” is too narrow as the central future skill. Evidence and policy guidance point toward:

- domain knowledge;
- judgement;
- verification;
- problem formulation;
- critical thinking;
- ethical/professional reasoning;
- knowing when AI output is inadequate.

Prompt skill matters, but it is not the whole competence.

## 14. “Calculator for words/text” — attribution and usefulness

The phrase is commonly associated with Simon Willison, who described large language models as a “calculator for text” and later used related “calculator for words” language in explaining why generated text should not automatically be treated as factual storage/retrieval.

A contemporary discussion attributing the analogy to Willison:  
https://blog.chriswm.com/2023/04/02/a-calculator-for.html

### Where the analogy works

- a tool can amplify a human capability;
- user still needs to choose the problem and evaluate the result;
- interface hides complex internal computation.

### Where it breaks — important

Calculators are deterministic and exact for well-defined arithmetic; language models are probabilistic and can produce plausible falsehoods. If used, the break is the point:

> “It is like a calculator for language only until you expect calculator-level correctness.”

Do not present the phrase as an uncontested technical definition of an LLM.

## 15. “Bicycle for the mind” — Steve Jobs verification

The phrase is verifiably associated with Steve Jobs discussing personal computers. In an archival interview, Jobs explains an analogy based on human locomotion efficiency and describes the computer as a “bicycle of the mind,” meaning a tool that amplifies human ability. **[Archival primary audiovisual record]**  
https://americanarchive.org/catalog/cpb-aacip-15-qj77s7j327

### Where it works for AI

- augmentation;
- leverage;
- tool does not define destination;
- skill still matters.

### Where it breaks

- modern AI can generate/plan/act more autonomously than a bicycle;
- some uses substitute tasks rather than merely amplify them;
- the underlying locomotion story is a rhetorical analogy, not evidence about AI productivity.

### Recommendation

Use only if the chapter needs a memorable augmentation metaphor. Do not use it as proof that “AI will not replace work.”

# Part C — Seniors and older adults

## 16. Avoid treating older adults as one fearful group

Research shows age-related differences in adoption/confidence, but “seniors are terrified of AI” is itself a stereotype.

The Australian Adult Media Literacy survey (2024) surveyed 4,442 adults and found strong age gradients in confidence/use of generative AI. Older respondents were more likely to report not knowing what generative AI was and less likely to report regular use or interest in learning new technology. **[Australian survey]**  
Western Sydney University summary:  
https://www.westernsydney.edu.au/news-centre/stories/2024/new-survey-reveals-high-media-usage-but-low-confidence-in-ai-among-adult-australians  
Report: https://medialiteracy.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/AML2024_report_final-compressed.pdf

### Important caveat

Age correlates with digital experience/access but does not determine it. Some older adults are expert users; some younger adults have poor media literacy.

## 17. Scams and security concern are not irrational

Scamwatch explicitly warns that scammers use AI to create convincing text, images, video and voice content. **[Australian government consumer resource]**  
https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/stop-check-protect/help-to-spot-and-avoid-scams/how-scammers-use-technology-and-ai

### Chapter boundary

Detailed scam protection belongs in Chapter 9/13. Chapter 11's role is to avoid dismissing reasonable security concerns as “sci-fi fear.”

## 18. Anthropomorphism: why chatbots feel more alive than conventional software

Humans readily attribute mental states and social qualities to systems that use language, voices, names, faces, memory or socially responsive behaviour. AI product design can therefore encourage anthropomorphic interpretation even without explicitly claiming consciousness.

Recent scholarship examines anthropomorphism in conversational AI and its implications for trust and relationships. **[Academic research]**  
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-02108-6

### Careful wording

Do not claim that “companies deliberately trick users into thinking AI is conscious” without product-specific evidence. Safer:

> “Humanlike design cues make anthropomorphism easier, whether or not that is the designer's intent.”

## 19. Science-fiction imagery and media framing

It is plausible that robot/Terminator imagery shapes public mental models, but avoid a cliché that “the media always shows humanoid robots.” A 2025 content analysis of 818 German news images about AI found humans were more common than humanoid robots and documented a more varied visual vocabulary. **[Media-study evidence]**  
https://jcom.sissa.it/article/pubid/JCOM_2402_2025_A09/

### Editorial use

Instead of asserting a universal image problem, say that **science-fiction language and humanoid imagery are one source of public mental models**, alongside actual chatbot interaction and news coverage.

## 20. AI consciousness: update the author's “zero awareness” wording

The book can safely say there is **no established scientific evidence that today's mainstream AI systems are conscious**. It should be more cautious about asserting metaphysical impossibility.

Butlin et al. assessed AI systems using indicators derived from several theories of consciousness and concluded no current systems met their criteria for consciousness, while arguing there was no obvious technical barrier to constructing systems satisfying the proposed indicators in principle. **[Preprint technical assessment]**  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708

### Recommendation

For Chapter 11:

> “A chatbot sounding human is not evidence that it is conscious. Current systems have no established scientific evidence of consciousness, and the deeper question remains a research/philosophical debate.”

Leave the detailed topic in Chapter 4.

## 21. Microwave analogy — historical verification

Microwave ovens did face public uncertainty about radiation/safety and questions about how they would change cooking. Manufacturers also promoted ambitious visions of fast, modern cooking. Over time microwaves became ordinary kitchen tools rather than replacements for every cooking method.

Useful historical sources:

- IEEE Spectrum history: https://spectrum.ieee.org/a-history-of-the-microwave-oven
- Smithsonian food/kitchen history: https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/food/online/new-and-improved/help-home-cook/cook-today-tomorrows-way
- Historical scholarship on microwave safety/gender/household labour: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/enterprise-and-society/article/abs/other-kitchen-debate-gender-microwave-safety-and-household-labor-in-late-cold-war-america/057A2C2C284DF1B56CE5B9160C9B7CE9
- CSIRO microwave safety explainer: https://www.csiro.au/en/research/health-medical/nutrition/microwave-oven-safety

### Where it works

- new technology can look intimidating before becoming mundane;
- you do not need engineering knowledge to use a tool;
- a technology need not replace an older one to be useful.

### Where it breaks

A microwave:

- performs a narrow physical function;
- behaves comparatively predictably;
- does not generate fluent false information;
- is not normally connected to cloud accounts/data;
- does not make recommendations that appear knowledgeable;
- has much simpler social/systemic effects.

### Recommendation

Use only for **“unfamiliar technology can become ordinary”**, never “AI is just like a microwave.”

## 22. Better senior-facing framing

Instead of “AI is lifeless, just an appliance,” the evidence supports:

- humanlike conversation does not prove humanlike awareness;
- you can learn useful AI functions without understanding the mathematics;
- security/privacy concerns deserve normal digital caution;
- useful tasks and genuine limitations can coexist.

A better analogy may be **satnav**:

- ordinary people use it without knowing satellite navigation engineering;
- it can be extremely useful;
- it can still send you the wrong way;
- you retain responsibility for context and obvious hazards.

Break: satnav is much narrower and usually has a more explicit map/route objective.

# Part D — Cross-audience structure

## 23. Should the chapter be split into three audiences?

Research recommendation: **no**, not as the primary chapter structure.

Reasons:

- the approved plan's durable evaluation skill should remain the spine;
- many readers occupy multiple categories (manager + parent + student; older professional etc.);
- three separate sections risk repetition;
- it can unintentionally stereotype groups.

Better editorial pattern:

- teach one claim-evaluation principle;
- show three short examples: executive/vendor, student/exam, older consumer/humanlike chatbot;
- return to a common method.

## 24. Other audiences with distinctive hype patterns

### Small-business owners
Common hype: “AI side hustle,” “replace your staff,” “fully automate sales/marketing.”  
Best fit: companion website / Book 3, with consumer-protection boundary to Chapter 13.

### Parents
Common hype/fear: homework cheating, AI companions, “AI tutor replaces teacher.”  
Best fit: student example in Chapter 11; detailed guidance elsewhere.

### People with disabilities
Distinct issue: AI accessibility tools can provide genuine autonomy while unreliable captions/vision/voice systems can cause harm. This is important but risks becoming an entire accessibility subsection. Better for companion website unless already covered elsewhere in Book 1.
