# 1 and 2. AGI panic and doom claims

This section requires particular care, because the plan explicitly separates:

- **legitimate, ongoing research and policy debate about long-term AI safety**, which is a real, serious field with genuine expert disagreement; from
- **dramatic, attention-grabbing claims of imminent catastrophe** with little specific reasoning behind them.

Research both sides of that distinction thoroughly, so the later writer can draw the line accurately and fairly.

Research:

- what "AGI" (artificial general intelligence) means, the fact that there is no agreed definition, and how different organisations define it differently (including definitions tied to commercial contracts or economic value);
- what "superintelligence" means in the debate;
- the main arguments made by researchers who consider advanced AI a serious long-term or catastrophic risk;
- the main arguments made by researchers who consider those concerns overstated, premature, or a distraction from present harms;
- the debate between researchers focused on long-term or catastrophic risk and researchers focused on present harms (bias, labour, surveillance), and where these communities agree and disagree;
- public statements and open letters signed by researchers and industry leaders about AI risk, what they actually said, and who signed them;
- surveys of AI researchers about the likelihood and timing of advanced AI and of extreme outcomes, including methodology, response rates, and criticism;
- forecasting platforms and their track records;
- international scientific assessments of AI risk (for example, government-commissioned international AI safety reports);
- the history of confident AI predictions that did not come true (for example, predictions by early AI pioneers, and the "AI winters");
- the concern that doom narratives can themselves serve commercial or political interests (for example, making products seem more powerful, or supporting regulation that favours incumbents), and the counter-argument that many people raising concerns have no such interest;
- and how the media reports AI-risk stories.

Do not decide whether long-term AI risk is real or exaggerated.

Collect enough material for the later writer to explain:

> Serious people disagree about long-term AI risk, and some of them are very worried for reasons worth understanding.

and:

> A claim of imminent catastrophe with no specific reasoning is not the same thing as that serious debate.

Handle this area respectfully. Some readers will be genuinely anxious about it. Research should support a calm, honest treatment.

# Why hype persists

The plan says hype persists because attention, investment, and fear all reward exaggeration.

Research the evidence behind each mechanism:

- **Attention:** research on how negative, dramatic, or novel news spreads faster or further online; research on headline framing of AI stories; research on science-news exaggeration (for example, studies showing that exaggeration in press releases carries into news coverage).
- **Investment:** research on technology investment cycles, "bubbles," and the incentive to signal AI capability to investors; evidence on how mentioning AI affects valuations or funding.
- **Fear:** research on fear appeals, risk perception, and why fear-based messages attract attention.
- **Other incentives:** career and reputational incentives for researchers and commentators; competition between companies; political incentives; the role of benchmarks and leaderboards in competitive marketing.

Research frameworks such as the **Gartner Hype Cycle**, and research the **criticism** of that framework (for example, studies finding that many technologies do not follow it). Decide whether it is a useful teaching tool for beginners or whether it would mislead them.

Research related ideas that may help beginners, such as **Amara's Law** (people tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run), including its origin and whether there is evidence for it.

# A practical method for evaluating AI claims

The current draft proposes three questions:

1. Is this a demo, a product, or a research claim, and does the framing match?
2. Who benefits if I believe this claim?
3. What would change my mind, and is there any specific, checkable detail?

Research this method rather than simply accepting it.

Investigate existing, evidence-based approaches to evaluating information and claims, such as:

- **lateral reading** (checking what other sources say about a source, as professional fact-checkers do);
- the **SIFT** method;
- the **CRAAP** test, and criticism of it;
- **prebunking** and inoculation research;
- research on teaching people to evaluate scientific and health claims;
- structured critical-thinking frameworks used in journalism, law, science, or intelligence analysis;
- published guides to evaluating AI claims specifically, from researchers, journalists, or public institutions;
- and books or long-form work by AI researchers who specifically critique AI hype.

For each approach, research:

- what it asks the reader to do;
- evidence that it works;
- whether it is simple enough for a beginner;
- and how it could inform or strengthen the chapter's own method.

Research whether "who benefits?" risks encouraging reflexive cynicism (dismissing true claims because someone benefits from them), and how the method can avoid that.

Research whether "what would change my mind?" (falsifiability) is practical for ordinary readers, and how to phrase it accessibly.

Research whether the method needs a fourth or fifth question (for example, "Has anyone independent checked this?", "Is this about today or about the future?", "Compared with what?", "What's the evidence, and what kind of evidence is it?").

The method will also be used in Chapter 14 on forward-looking claims. Research whether it works for predictions as well as for present-day capability claims, and what adjustments might help.

Record these as research-backed editorial possibilities. Do not write the final method.

# Contrasting viewpoints

Chapter 11 concerns questions where serious people disagree, including the likelihood and timing of advanced AI, the seriousness of long-term risk, whether current AI investment is a bubble, and how transformative AI is.

Do not manufacture artificial "both sides" symmetry.

Instead:

- identify the main positions;
- explain the reasoning behind each;
- identify what evidence supports each;
- identify where evidence is weak;
- distinguish technical claims from economic claims and from philosophical claims;
- distinguish predictions from observed capabilities;
- distinguish company claims from independent evaluation;
- distinguish expert surveys from expert consensus;
- identify where two apparently conflicting claims can both be true.

Examples:

> AI capabilities have improved rapidly on many measurable tasks.

and:

> Many AI products are marketed as more capable and reliable than they are.

These are not necessarily contradictory.

Likewise:

> Serious researchers have raised concerns about long-term AI risk.

and:

> Many dramatic claims of imminent AI catastrophe have little specific reasoning behind them.

may both be true.

Likewise:

> AI investment may include speculative excess.

and:

> The underlying technology may still be widely useful.

have both been true of earlier technologies.

Capture this nuance.

# Research philosophy

Do not approach this task trying to prove that:

- AI is overhyped;
- AI is underhyped;
- AI doom is nonsense;
- AI doom is justified;
- AI companies are dishonest;
- AI companies are trustworthy;
- AI is a bubble;
- AI is not a bubble.

Approach these as questions to investigate.

The research package should contain enough evidence for the later writer to say:

> Here is what AI hype looks like, and why it keeps happening.

> Here is what serious people actually disagree about.

> Here is how to tell a demo from a product from a research result.

> Here is a simple method you can use on the next claim you see.

> Here is how to stay sceptical without becoming cynical.

That is more valuable than producing a predetermined narrative.
