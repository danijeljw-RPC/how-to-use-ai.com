# 14 — Common Misconception Matrix

This matrix directly addresses the claims listed in the commissioning brief. The purpose is not to create one-line “myth busted” slogans; each entry records what is incomplete, the strongest evidence direction, and the important exception.

| Claim | What is wrong or incomplete | Stronger explanation | Important exception / caution | Useful sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **“AGI is just around the corner.”** | “AGI” has no single agreed operational definition; timelines vary widely. | Ask what milestone counts as AGI, by what date, and on what evidence. Treat as forecast. | Short timelines are held by some credible experts; disagreement does not prove they are wrong. | Morris et al.; Grace et al.; International AI Safety Report |
| **“AGI is impossible.”** | No scientific theorem establishes impossibility; often reflects scepticism about current approaches. | Separate “current methods may not get there” from “machine general intelligence cannot exist.” | Philosophical/technical constraints remain legitimate subjects of debate. | Morris et al.; AI as Normal Technology (contrast) |
| **“Everyone in AI agrees AI is an existential risk.”** | Surveys/statements show substantial disagreement in probability, priority, mechanism and timeline. | Some prominent researchers assign serious weight to catastrophic risk; others do not. | Public signatories are not a random sample of all AI researchers. | Grace et al.; CAIS statement; IAI Safety Report |
| **“Nobody serious worries about long-term AI risk.”** | Demonstrably false; major researchers, governments and international assessments study it. | Long-term risk is a legitimate research/policy topic even though evidence and probabilities are uncertain. | Serious study does not validate every doom headline. | International AI Safety Report 2026 |
| **“AI companies only talk about risk to make products sound powerful.”** | Motive hypothesis cannot explain independent academic/government risk work and does not test the argument. | Commercial incentives can coexist with sincere concern and real evidence. Evaluate mechanism/source. | Strategic incentives may influence some communications; conflict of interest should increase scrutiny. | International report; SEC incentive examples; independent risk research |
| **“AI is conscious.”** | Fluent language and self-reference are not scientific evidence of consciousness. | There is no established evidence current mainstream systems are conscious. | Machine consciousness in principle remains debated; avoid “never possible.” | Butlin et al. |
| **“AI definitely cannot ever be conscious.”** | Stronger than current science can establish. | Current evidence does not establish consciousness; future possibilities depend on unresolved theories/architectures. | Do not let openness about future consciousness imply current systems are sentient. | Butlin et al. |
| **“AI will cure cancer/all disease.”** | Usually a forecast extrapolating from upstream research acceleration to clinical outcomes. | AI can improve discovery/prediction; cures require validation, trials, regulation, delivery and biology. | Specific AI-enabled therapies may eventually produce major clinical gains. | AlphaFold; Nature Methods; contemporary leader forecasts |
| **“AI will end poverty.”** | Poverty is not only an information/prediction problem. | AI can improve targeting, measurement and service delivery but institutions/resources/policy remain causal. | Targeting improvements can materially help people even without “solving poverty.” | Nature poverty feature; World Bank-linked studies |
| **“AI will solve climate change.”** | Compresses infrastructure, policy, economics and behaviour into a technical claim. | AI may improve forecasting, materials, grids and monitoring; outcome depends on deployment and net costs. | Individual applications can be highly valuable. | GraphCast; materials studies; Chapter 9 environmental evidence |
| **“AI is just autocomplete, so nothing it does matters.”** | Mechanistic shorthand does not determine practical capability; modern systems can use tools, multimodal inputs and complex learned representations. | Judge systems by validated capability/reliability, not dismissive labels. | Next-token prediction remains central to many LLMs; anthropomorphic over-interpretation should still be resisted. | Stanford technical performance; International report |
| **“AI is just a fad.”** | Large-scale adoption, infrastructure, scientific use and commercial deployment are observable. | Some products/companies/valuations may be faddish while underlying techniques/services persist. | Adoption does not guarantee today's products, business models or valuations endure. | Stanford AI Index; AlphaFold; Waymo |
| **“AI is a bubble, so the technology is useless.”** | Asset prices and technical utility are separate. | Speculative investment excess can coexist with durable infrastructure/usefulness. | A severe investment correction could still affect development/adoption. | Stanford AI Index; IMF 2026 |
| **“If a demo shows it, the product can do it.”** | Demo may be selected/edited/controlled and prove possibility, not normal reliability. | Ask about repeated success, live conditions, latency, availability, cost and operating envelope. | A well-designed transparent demo can be strong evidence of capability. | Gemini demo; product reviews |
| **“If AI passed an exam, it can do the job.”** | Exams capture a subset of knowledge/skills and may have comparator/contamination issues. | Treat exam performance as evidence of particular competence, not full professional practice. | Some jobs contain large task components that correlate strongly with tested knowledge. | Martínez bar analysis; benchmark literature |
| **“Benchmarks prove which AI is best.”** | “Best” depends on task, scoring, sample, contamination, latency, cost and preference aggregation. | Benchmark score answers a defined measurement question. Use several relevant measures/independent tests. | For narrowly defined tasks, one benchmark may be highly decision-relevant. | Stanford technical; contamination survey; Leaderboard Illusion |
| **“95% of AI projects fail.”** | Viral wording obscures source, denominator, sampling and “failure”/business-impact definition. | Treat the NANDA finding as preliminary enterprise evidence, not a universal law. | Enterprise scaling problems are still real and deserve attention. | Project NANDA report; McKinsey comparisons |
| **“Every AI claim is marketing.”** | Peer-reviewed studies, regulators, independent tests and public datasets exist. | Classify the source; company marketing is one evidence type among several. | Research/advocacy can also have incentives and weak methods. | This package broadly |
| **“If an expert said it, it must be true.”** | Expertise raises relevance, not infallibility; experts can forecast beyond evidence or specialty. | Inspect evidence, domain relevance, uncertainty and independent agreement. | Expert judgement can be the best available evidence under uncertainty. | International report; Grace survey |
| **“If a company benefits from a claim, it must be false.”** | Motive fallacy; true claims can be profitable. | Conflict of interest means verify more carefully, not invert the claim. | Regulators have documented real cases of misleading AI claims, so incentives are not irrelevant. | SEC AI washing; FTC cases |
| **“AI hype is unique to AI.”** | Emerging technologies repeatedly attract utopian, fearful and investment narratives. | AI has unusual speed/generality/anthropomorphic interfaces, but core incentive structures recur historically. | Historical analogy cannot determine AI's eventual trajectory. | electrification; hype-cycle criticism; technology history |

## Additional misconception worth adding — “Human review means it is fake AI”

**Why add it:** modern AI systems often rely on human labelling, evaluation, escalation or operations. Human involvement is compatible with genuine automation.

**Corrective:** ask which steps are automated, which are human, and whether marketing accurately describes the division.

**Example:** Amazon Just Walk Out reporting/company response.

## Additional misconception worth adding — “If an AI claim was wrong before, the same claim must still be wrong now”

**Why add it:** capability changes rapidly. A 2022 dismissal of autonomous driving, multimodal models or coding agents may not describe 2026 systems.

**Corrective:** every capability claim needs a **date and version**.

**Example:** public autonomous ride-hailing in multiple US cities in 2026.

## Additional misconception worth adding — “Peer reviewed means settled”

**Corrective:** peer review is a quality-control layer; replication, external validity and later evidence still matter.

## Additional misconception worth adding — “Preprint means worthless”

**Corrective:** AI research often appears first as preprints. Treat conclusions as more provisional, inspect method, and check later review/replication.
