# 04 — Commercial Hype, Demos, AI Washing, and Investor Marketing

## 1. Commercial claims require a different evidence discipline

A company is the best primary source for:

- what it announced;
- how it says a demo was produced;
- what its product terms/specifications say;
- what it disclosed to investors.

A company is **not independent evidence** that the advertised capability is reliable or valuable. For that, look for independent testing, customer outcomes, regulators, audits, or peer-reviewed evaluation.

The same applies in reverse: a critic's viral post is not independent proof that a product was fake. The underlying architecture, human involvement, and actual workflow matter.

## 2. Edited multimodal demo: capability was real, presentation changed the inference

In December 2023 Google published a highly polished Gemini demonstration that looked like a person and model were interacting fluidly in real time with video/voice. Google's accompanying developer explanation describes how the demonstration was actually created from still-image frames and text prompts, with outputs assembled for presentation. **[Company disclosure]**  
https://developers.googleblog.com/how-its-made-interacting-with-gemini-through-multimodal-prompting/

TechCrunch reported that the video had been edited to suggest a real-time interaction and quoted Google clarifying that the underlying prompts used still image frames and text. **[Independent technology press]**  
https://techcrunch.com/2023/12/07/googles-best-gemini-demo-was-faked/

### Generalisable lesson

A demo can be based on genuine model outputs and still give a misleading impression of:

- latency;
- continuity;
- input modality;
- success rate;
- normal user experience.

### Generic print rendering

> “A viral multimodal AI demo appeared to show a smooth live conversation with a camera feed. The company later explained that the demonstration had been assembled using still-image frames and text prompts. The capability existed, but the polished presentation did not show the ordinary live interaction a viewer might reasonably infer.”

### Naming defensibility

High: the company's own technical post documents how it was produced. Avoid saying the underlying capability was fabricated.

## 3. Launch-ad factual error: separate the error from the market story

A 2023 advertisement for Google's Bard chatbot included an inaccurate claim about the James Webb Space Telescope. News coverage linked the error, broader launch criticism, and a large same-day fall in Alphabet's market value. **[Contemporary reporting]**  
Australian coverage: https://www.abc.net.au/news/2023-02-09/google-parent-company-loses-100-billion-in-shares/101950476

### Caution

Do **not** write “one chatbot error wiped $100 billion from the company.” Share prices are multicausal and market moves reflect expectations, competitive positioning, the presentation event, and broader trading. The useful example is simpler:

> A factual error appeared in the company's own launch material, demonstrating that fluent AI output needed verification even in high-profile marketing.

This also links back to Chapters 3–4 without repeating them.

## 4. SEC “AI washing” cases: strong regulatory evidence

On 18 March 2024, the US SEC announced settled charges against investment advisers Delphia (USA) Inc. and Global Predictions Inc. for false and misleading statements about their purported use of AI. The firms agreed to civil penalties totalling $400,000 without admitting or denying the SEC's findings. **[Regulator; primary]**  
https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024-36

SEC Chair Gary Gensler separately used the phrase “AI washing” to describe potentially misleading AI-related claims to investors. **[Regulator speech]**  
https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/sec-chair-gary-gensler-ai-washing

### Generalisable lesson

“Uses AI” is itself a commercial/investment claim. Ask:

- What component actually uses AI?
- Is it deployed or planned?
- Is the description material to customers/investors?
- Is there evidence beyond marketing copy?

### Generic print rendering

> “A financial regulator has already penalised investment firms for overstating how they used AI. The problem was not that AI was dangerous; it was that an impressive technology label was used in statements regulators found misleading.”

### Naming defensibility

Very high if exact settlement language is followed.

## 5. FTC Operation AI Comply: consumer hype can be conventional deception with an AI label

The US FTC announced “Operation AI Comply” in September 2024, bringing actions involving deceptive AI claims and schemes. **[Regulator; primary]**  
https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes

One action involved DoNotPay's marketing of an “AI lawyer.” The FTC later finalised an order requiring the company to stop making certain unsupported claims and pay $193,000. **[Regulator; primary]**  
https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/donotpay

### Generalisable lesson

AI does not create a new exception to old questions:

- What exactly does the product do?
- Has the seller tested the claim it is making?
- Does a professional-sounding label imply capability that has not been substantiated?

### Australian relevance

Australia's consumer-law framework similarly applies ordinary misleading/deceptive-conduct principles to AI goods/services. See Treasury's 2025 review:  
https://treasury.gov.au/publication/p2025-702329

## 6. Australia: ASIC's AI governance evidence

ASIC Report 798, *Beware the gap: Governance arrangements in the face of AI innovation* (29 October 2024), examined AI adoption/governance across a sample of Australian financial-services and credit licensees. ASIC identified rapid adoption alongside governance weaknesses, including gaps in risk management and disclosure. **[Australian regulator; primary]**  
https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/find-a-document/reports/rep-798-beware-the-gap-governance-arrangements-in-the-face-of-ai-innovation

### Why it fits Chapter 11

It avoids a cartoon of “corporate AI = fake.” Organisations can be deploying real AI while their governance, measurement and communication lag behind.

### Boundary

Detailed governance belongs more naturally in Chapter 7 / Book 3. Chapter 11 should use this only to demonstrate the gap between adoption claims and operational controls.

## 7. Human-in-the-loop systems: Amazon Just Walk Out as a calibration case

Reporting in 2024 highlighted large numbers of remote human reviewers involved in Amazon's Just Walk Out cashierless shopping technology. Some headlines/social posts compressed this into “the AI was actually humans.” Amazon disputed that framing and said humans labelled/reviewed data rather than manually generating each receipt in real time. Reuters reported both the human-review role and Amazon's characterisation. **[Independent reporting + company position]**  
https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/amazon-push-cashierless-shopping-tech-into-more-third-party-stores-while-backing-2024-04-17/

Amazon's current description says its systems automatically generate receipts, with human annotators used to improve models rather than watch customers live to create receipts. **[Company source]**  
https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-just-walk-out-dash-cart-grocery-shopping-checkout-stores

### Why this is unusually useful

It teaches two opposite errors:

- **Hype error:** calling a system fully autonomous while minimising important human operations can mislead.
- **Anti-hype error:** discovering human review does not prove the AI component is fictitious.

Many practical AI systems are socio-technical systems with humans supervising, correcting or labelling.

### Generic print rendering

> “A cashierless retail system was widely mocked online after reports revealed extensive human review. The company disputed the claim that people were secretly generating every transaction, describing the workers as model reviewers and data annotators. The useful question is not ‘AI or humans?’ but which steps are automated, which require people, and how much human intervention the claim ‘automated’ reasonably allows.”

## 8. Consumer AI hardware: launch promise vs ordinary product

### Humane AI Pin

WIRED's April 2024 review scored Humane's AI Pin poorly and documented latency, unreliability, missing/limited features, battery/heat issues and a product experience substantially weaker than the launch vision. **[Independent review]**  
https://www.wired.com/review/humane-ai-pin/

Humane later announced that consumer Ai Pin services would shut down on 28 February 2025. **[Company support notice / primary operational status]**  
https://support.humane.com/hc/en-us/articles/34243204841997-Ai-Pin-Consumers-FAQ

### Rabbit R1

WIRED's May 2024 review similarly found the Rabbit R1 unreliable and much narrower than its launch promise at that time. **[Independent review]**  
https://www.wired.com/review/rabbit-r1/

### Generalisable lesson

A product can be physically real and technically interesting while still being:

- incomplete at launch;
- unreliable;
- dependent on cloud services;
- missing promised integrations;
- weaker than a compelling demo/story.

### Generic print rendering

> “Several heavily promoted AI-first gadgets shipped as real products but independent reviewers found that early versions were unreliable, slow, or much narrower than the launch story suggested. Shipping hardware is not the same as delivering a dependable everyday product.”

## 9. Builder.ai: strong collapse case, but also a warning about viral counterclaims

Builder.ai raised substantial funding and marketed an AI-assisted/no-code approach to software development. In May 2025 the company entered insolvency proceedings. Subsequent reporting described major revenue discrepancies and investigations. **[Independent reporting]**  
TechCrunch: https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/20/once-worth-over-1b-microsoft-backed-builder-ai-is-running-out-of-money/  
Bloomberg (revenue reporting): https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-05-22/startup-builder-ai-overestimated-sales-by-300-to-key-creditors

Builder.ai's own historical materials openly referred to a network of human developers, which matters when evaluating later viral claims that the service had secretly been “700 engineers pretending to be AI.” **[Company archive/current historical page]**  
https://www.builder.ai/natasha

The Pragmatic Engineer investigated the “humans pretending to be AI” story and challenged/retracted the strongest viral version after interviews. **[Independent technical journalism]**  
https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pulse-137

Rest of World reporting documented heavy reliance on outsourced human software work, but that is more nuanced than “the AI never existed.” **[Investigative journalism]**  
https://restofworld.org/2025/builderai-ai-apps-downfall/

### Chapter value

This may be the best **anti-hype can itself be hype** example after the 95% statistic.

### Generic print rendering

> “After a heavily funded AI software company collapsed, a viral explanation claimed its AI had simply been hundreds of human engineers in disguise. Investigative reporting supported the importance of outsourced human labour but challenged the neat viral version. The collapse was real; the most shareable explanation was still too simple.”

### Naming defensibility

Moderate-to-high for insolvency and documented company history; cautious for allegations/investigations. Avoid criminal/fraud conclusions unless and until official findings support them.

## 10. The “95% of AI pilots fail” statistic

### Origin

The 2025 report *The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025*, associated with MIT Project NANDA, was marked **Preliminary Findings** and described a multi-method design including a review of 300+ public AI initiatives, 52 structured organisational interviews and 153 senior-leader survey responses. A publicly accessible mirror of the report preserves those details. **[Preliminary report; mirror because official hosting has been unstable]**  
https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf

A public summary likewise records the report title, authors and methodology.  
https://www.aigl.blog/state-of-ai-in-business-2025/

### How the claim spread

Media headlines converted the report into formulations such as “95% of generative AI pilots at companies are failing.” The problem is not that the report contained no 95/5 split; it is that “failure” became a generic label detached from the report's definitions, sample and measures of business/P&L impact and scaling. 

### Why this is a gold-standard Chapter 11 worked example

It lets the reader use every part of the evaluation method:

1. **Claim type:** research/report claim, then headline.
2. **Original source:** preliminary report, not the headline.
3. **Denominator:** what population of initiatives/organisations was actually studied?
4. **Outcome:** “measurable P&L impact,” “scaled,” “pilot,” and “failed” are not interchangeable.
5. **Method:** public initiatives + interviews + surveys, not a census of all enterprise AI projects.
6. **Independent check:** compare other organisational surveys and controlled studies.
7. **Date:** enterprise AI adoption was changing quickly.

### Generic print rendering

> “A preliminary enterprise-AI report was summarised online as proving that ‘95% of AI projects fail.’ The underlying work used several samples and business-impact measures, and the viral slogan erased the distinctions. The lesson is not that the report was worthless; it is that a memorable percentage can become a different claim as it travels.”

### Recheck before publication

Yes. Find the canonical report host if it becomes stable and re-evaluate later peer review/author clarifications.

## 11. Tesla 2016 self-driving promotional video — potentially useful, but caveated

Reuters reported in 2024 on a US Justice Department investigation and earlier testimony from a Tesla engineer that a 2016 video intended to demonstrate self-driving capability had been staged/engineered to portray a capability beyond ordinary operation at the time. **[Investigative/legal reporting; not final adjudication of fraud]**  
https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/tesla-autopilot-probe-us-prosecutors-focus-securities-wire-fraud-2024-05-08/

### Editorial recommendation

Prefer this for the companion website, not the main print example, unless a final primary legal finding or unambiguous company admission is available at publication. The Google Gemini demo is cleaner because the company's own technical explanation is sufficient.

## 12. Investor marketing and the “AI bubble” question

The chapter should **not** decide whether AI investment is a bubble.

Facts that can coexist:

- investment and infrastructure spending are historically large;
- AI adoption and revenue are real;
- some valuations may embed aggressive future expectations;
- some organisations report weak enterprise-wide financial impact;
- financing structures and circular demand can increase systemic risk;
- useful underlying technology can survive a speculative investment correction.

Stanford AI Index 2026 provides current investment/adoption data:  
https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy

The IMF's 2026 annual-report discussion flags both deployment effects and the possibility that business payoff may disappoint amid large financing commitments. **[International institution]**  
https://www.imf.org/annual-report/2026/in-focus/ai-deployment-and-disruption/

### Historical teaching point

“Bubble” is a statement about asset prices/expectations, not a synonym for “technology is useless.” The dot-com era is the obvious historical example: many valuations and firms collapsed while internet infrastructure and services continued to reshape the economy.

## 13. What “AI washing” should mean in this chapter

Use it narrowly:

- claiming AI is used when it is not used as represented;
- overstating the extent/materiality of AI use;
- using an AI label to imply unsupported capability/performance;
- presenting planned capability as present capability.

Do not use “AI washing” merely for software that uses simple ML, rules, or statistics. “AI” has broad technical and commercial definitions; the issue is misleading representation, not whether the author personally finds the implementation sophisticated enough.

## 14. Commercial-hype recognition questions for readers

Potential research-backed prompts for the later writer:

- Can I use this feature today, or is it announced/previewed?
- Is the demo continuous and live, or edited/selected?
- What percentage of attempts work?
- What human review is required?
- What is the cost/latency at ordinary scale?
- Is the company comparing against the strongest reasonable alternative?
- Has anyone independent reproduced/tested it?
- What did the regulator/order actually find, rather than what a headline says?
