# 05 — Why Hype Persists: Attention, Investment, Fear, and Competition

## Central finding

The plan's “attention, investment, and fear reward exaggeration” framing is broadly defensible, but each mechanism should be stated at the level the evidence supports.

The best evidence is strongest for **attention incentives** and **commercial signalling**. “Fear” is well supported as a persuasive mechanism, but the chapter should avoid a simplistic claim that every frightening AI story is deliberately engineered for clicks.

## 1. Attention: negative and emotionally charged headlines attract clicks

A large Nature Human Behaviour study tested 22,743 headline variants from Upworthy, covering more than 5.7 million clicks and hundreds of millions of impressions. Adding negative words was associated with increased click-through rates; positive words decreased them on average. **[Peer-reviewed large-scale experiment]**  
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4

### What this supports

- publishers/platforms have a measurable attention incentive to use negative framing;
- tiny wording changes can change click behaviour;
- fear/negative framing can be rewarded even when underlying content is unchanged.

### What it does not establish

- all AI journalism is fear-driven;
- negative AI news is false;
- the same effect size applies identically to every platform/audience/topic.

## 2. Science-news exaggeration can begin before the newsroom

Sumner et al. analysed 462 university press releases and 668 associated news stories about health science. Exaggerated advice, causal claims, and extrapolation from animal studies in press releases were strongly associated with exaggeration in news. The study found little evidence that more exaggerated press releases produced more news coverage. **[Peer-reviewed, BMJ 2014]**  
https://www.bmj.com/content/349/bmj.g7015

### Chapter 11 value

This stops the chapter blaming “the media” as a single villain. Exaggeration can enter at several stages:

research result → institutional press release → news headline → social-media summary → influencer commentary.

The chapter's method should encourage tracing a claim back toward the original evidence.

## 3. Fear appeals: persuasive, especially when paired with efficacy

A meta-analysis covering 127 papers, 248 independent samples and more than 27,000 participants found fear appeals had a positive average effect on attitudes/intentions/behaviours, particularly where messages included effective recommended actions. **[Peer-reviewed meta-analysis]**  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789790/

### Careful interpretation

This supports the general proposition that fear can be persuasive. It does not prove that a specific AI-risk message was selected because a communicator consciously wanted engagement.

### Useful wording

> “Fear gets attention and can change behaviour, which creates an incentive for dramatic framing. That does not make the underlying risk false.”

## 4. Investment: novelty and growth narratives have financial value

AI investment is exceptionally large. Stanford AI Index 2026 reports major increases in private/corporate AI investment and widespread organisational adoption. **[Research synthesis; current]**  
https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy

Financial regulators have directly documented cases where firms overstated AI use. That is stronger evidence of the incentive than trying to infer motives from valuation alone.  
SEC AI-washing cases: https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024-36

### What can be said safely

- calling a company/product “AI-powered” can influence how investors/customers perceive growth and differentiation;
- firms have incentives to emphasise impressive capabilities;
- regulators have acted where AI-related statements were allegedly false/misleading;
- large investment does not prove the technology is overvalued, and a high valuation does not prove a product claim is false.

## 5. Competition produces benchmark and launch incentives

When labs/products are compared using public leaderboards and benchmark scores, there are incentives to:

- optimise for widely watched tests;
- announce “state of the art” improvements quickly;
- choose favourable evaluation subsets;
- present a single aggregate score as broad superiority;
- release polished benchmark/demo material before independent use is widespread.

This does not imply manipulation. Competitive measurement always creates **Goodhart-like** pressure: once a measure becomes a target, it may become less informative about the broader goal.

See `06-evaluation-methods-and-benchmarks.md`.

## 6. Career and reputational incentives cut in multiple directions

Researchers, journalists, consultants, policy advocates, executives and critics can all gain attention from strong claims.

Potential incentives include:

- first-to-report novelty;
- publication/citation incentives;
- funding for a risk or solution area;
- consulting demand;
- audience growth;
- ideological/policy influence;
- company valuation/recruitment;
- reputation for contrarian scepticism.

### Crucial editorial rule

Incentives should change the amount of verification, **not determine truth**.

A claim does not become false because the speaker benefits if it is believed.

## 7. Why anti-hype also spreads

“Most AI projects fail,” “AI is just autocomplete,” “AI is a bubble,” and “X was secretly humans” can be as clickable as promotional hype because they offer:

- contrarian certainty;
- relief from anxiety/FOMO;
- a simple debunking story;
- status for seeing through the hype;
- conflict with powerful companies.

The 95% enterprise-AI statistic and Builder.ai viral story are useful evidence that **skepticism itself can be packaged for attention**.

This is essential to the chapter's credibility.

## 8. Gartner Hype Cycle — useful picture, weak law

Gartner's Hype Cycle is a well-known conceptual model describing a technology moving through an innovation trigger, inflated expectations, disillusionment, enlightenment and productivity.

### Why beginners like it

- intuitive;
- communicates that expectations and useful deployment can move differently;
- normalises a period where early excitement outruns practical results.

### Why it should not be taught as a predictive law

Academic analyses have found many technologies do not neatly follow the stylised curve, and category selection/timing can be subjective. One empirical critique examined multiple technologies and reported incongruences with a universal repeatable pattern. **[Academic critique]**  
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0040162516300270

More recent modelling also shows hype-shaped dynamics can arise from social processes without being a direct measurement of underlying technical progress. **[2026 academic study]**  
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0040162526002830

### Recommendation

If used at all, label it explicitly as a **communications/expectations heuristic**, not a forecasting tool. It is optional; Chapter 11 does not need it.

## 9. Amara's Law — memorable heuristic, provenance/evidence weaker than its name suggests

The saying commonly attributed to futurist Roy Amara is that people tend to overestimate technology effects in the short run and underestimate them in the long run.

### Research finding

The line is widely quoted but does not appear to originate as a rigorously tested “law” in a single canonical paper. Provenance work points to circulating formulations/interviews rather than a foundational empirical study.  
Useful provenance discussion: https://www.critical.design/post/amaras-law

### Recommendation

- If used, call it **a heuristic often attributed to Roy Amara**, not a scientific law.
- Do not let it become a prediction that every overhyped technology eventually exceeds expectations.
- It may be more suitable for the companion website than the main chapter.

## 10. Technology hype can have useful functions

Hype is not purely harmful. Optimism and ambitious narratives can:

- attract capital to expensive research/infrastructure;
- recruit skilled people;
- increase experimentation;
- create standards/policy attention;
- motivate early adopters;
- make previously obscure research legible to the public.

### Costs

- capital misallocation;
- rushed procurement;
- weak pilots selected for FOMO rather than need;
- unrealistic expectations followed by abandonment;
- public distrust when promises fail;
- over-reliance by users;
- distorted policy priorities;
- opportunity cost for less fashionable approaches.

### Balanced teaching point

> “Hype can finance real progress and still mislead people about what works today.”

## 11. Why AI is particularly hype-prone

Research suggests several structural reasons:

1. **General-purpose presentation:** the same chatbot interface can appear to do hundreds of tasks.
2. **Humanlike language:** fluent output feels more intelligent than a conventional software error code.
3. **Low demo cost:** a striking demonstration can be produced before reliability is measured.
4. **Rapid version turnover:** capabilities genuinely change before public understanding stabilises.
5. **Benchmark abundance:** easy to publish headline numbers that outsiders struggle to interpret.
6. **Future optionality:** enormous potential markets reward strong forecasts.
7. **Cultural science-fiction vocabulary:** “intelligence,” “agents,” “learning,” “memory” and “hallucination” carry human meanings.
8. **Invisible internals:** users cannot easily inspect why a model produced a result.

## 12. A simple “hype pipeline” editorial possibility

Not a factual law; useful diagram concept:

**Research result / prototype**  
→ institutional/company interpretation  
→ press release / launch demo  
→ news headline  
→ social post / influencer clip  
→ audience memory (“AI can do X”)

At each step ask: **what qualification disappeared?**

This would visually support lateral reading without adding a complex technical diagram.

## 13. Claims to avoid

Avoid:
- “fear always gets more clicks”;
- “media exaggerate because fear sells” as a universal motive claim;
- “venture capital requires lying”;
- “researchers hype AI for grants” as a blanket accusation;
- “Gartner proves every technology goes through a hype cycle”;
- “Amara's Law proves AI is underestimated long-term.”

Prefer evidence-specific, probabilistic language.
