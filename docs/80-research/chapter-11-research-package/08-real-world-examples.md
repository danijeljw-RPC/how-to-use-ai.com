# 08 — Strong Real-World Examples for Chapter 11

The commissioning brief asks for approximately 15–20 genuinely strong examples. This file contains **20**, selected for teaching value rather than embarrassment value.

Each example records the named evidence here while offering a generic rendering for the printed book.

## Example 1 — Gemini multimodal “hands-on” demo

**Hype category:** misleading/edited demo  
**Date:** December 2023  
**What happened:** A polished video appeared to show fluid live multimodal interaction. Google's technical follow-up described the underlying process as selected still-image frames and text prompts; outputs were edited/assembled for presentation.  
**Strongest original source:** Google Developers, “How it's Made: Interacting with Gemini through multimodal prompting.”  
https://developers.googleblog.com/how-its-made-interacting-with-gemini-through-multimodal-prompting/  
**Independent source:** TechCrunch.  
https://techcrunch.com/2023/12/07/googles-best-gemini-demo-was-faked/

**What it demonstrates:** A real capability can be presented in a way that implies lower latency, different inputs or more seamless reliability than was actually demonstrated.  
**Plan fit:** fake/misleading demos; demo vs product.  
**Best audience:** all, especially consumers/students.  
**Generic description:** “A viral multimodal demo looked like live camera-and-voice interaction, but the maker's own technical explanation showed it had been assembled from still images and text prompts.”  
**Naming defensible?** Yes; company disclosure. Avoid “fabricated capability.”  
**Print:** excellent.  
**Website:** excellent; can compare video framing with technical explanation.  
**Reader can recreate evaluation?** Yes—watch demo, then read “how it was made.”  
**Caveats:** underlying outputs/capability were not necessarily fake.  
**Date risk:** low as historical case.

## Example 2 — Bard/JWST launch-ad factual error

**Hype category:** launch marketing / confident error  
**Date:** February 2023  
**What happened:** A Bard advertisement contained an inaccurate statement about the James Webb Space Telescope. Alphabet shares fell substantially around the launch event and negative coverage.  
**Source:** ABC Australia contemporary reporting.  
https://www.abc.net.au/news/2023-02-09/google-parent-company-loses-100-billion-in-shares/101950476

**What it demonstrates:** Marketing teams themselves need to verify generated claims; fluent output is not safe because it appears in an official launch.  
**Plan fit:** startup/product hype; verification.  
**Audience:** all.  
**Generic description:** “A major chatbot launch advertisement included a confident factual error that experts quickly spotted.”  
**Naming defensible?** Yes for the factual error.  
**Print:** good, but do not say the error alone caused the market loss.  
**Website:** good.  
**Caveat:** stock-price movement was multicausal.  
**Date risk:** low.

**Naming defensible?** Very high if exact order language is respected.

**Caveat:** settlements not admissions of findings.  
**Date risk:** low.

## Example 6 — Amazon Just Walk Out and human reviewers

**Hype category:** autonomy claim / anti-hype oversimplification  
**Date:** major reporting 2024; current explanation 2026  
**What happened:** Reporting described extensive human review in cashierless retail. Online retellings often became “it was humans all along.” Amazon said its ML systems generated receipts and humans labelled/reviewed data rather than manually running each transaction.  
**Sources:** Reuters  
https://www.reuters.com/business/retail-consumer/amazon-push-cashierless-shopping-tech-into-more-third-party-stores-while-backing-2024-04-17/  
Amazon explanation  
https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-just-walk-out-dash-cart-grocery-shopping-checkout-stores

**Demonstrates:** human-in-the-loop does not equal fake AI; “automated” still needs scope.  
**Plan fit:** startup/product hype; calibration.  
**Audience:** all/executives.  
**Generic description:** “A supposedly seamless automated retail system turned out to involve substantial human review, but the viral claim that people secretly performed every checkout was also disputed.”  
**Naming defensible?** Yes with both sides.  
**Print:** excellent calibration example.  
**Website:** excellent.  
**Caveat:** distinguish model annotation/review from live transaction generation.  
**Date risk:** medium; architecture can change.

## Example 9 — Builder.ai collapse and the viral “humans pretending to be AI” story

**Hype category:** startup hype + anti-hype hype  
**Date:** collapse May 2025; reporting 2025  
**What happened:** Builder.ai entered insolvency after raising substantial capital. Reporting described revenue problems and heavy reliance on outsourced human development. A viral narrative that hundreds of engineers had simply pretended to be an AI system was challenged by later technical journalism; the company's own materials had acknowledged human developers.  
**Sources:**  
TechCrunch: https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/20/once-worth-over-1b-microsoft-backed-builder-ai-is-running-out-of-money/  
Rest of World: https://restofworld.org/2025/builderai-ai-apps-downfall/  
Pragmatic Engineer correction/investigation: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pulse-137  
Company historical page: https://www.builder.ai/natasha

**Demonstrates:** a real corporate failure can attract a false neat explanation.  
**Plan fit:** startup hype; calibration.  
**Audience:** executives, technical-curious readers.  
**Generic description:** “After a highly funded AI company collapsed, a viral story reduced the failure to ‘the AI was secretly hundreds of humans.’ Deeper reporting supported extensive human labour but challenged the clean viral claim.”  
**Naming defensible?** Moderate/high for insolvency/human-work facts; cautious for allegations.  
**Print:** very strong if carefully generic.  
**Website:** strong named case.  
**Legal/ethical caveat:** do not call fraud without official finding.  
**Date risk:** recheck investigations.

## Example 10 — “95% of AI pilots fail”

**Hype category:** anti-hype that became hype / research misreading  
**Date:** July–August 2025  
**What happened:** A preliminary Project NANDA report used mixed methods to study enterprise generative-AI adoption and business impact. Headlines converted a nuanced 95/5 result into the universal slogan “95% of AI projects fail.”  
**Report mirror:** https://cloudelligent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf  
**Public report summary:** https://www.aigl.blog/state-of-ai-in-business-2025/

**Demonstrates:** denominator, outcome definition and study design can disappear in a headline.  
**Plan fit:** evaluation method; executives; anti-hype.  
**Audience:** all, especially executives.  
**Generic description:** “A preliminary enterprise study with multiple samples and business-impact measures turned into a viral claim that ‘95% of AI projects fail.’”  
**Naming defensible?** Yes for report; do not attribute bad faith to journalists.  
**Print:** arguably the best worked example in the chapter.  
**Website:** excellent interactive/headline-v-source case.  
**Reader recreation:** yes.  
**Caveat:** canonical report host should be rechecked; terminology/denominators need exact quotation from source before publication.  
**Date risk:** medium/high.

## Example 11 — GPT-4 “90th percentile” bar-exam claim revisited

**Hype category:** benchmark misinterpretation  
**Date:** GPT-4 claim 2023; peer-reviewed reevaluation 2024/25  
**What happened:** GPT-4's bar-exam performance was widely described as around the 90th percentile. Eric Martínez showed percentile placement changed materially under different reasonable comparator assumptions.  
**Source:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10506-024-09396-9

**Demonstrates:** “beats X% of humans” depends on which humans and how scores are mapped.  
**Plan fit:** benchmark; research claim.  
**Audience:** students/professionals.  
**Generic description:** “A famous claim that an AI scored in the top tenth of bar-exam takers weakened when a researcher changed the comparison group to better match the population being described.”  
**Naming defensible?** High.  
**Print:** excellent.  
**Website:** excellent side-by-side.  
**Caveat:** reevaluation does not mean GPT-4 performed poorly; it challenges percentile framing.  
**Date risk:** low.

## Example 12 — METR developers slower while believing AI made them faster

**Hype category:** perception vs independent measurement  
**Date:** July 2025  
**What happened:** In a randomised study of experienced developers working on familiar open-source codebases, early-2025 AI tools made task completion 19% slower on average, although developers expected and later believed AI had made them faster.  
**Primary study report:** https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/  
**Follow-up caveat:** https://metr.substack.com/p/2026-02-24-uplift-update

**Demonstrates:** subjective productivity and measured productivity can diverge.  
**Plan fit:** executives; research claim; measurement.  
**Audience:** professionals/students.  
**Generic description:** “Experienced developers in a controlled study believed an AI assistant saved time, while timing data showed the opposite for those particular tasks and tools.”  
**Naming defensible?** Yes.  
**Print:** strong evidence box.  
**Website:** excellent.  
**Caveat:** narrow sample/task/tool era; do not generalise to all developers.  
**Date risk:** high because tools improve quickly.

**Plan fit:** students; research claim.  
**Audience:** students/parents/teachers.  
**Generic description:** “Students using a generic AI helper solved more practice problems, but when the helper disappeared they performed worse than students who had practised without it.”  
**Naming defensible?** Yes.  
**Print:** excellent.  
**Website:** quiz possibility.  
**Caveat:** task/population-specific; structured tutoring produced different result.  
**Date risk:** low/medium.

## Best three for the printed chapter

1. **95% of AI projects fail** — shows how a claim mutates between source and headline.
2. **Gemini demo** — shows demo presentation versus actual method without needing to accuse anyone of fabricating capability.
3. **AlphaFold or Waymo** — prevents the chapter from becoming reflexively anti-AI by showing genuine progress.

- SEC AI washing;
- 95% statistic;

- FTC deceptive AI claims;

- AlphaFold;
