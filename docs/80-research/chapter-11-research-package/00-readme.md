# Chapter 11 Research Package — AI Hype vs Reality

**Book:** *How-To-Use-AI.com* — Book 1  
**Chapter:** 11 — AI Hype vs Reality  
**Research date:** 2026-10-01  
**Purpose:** research package for a later chapter-writing system; this package is **not** a draft of Chapter 11.

## Authoritative scope

The approved Chapter 11 plan is the authority for chapter purpose, reader state, learning outcomes, planned sections, and acceptance criteria. The current draft/template and DJ's notes are treated as editorial direction and material to test, not as facts to preserve uncritically.

The package follows the commissioning brief's central discipline:

- research real cases even when the printed chapter later describes them generically;
- distinguish what a source establishes from what it merely claims;
- prefer primary sources and original research;
- record disputes and methodological limits;
- collect evidence of both **overhype** and **underhype**;
- protect the distinction between hype-scepticism and denial of genuine risks;
- give the later writer practical material that helps readers evaluate claims themselves.

## Major findings in one page

1. **The chapter's core framing is defensible if “transformative” is defined rather than used as a slogan.** Adoption, investment, capability gains, and specific scientific uses are large and measurable. None of those facts establishes every stronger claim about productivity, job replacement, AGI, or social transformation.
2. **“AI hype” is not one phenomenon.** At least six mechanisms recur: extrapolating a narrow result, presenting a controlled demonstration as ordinary use, presenting a forecast as a current fact, changing the denominator or success criterion in a headline, anthropomorphic framing, and commercial/regulatory “AI washing.”
3. **Prediction should be added as its own claim type.** The existing demo/product/research distinction is useful but incomplete. AGI timelines, extinction-risk estimates, job forecasts, investment forecasts, and “AI will cure X” statements are not demonstrations or product claims; they are forecasts with uncertainty and dates.
4. **The existing self-driving example is outdated in its current form.** Fully autonomous public ride-hailing is now a real product in multiple US cities. The teaching point should be scoped reliability: a product can be genuine while operating only in defined locations, weather, road, regulatory, and service conditions.
5. **The “95% of AI pilots fail” story is an unusually good worked example.** The underlying 2025 Project NANDA report is explicitly preliminary and uses multiple methods and outcome concepts. Viral coverage often compressed these into the much stronger universal claim “95% of AI projects fail.” This demonstrates that anti-hype can itself become hype.
6. **Regulators provide unusually strong examples because they establish specific misleading conduct without requiring the book to infer motives.** The US SEC's 2024 “AI washing” cases, FTC's 2024–25 deceptive-AI enforcement, ASIC's Australian governance report, and Australia's consumer-law review are particularly useful.
7. **The executive analogy in DJ's notes should change.** The horses-to-cars analogy can backfire because working horses actually were displaced by mechanisation. Factory electrification is a stronger organisational analogy: benefits depended on complementary workflow, layout, skill, and management changes rather than installing the technology alone.
8. **The student analogies can work only with explicit break-points.** “Calculator for words/text” is memorable precisely because language models are unlike calculators in reliability. “Bicycle for the mind” is verifiable as Steve Jobs's phrase about computers, but it fits augmentation better than autonomous delegation.
9. **The seniors' “microwave” analogy has some historical basis but is too reassuring if presented literally.** A microwave is narrow and predictable; generative AI is probabilistic, networked, data-collecting, and can be confidently wrong. The analogy is useful only for the idea that unfamiliar technology can become ordinary.
10. **The existing three-question evaluation method is good but underpowered.** Research on lateral reading and SIFT supports adding an original-source/independent-check step. “Who benefits?” should be reframed as an incentive/conflict prompt, not a truth test, to avoid cynical motive reasoning.
11. **Benchmarks need their own beginner explanation.** “AI beats humans” often means a score on a particular protocol. Benchmark contamination, saturation, scoring design, comparator choice, and benchmark-to-job transfer are separate issues.
12. **Calibration requires positive counterexamples.** AlphaFold, GraphCast, materials-discovery systems, and current autonomous ride-hailing prevent the chapter from becoming “every dramatic claim is marketing.”

## Naming guidance for the later writer

The approved plan prefers generic examples in print. The research package therefore distinguishes between:

- **documented named case** — retained here so evidence is auditable;
- **generalisable pattern** — the teaching point that can survive product/company turnover;
- **suggested generic rendering** — a way to describe the case without identifying the party;
- **naming defensibility** — whether a regulator, court, original disclosure, or robust public record makes naming comparatively low-risk if the author later changes the editorial rule.

Avoid “lied,” “fake,” “fraud,” or “dishonest” unless the legal or primary-source record establishes that characterization. Prefer precise descriptions such as **edited**, **staged**, **simulated**, **pre-scripted**, **cherry-picked**, **not representative of live use**, **misleading according to regulator**, or **disputed**.

## Evidence labels used in this package

- **[Primary]** — regulator, official body, company disclosure, original paper, original dataset/report.
- **[Peer-reviewed]** — peer-reviewed academic publication.
- **[Preprint]** — not necessarily peer reviewed.
- **[Company source]** — authoritative for what the company says/does, not independent evidence of performance.
- **[Regulator]** — enforcement/guidance/report from a public regulator.
- **[Independent review]** — hands-on external testing, useful but usually not controlled research.
- **[Consulting survey]** — useful organisational evidence; often self-reported and commercially produced.
- **[Investigative/secondary]** — journalism that adds reporting or context; verify important factual conclusions against primary material where possible.

## Important cautions

- AI capabilities and deployment are moving rapidly. Anything labelled **Recheck before publication** should be refreshed close to manuscript lock.
- Forecasts are not observations. Expert surveys are distributions of beliefs, not collective facts.
- Company claims establish what was claimed; they do not independently establish that the claim is true.
- A regulator settlement can establish the regulator's findings and agreed settlement terms without necessarily establishing an admission of wrongdoing; wording must track the order/notice.
- “Peer reviewed” is not a synonym for “true,” and “preprint” is not a synonym for “wrong.” These labels change the strength and type of scrutiny, not the logical status of a claim.
- This package is research/editorial material, not legal advice.

## Best candidate spine for the eventual chapter

Without writing the chapter, the research points toward a simple editorial progression:

1. AI can be genuinely consequential **and** frequently oversold.
2. Learn to identify **what kind of claim** you are hearing.
3. Separate what was demonstrated/measured from what was inferred/promised.
4. Check the original source and independent evidence.
5. Treat incentives as a reason to verify, not a reason to disbelieve automatically.
6. For forecasts, demand a timeframe, observable milestone, and uncertainty.
7. Preserve calibration by examining examples where sceptics were too dismissive as well as where promoters oversold.

That spine is consistent with the approved “informed, not panicked, not gullible” purpose and is reusable in Chapter 14.
