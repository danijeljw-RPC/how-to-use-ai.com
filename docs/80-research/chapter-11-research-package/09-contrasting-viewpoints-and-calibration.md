# 09 — Contrasting Viewpoints, Risk-Denial, and Calibration

## 1. The chapter's real target is miscalibration

The evidence supports an editorial shift from “hype versus reality” as a binary to **claim calibration**:

- How strong is the evidence?
- How broad is the claim?
- Is it observation or prediction?
- How uncertain is it?
- What would make confidence rise or fall?

This lets the book preserve the approved title while avoiding a simplistic “hype = false, reality = sceptical” structure.

## 2. Apparently conflicting statements that can both be true

### Capability and reliability

> AI capabilities have improved rapidly on many measured tasks.

and

> The same systems remain unreliable on some simple tasks and in open-ended settings.

The International AI Safety Report 2026 explicitly describes capability gains as rapid but **jagged**.  
https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026

### Commercial value and implementation difficulty

> Organisations use AI widely and some task-level experiments show significant gains.

and

> Enterprise-wide financial return is uneven and often requires workflow redesign.

Sources: Stanford AI Index; McKinsey; NBER studies.  
https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

### Risk and uncertainty

> Serious researchers identify plausible severe future risks.

and

> Probabilities, timelines and mechanisms remain highly uncertain and disputed.

Those are not contradictory. Uncertainty is often the core reason risk assessment exists.

### Investment excess and technological usefulness

> Some AI valuations/investment may embed speculative expectations.

and

> AI systems may still become widely useful infrastructure.

Asset pricing and technical utility are separate propositions.

## 3. Main positions on long-term AI risk

### Position: precaution around potentially catastrophic systems

Reasoning:
- capabilities are improving;
- some risk pathways have severe consequences;
- direct evidence may arrive too late;
- safety/governance should precede deployment of dangerous capabilities.

Evidence emphasis:
- frontier evaluations;
- controlled uplift studies;
- theoretical/control arguments;
- historical risk-management precedent.

Uncertainty:
- future system architecture/autonomy;
- real-world deployment and access;
- probability of escalation from laboratory indicators.

### Position: focus on current, concrete harms

Reasoning:
- present harms are measurable and affecting people now;
- speculative scenarios can dominate resources/policy attention;
- anthropomorphic/exponential assumptions may overstate autonomous agency;
- institutions/adoption constrain technical capability.

Evidence emphasis:
- discrimination, labour, surveillance, data practices, misinformation, environmental costs;
- historical technology diffusion;
- observed limitations.

Uncertainty:
- present-focus could underweight low-probability severe future hazards;
- capability progress can invalidate assumptions quickly.

### Position: both present and future risks deserve study

This is effectively the stance of broad international assessments: distinguish evidence types, keep uncertainty explicit, and manage multiple risk categories without forcing them into one ideological camp.

## 4. “AI is a bubble” — map the disagreement without verdict

### Evidence commonly used for bubble concerns

- exceptional capital expenditure and private investment;
- valuations that assume large future markets;
- weak enterprise-wide ROI at many organisations;
- dependence on continued infrastructure spending;
- financing concentration/circularity concerns;
- rapid entry of low-differentiation “AI” products.

### Evidence commonly used against a simple bubble narrative

- real model/service revenue;
- rapid consumer and enterprise adoption;
- measurable task productivity gains;
- demand for compute/infrastructure;
- deployment into existing software/services;
- scientific/industrial use cases.

### Editorial discipline

“Bubble” should always be unpacked:

- bubble in **which assets**?
- measured against **what expected cash flows**?
- over **what horizon**?
- does the speaker mean “prices are too high,” “companies will fail,” or “technology is useless”?

Those are different claims.

## 5. Motivated scepticism

People do not apply scepticism evenly. They tend to scrutinise claims they dislike more harshly than claims that support existing beliefs/identity.

For Chapter 11, the practical response is not to teach a psychology lecture. It is to make the method **symmetric**:

- apply the same “show me the source” rule to a bullish product claim and a viral debunking;
- ask for denominator/method whether the statistic is good news or bad news;
- ask for a date/definition whether the forecast is utopian or catastrophic.

The 95% statistic is useful precisely because it asks an AI-sceptical reader to scrutinise a claim they may be predisposed to like.

## 6. Trust in experts without blind deference

A beginner needs a middle path between:

- “an expert said it, therefore true”; and
- “experts disagree, therefore nobody knows anything.”

Useful hierarchy:

1. **Relevant expertise** increases the prior credibility of interpretation in that domain.
2. **Direct evidence and method** still matter.
3. **Agreement among independent sources** can strengthen confidence.
4. **Conflict of interest** is relevant context, not automatic disqualification.
5. **Prediction outside a person's specialty** should not inherit all the authority of their technical achievements.

Example: a Nobel-winning scientist can be exceptionally authoritative about a demonstrated scientific result while a ten-year forecast about society/medicine remains a forecast.

## 7. When real risks were dismissed as hype — pattern warning

Chapter 11 does not need an extensive non-AI historical catalogue. The general lesson is that warnings can look alarmist before enough data accumulates.

Good generic examples from other domains include:

- cybersecurity vulnerabilities initially treated as unlikely edge cases before widespread exploitation;
- public-health risks where early uncertainty was used by some parties to delay action;
- environmental hazards where long lag times separated evidence from policy response.

### Caution

Do not use these analogies to imply AI catastrophe is therefore likely. They only show the logical error in “people have exaggerated before, therefore this warning is false.”

## 8. When hyped risks were smaller than claimed

Technology histories also contain moral panics and inflated forecasts. Examples include predictions that particular media/communications technologies would rapidly destroy existing social institutions, and recurrent claims that one new technology would immediately eliminate whole professions.

Again, the point is not “fear is always wrong.” It is that **historical analogy cannot settle a new claim without mechanism/evidence**.

## 9. Calibration language for the eventual writer

Potential language patterns, not final chapter copy:

- “The evidence is strong for X, weaker for Y, and mostly predictive for Z.”
- “This is possible; it is not yet demonstrated as reliable.”
- “The company has announced it; independent testing has not yet established it.”
- “Researchers disagree mainly about the timeline/probability, not about whether the mechanism deserves study.”
- “The headline is broader than the experiment.”
- “This result is real; the social conclusion attached to it is a separate claim.”
- “A conflict of interest is a reason to verify, not a reason to reverse the claim.”

## 10. A confidence vocabulary may help beginners

Instead of true/false only:

- **demonstrated** — directly shown/measured;
- **well supported** — multiple strong sources;
- **promising** — evidence exists but limited/generalisation uncertain;
- **plausible** — mechanism/reasoning exists but evidence incomplete;
- **speculative** — depends heavily on uncertain assumptions;
- **unsupported** — assertion without adequate evidence;
- **contradicted** — strong evidence points against it.

### Warning

Do not turn these into formal scores. They are prose tools for explaining confidence.

## 11. Calibration through positive and negative examples

A recommended sequence for Chapter 11:

1. **Misleading demo** — scepticism catches overstatement.
2. **95% failure headline** — scepticism catches overstatement from critics.
3. **AlphaFold/Waymo** — scepticism does not become denial of genuine progress.
4. **International AI Safety Report** — uncertainty does not become dismissal of serious risk.

That four-example sequence demonstrates the chapter's philosophy better than a page of caveats.

## 12. Common misconceptions and corrections

### “Every AI claim is marketing.”
No. Peer-reviewed research, regulator reports, independent evaluations and public datasets provide evidence outside product marketing.

### “If a company benefits, the claim must be false.”
No. Incentive is context; evidence decides.

### “If experts disagree, both sides are equally supported.”
No. Disagreement can involve different evidence strength, definitions, or values. Do not manufacture symmetry.

### “A cautious claim is more trustworthy because it sounds cautious.”
Not necessarily. Style is not evidence. A carefully hedged unsupported claim can still be weak.

### “A dramatic claim is false because it sounds dramatic.”
Also invalid. Some genuine results are dramatic.

### “AI hype is unique.”
No. Emerging technologies repeatedly produce cycles of promotional, fearful and utopian narratives. AI is unusual in speed, generality and anthropomorphic interaction, but the incentive structures are not unique.

## 13. What the later writer should avoid

- “both sides” paragraphs that imply equal evidence when one side is poorly supported;
- labelling researchers as doomers/boosters in a way that substitutes identity for arguments;
- using financial incentives to dismiss technical results;
- presenting survey beliefs as probabilities generated by a physical model;
- false certainty about consciousness, AGI timing, net jobs or investment outcomes;
- cynicism as a personality style.
