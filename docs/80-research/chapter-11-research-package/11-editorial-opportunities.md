# 11 — Editorial Opportunities

Everything in this file is an **editorial possibility**, not final chapter copy.

## 1. Best single worked example: “95% of AI projects fail”

### Concept explained
How a memorable statistic mutates when method, denominator and success definition disappear.

### Suggested presentation

**Headline:** “95% of AI projects fail.”  
↓  
**Find original:** preliminary enterprise GenAI report.  
↓  
**Ask 95% of what:** selected initiatives/organisational evidence, not every project worldwide.  
↓  
**Ask what ‘fail’ means:** no measurable business/P&L impact or failed scaling is not the same as technical failure.  
↓  
**Check method:** reviews + interviews + survey; not a census/random global sample.  
↓  
**Calibrated conclusion:** meaningful warning about enterprise implementation; viral wording much broader.

### Audience
Everyone; especially executives.

### Why it works
It forces scepticism toward an anti-hype claim, proving the chapter is not merely defending AI companies.

### Website extension
Interactive “X-ray the statistic” with clickable denominator, sample, outcome, source date.

## 2. Best demo example: live-looking multimodal video

### Concept
Demo vs ordinary product interaction.

### Side-by-side

| Viewer impression | Technical explanation |
| --- | --- |
| continuous live interaction | selected still-image frames |
| spoken back-and-forth | text prompts used in production |
| natural response latency | video edited/shortened |
| representative ordinary use | demonstration assembled to showcase capability |

### Critical wording
“The model outputs were real; the presentation made the interaction look more live/seamless than the underlying prompting process.”

### Why it works
Avoids the childish binary “fake/not fake.”

## 3. Best benchmark example: bar-exam percentile

### Concept
Comparator/denominator choice.

### Reader exercise
Give three statements:

- “The model passed the exam.”
- “The model scored around the 90th percentile under a particular comparison.”
- “The model is better than 90% of lawyers.”

Ask which statements are established by the evidence. This makes the inference ladder visible.

## 4. Best student example: assisted performance vs learning

### Concept
Metric mismatch.

### Simple visual

**While AI is present:** score ↑  
**AI removed later:** retained performance may ↓  
**With constrained tutor:** different result

### Why it works
Students understand immediately that “got more answers right” and “learned more” are different outcomes.

## 5. Best positive example: breakthrough → deployment chain

Use AlphaFold or materials discovery.

## 6. Claim-type table

A compact table may replace multiple paragraphs:

| If you are looking at… | It can show… | Ask… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demo | possible capability | Was it live/selected/edited? |
| Product | available scoped capability | How reliable, where, for whom? |
| Research result | measured result | What metric/sample/comparator? |
| Forecast | expectation about future | By when? What would count as wrong? |
| Advertisement | seller's representation | Where is the evidence? |
| Anecdote | one observed case | How typical is it? |

## 7. Possible → dependable ladder

### Concept
One successful output is not a product guarantee.

### Ladder

Possible  
→ Repeatable  
→ Reliable  
→ Available  
→ Affordable  
→ Safe to depend on

### Breakpoint
Not every use needs the final rung. Brainstorming can tolerate failures that medical/financial control cannot.

## 8. “Compared with what?” mini-callout

This can be one of the chapter's most useful phrases.

Examples:

- “90th percentile” — compared with which people?
- “50% more productive” — compared with what workflow?
- “more accurate than doctors” — which doctors, doing which task, with what information?
- “95% fail” — which projects and what counted as failure?
- “#1 model” — on which benchmark/leaderboard and when?

A reader who learns only this habit will catch many inflated claims.

## 9. Forecast-specific callout

### Possible mnemonic (editorial option)

**DATE**

- **D**efine the event.
- **A**ssumptions/evidence.
- **T**imeframe.
- **E**vidence that would prove it wrong/right.

This has not been validated as a research framework; it is an editorial mnemonic only. Do not present it as established science.

## 10. “Who benefits?” replacement variants

Potential formulations to test for tone/memorability:

- “Who has a stake in this claim?”
- “What incentive means I should verify this?”
- “Who gains if this framing catches on?”
- “Is the source selling the product, the fear, or the debunk?”

Last version is punchier but risks cynicism; first two are more neutral.

## 11. Four-source lateral-reading demonstration

Take a fictional claim: “New model beats doctors at diagnosis.”

Reader is shown four tabs:

1. company press release;
2. original paper;
3. independent expert/news analysis;
4. regulator/professional guidance.

Ask how confidence changes as scope becomes visible.

Companion website could make this interactive.

## 13. Executive vignette

Vendor claim: “Our agent can automate your customer-service department.”

Apply method:

- claim type: product/vendor forecast;
- evidence: demo + vendor case study;
- measurement: resolution time? cost? escalation? customer satisfaction? error rate?
- independent check: reference customers/evaluation;
- human work: review/escalation/training;
- scope: which queries/languages/channels;
- organisational dependency: workflow/data/policy.

### Teaching point
Not “reject the vendor.” Convert the pitch into measurable claims.

## 14. Student vignette

Claim: “This AI passed a medical exam, so doctors are obsolete.”

Apply:

- exam result may be real;
- exam measures a subset of professional competence;
- compare model conditions with candidate conditions;
- check clinical validation, responsibility and real-world performance;
- separate current result from job forecast.

## 15. Older-reader vignette

Claim: “The chatbot talks like a person, so it understands what I mean.”

Apply:

- conversational fluency is observable;
- consciousness/understanding is a separate claim;
- test practical reliability: ask verifiable questions, inspect errors;
- humanlike voice/name is interface evidence, not mind evidence.

## 16. Alternative analogy for executives: electrification

Short version:

> Early factories gained little by swapping one power source for another while keeping the same layout. Larger gains arrived when factories were redesigned around electric motors. AI may similarly create more value when work changes around it, not when it is pasted onto the old process.

### Break
AI is not electricity; diffusion speed/capital needs differ. Use as organisational history, not prediction.

## 17. Alternative analogy for older readers: satnav

> You do not need to understand satellite navigation to use it. It can save enormous effort, and it can still confidently route you somewhere stupid if the map/context is wrong.

### Works for
Usability without technical expertise + retained judgement.

### Break
LLMs are much broader and less grounded than navigation systems.

## 20. Reader exercise: rank the evidence, not the claim

Present the same capability claim supported by:

A. anonymous social post  
B. company launch video  
C. company technical report  
D. independent controlled test  
E. replicated real-world evidence

Ask what each additional layer changes. Avoid saying one source type is always correct.

## 21. Reader exercise: identify the missing word

Statements:

- “AI beats humans.” → **which** humans / **at what**?
- “AI projects fail.” → **which** projects / **failure how**?
- “AGI soon.” → **defined how / by when**?
- “AI cured X.” → **in lab, trial, approved, deployed**?
- “AI is #1.” → **on what / when**?

This could be a very effective page-level callout.

## 22. Website interactive: Claim X-Ray

Input fields:

- paste claim/headline;
- claim type;
- original source;
- observation vs forecast;
- date;
- measured outcome;
- denominator/sample;
- comparator;
- independent check;
- incentives/conflicts;
- important missing qualification;
- what would falsify/weaken it;
- confidence note.

The site should avoid automatically declaring “true/false”; it should teach the user to expose structure.

## 23. Website current case library

Because named cases date quickly, the companion website can maintain:

- original claim/demo;
- later disclosure/update;
- regulator action;
- independent test;
- outcome after 6/12/24 months;
- “what we knew at the time” vs hindsight.

This would solve the print chapter's longevity problem.

## 27. Potential concise chapter table

| Claim | Better question |
| --- | --- |
| “It can do X.” | Once, or reliably? |
| “Research proves X.” | What exactly did the study measure? |
| “AI beats humans.” | Which humans, on what task? |
| “95% fail.” | 95% of what; failure defined how? |
| “AGI in five years.” | What counts as AGI; what resolves by what date? |
| “The company benefits.” | What evidence changes because of that conflict? |
| “It sounds human.” | What evidence shows understanding/consciousness? |

This is likely more useful than an additional diagram.
