# 15 — Additional Findings and Chapter Boundaries

These topics emerged from research but should only enter Chapter 11 where they strengthen the claim-evaluation skill.

## 1. Anthropomorphism belongs in Chapter 11 only as a hype mechanism

### Why relevant
Humanlike language/voices/personas can make users infer understanding, intention or consciousness from interface behaviour.

### Chapter 11 use
One short section or example explaining:

> humanlike presentation is evidence about the interface, not independent evidence about a mind.

### Better home for depth
Chapter 4 (what AI is/isn't) and companion website.

### Sources
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-02108-6  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708

## 2. Sycophancy is a product-level reason fluent confidence can mislead

### Concept
Language models can sometimes adapt answers toward the user's stated beliefs/preferences, including agreeing when disagreement would be more accurate. This is distinct from traditional “hype,” but it reinforces why users should not interpret agreeable conversational behaviour as independent validation.

### Chapter 11 fit
Small sidebar at most. The chapter is about claims **about AI**, not primarily failures in AI answers.

### Better home
Chapter 4/5 or companion website.

## 3. “AI income” influencers and get-rich schemes

### Why relevant
They combine technology novelty, financial aspiration and low-verifiability testimonials—an almost ideal hype environment.

### Evidence anchor
FTC Operation AI Comply documented deceptive income/business-opportunity claims using AI framing.  
https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes

### Chapter boundary
One sentence/example can show consumer hype; detailed scam recognition belongs in Chapter 13.

## 4. Public trust in AI

### Why relevant
Hype can damage trust when promised capabilities fail, while excessive fear can suppress useful adoption.

### Chapter fit
Use qualitatively in conclusion/calibration. Avoid adding another public-opinion statistics subsection unless a current Australian survey directly serves the narrative.

### Better source direction
Adult Media Literacy in Australia; national surveys; Stanford AI Index where public-opinion chapter exists.

## 5. People with disabilities — high value, high stakes

### Why relevant
AI accessibility uses (captioning, speech, vision, summarisation) can provide genuine benefits, but unreliable output can create disproportionate harm.

### Chapter fit
Potential underhype/calibration example if the book needs one more consumer audience.

### Boundary
Detailed accessibility use belongs Chapter 6/companion website. Do not present disability as a monolithic audience.

### Chapter fit

### Boundary
Parent guidance and education workflows belong elsewhere.

## 7. “Agent washing”

### Concept
As “AI agent” becomes commercially valuable, conventional automation, chat wrappers or workflow scripts can be rebranded as autonomous agents.

### Why it belongs conceptually
It is a new instance of the same AI-washing pattern and will likely remain relevant as terminology changes.

### Editorial approach
Do not fight over the “true definition” of agent. Ask:

- What actions can it take?
- With what tools/permissions?
- How long can it operate without intervention?
- How often does a human approve steps?
- Can it recover from failure?
- What tasks were tested end-to-end?

### Best home
Short future-proofing paragraph or Chapter 14 cross-reference; current named examples on website.

## 8. Current autonomous systems show why “operating envelope” is a better concept than universal capability

### Finding
A product can be real and valuable while deliberately bounded.

### Examples
- autonomous ride-hailing within defined service regions;
- medical AI cleared for a particular indication/workflow;
- enterprise agent permitted to take only defined actions;
- AI tutor constrained to a curriculum.

### Chapter value
This concept avoids two errors:

- “doesn't work everywhere, therefore fake”;
- “works somewhere, therefore solved.”

## 9. Source provenance can itself be a worked lesson

The commissioning brief contains several “starting leads.” Research showed why that instruction was valuable:

- some leads were well documented and clean (SEC AI washing, Google Gemini demo);
- some required careful disputes/context (Amazon humans);
- some are legally/procedurally live (Tesla);
- some viral formulations were themselves suspect (Builder.ai human-engineer story);
- some catchy principles have weak primary provenance (Amara's Law, calculator wording).

### Companion website opportunity
Publish a “How we verified this chapter” source-provenance page. This demonstrates the method rather than merely teaching it.

## 10. “Underhype” deserves a name, but not a new ideology

### Useful definition
A claim/capability that is dismissed because it resembles prior hype even when new evidence now supports it.

### Why useful
Readers suffering hype fatigue can become systematically late to genuine changes.

### Good examples
- current autonomous ride-hailing;
- AlphaFold;
- rapid benchmark/capability gains documented by international assessment.

### Caution
Do not create an “underhype” section that becomes promotional cheerleading. It is there to demonstrate symmetric evaluation.

## 12. Chapter 14 should visibly “submit itself” to Chapter 11

A strong cross-book editorial device would be to label Chapter 14's own forward-looking statements as:

- **current capability**;
- **near-term announced/deploying**;
- **forecast**;
- **speculative scenario**.

This would prove the author applies the evaluation method to claims he wants the reader to take seriously, not only to claims he dislikes.
