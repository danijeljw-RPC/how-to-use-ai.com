# 02 — AGI, Doom Claims, and Long-Term Risk

## Purpose of this file

This file gives the later writer enough evidence to distinguish:

- serious long-term/catastrophic-risk research;
- uncertainty about AGI definitions and timelines;
- public forecasts and advocacy statements;
- unsupported claims of imminent catastrophe;
- arguments that long-term-risk narratives distract from present harms;
- arguments that uncertainty is not a reason to ignore potentially severe risks.

It deliberately does **not** decide whether long-term AI risk is “real” or “hype.”

## 1. AGI has no single settled operational definition

### DeepMind/academic “Levels of AGI” framework

Morris et al. propose separating **performance**, **generality**, and **autonomy**, with levels analogous to emerging/competent/expert/virtuoso/superhuman performance. The paper exists partly because “AGI” had become too vague to support clean comparisons. **[Peer-reviewed conference paper / primary research]**  
Source: Morris et al., “Position: Levels of AGI for Operationalizing Progress on the Path to AGI,” ICML 2024.  
https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/morris24b.html

**What it supports:** AGI is not a binary, universally operationalised milestone; useful definitions need dimensions and thresholds.  
**Limitation:** it is a proposed framework, not an agreed international standard.

### OpenAI Charter definition

OpenAI's Charter defines AGI as “highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work.” **[Company source]**  
https://openai.com/charter/

**What it supports:** prominent organisations can use economically framed definitions rather than philosophical “human-level mind” definitions.  
**Limitation:** a company's working definition does not establish a field-wide consensus.

### Beginner takeaway

When a headline says “AGI by 2030,” the first question should be **“What counts as AGI in this forecast?”** Two people can give different timelines while talking about different thresholds.

## 2. “Superintelligence” is also a term of debate

In broad use, superintelligence means an artificial system that substantially exceeds human capabilities across many cognitively important domains. The concept is often used in long-term-risk arguments because a system much more capable than people could create novel control, misuse, and governance problems.

For a beginner chapter, avoid implying that “AGI → superintelligence” is an established automatic sequence. It is a scenario or argument about possible development trajectories.

## 3. The 2026 International AI Safety Report: a useful calibration anchor

The *International AI Safety Report 2026* was written with guidance from more than 100 independent experts and an international advisory panel with nominees from more than 30 countries/international organisations. It explicitly says contributors differ over the speed of capability improvement, potential severity of risks, and adequacy of safeguards. It does not recommend a single policy. **[International scientific assessment]**  
https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026

Key findings relevant to Chapter 11:

- general-purpose AI capabilities continued to improve quickly, especially in mathematics, coding and autonomous operation;
- performance remains **jagged**: systems that excel at difficult tasks still fail at simpler ones;
- some harms are already documented;
- other emerging risks remain uncertain but could be severe if they materialise;
- evidence for future risks often comes from laboratory tests, modelling and theory rather than direct real-world observations;
- risk-management evidence remains incomplete.

**Why this is useful:** it makes it difficult to sustain either extreme slogan—“nothing serious is happening” or “the science proves catastrophe is imminent.”

## 4. Expert surveys: distributions of belief, not consensus

Grace et al. surveyed 2,778 researchers who had published at major AI venues. Respondents' forecasts varied widely. The study reported aggregate probabilities for milestones and for extremely good/bad outcomes, including non-trivial probabilities assigned by many respondents to severe outcomes. **[Research survey; later published in JAIR]**  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843

Important methodological cautions for the book:

- respondents were a subset of contacted researchers; response/non-response can affect representativeness;
- probability questions are sensitive to wording and framing;
- forecasts are beliefs about uncertain futures, not measurements of present capability;
- median or aggregate probabilities can hide enormous disagreement;
- “many experts assign X%” is not the same as “experts agree X will happen.”

**Editorial lesson:** expert surveys are excellent examples of how a headline can turn a **distribution** into a **consensus**.

## 5. Public risk statements and what they actually said

### Future of Life Institute open letter (March 2023)

The letter called for AI labs to pause for at least six months the training of systems more powerful than GPT-4 while shared safety protocols were developed. It became a major news event and attracted many signatures. **[Advocacy/open letter]**  
https://futureoflife.org/open-letter/pause-giant-ai-experiments/

**Important distinction:** signing a pause letter does not imply the signer predicts human extinction, and non-signature does not imply the opposite.

### Center for AI Safety statement (May 2023)

The one-sentence statement says that mitigating extinction risk from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks. It was signed by many prominent researchers and executives. **[Advocacy statement]**  
https://safe.ai/work/statement-on-ai-extinction-risk

**Important distinction:** the statement identifies a priority; it does not specify a probability, timeline, mechanism, or policy.

### Teaching value

A short statement is often quoted as if it supplies an argument. The reader should separate:

- **the proposition** (“this risk deserves priority”);
- **the mechanism** (how could it happen?);
- **probability/timeline**;
- **evidence**;
- **recommended action**.

Those are different claims.

## 6. Major long-term-risk arguments — neutral map

### Argument family A: loss of control / misaligned optimisation

A highly capable autonomous system may pursue objectives or strategies that conflict with human intent, especially if it can plan, acquire resources, evade oversight, or modify/replicate systems.

**Evidence types currently used:** theoretical arguments, controlled model evaluations, capability trends, agent simulations, observed specification gaming/deception-like behaviours in constrained experiments.

**Main uncertainty:** extrapolating from present systems and laboratory behaviours to future systems operating at much greater capability/autonomy.

### Argument family B: misuse amplification

Advanced models could lower barriers for cyber operations, biological design, fraud, influence operations, or weapons-related knowledge.

**Evidence types:** controlled uplift studies, red-team evaluations, incident reports, developer safety evaluations.

**Main uncertainty:** translating model assistance under tests into real-world actor capability, access, constraints and defences.

### Argument family C: concentration and strategic instability

Highly capable AI could concentrate economic/military power, speed decision cycles, and create arms-race dynamics even without “sentient” systems.

**Evidence types:** economics, political science, governance scenarios, current compute/market concentration.

**Main uncertainty:** institutional adaptation and future diffusion.

## 7. Major critiques of long-term-risk emphasis

### Critique A: present harms are more measurable

Researchers focused on bias, labour, surveillance, environmental burden, data exploitation and misinformation argue these effects are happening now and can be obscured by science-fiction-scale narratives.

### Critique B: speculative scenarios can inherit assumptions

Forecasts may compound uncertain assumptions about scaling, autonomy, agency, economic deployment and institutional response.

### Critique C: extraordinary capability narratives can benefit incumbents

A company can simultaneously warn that its systems may become extraordinarily powerful and use that perceived power to attract capital, policy influence or market attention. This is a **possible incentive**, not proof that the warning is false or insincere.

### Critique D: anthropomorphic language can smuggle agency into the argument

Phrases such as “AI wants,” “AI decides to escape,” or “AI competes with humanity” can conceal the technical steps required for a system to have durable goals, autonomy, tool access and real-world influence.

## 8. Counterarguments to those critiques

- Low-probability, high-consequence risks are routinely studied before direct evidence exists (nuclear safety, pandemics, aviation failure modes).
- Waiting for unambiguous real-world catastrophe can make prevention impossible.
- Present harms and future risks are not mutually exclusive research agendas.
- Some long-term-risk researchers are academics/non-profit researchers with no direct product-marketing interest.
- Incentive analysis cannot substitute for evaluating the mechanism and evidence.

## 9. “AI as Normal Technology” as a contrasting framework

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that even highly consequential AI should be analysed as technology embedded in institutions rather than as an autonomous species-like actor. They emphasise diffusion, complementary organisational change, regulation and social systems. **[Scholarly essay / position]**  
https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology

**Usefulness:** gives the writer a coherent non-doom framework that still accepts substantial impact.  
**Limitation:** it is an argument, not an empirical proof that extreme-risk scenarios cannot occur.

## 10. Historical predictions and AI winters

The history of AI contains both overconfident forecasts and genuine achievements that arrived later than predicted.

The US National Academies' AI history notes that Newell and Simon predicted in the late 1950s that a computer would become world chess champion within roughly a decade; IBM's Deep Blue defeated world champion Garry Kasparov in a match in 1997, roughly four decades later. **[Historical secondary/authoritative synthesis]**  
https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11106/chapter/8

**Teaching value:** a forecast can be directionally right and temporally wrong. “Eventually happened” does not validate the original timeline; “missed the timeline” does not prove the capability impossible.

AI experienced periods later called “AI winters,” where funding and enthusiasm fell after expectations outran results. Historical overclaiming can teach humility, but it should not be used mechanically to claim every current boom must crash.

Useful context: MIT Press/Daedalus historical review.  
https://direct.mit.edu/daed/article/151/2/28/110616/From-So-Simple-a-Beginning-Species-of-Artificial

## 11. Forecasting platforms and track records

Forecasting platforms can improve on vague predictions by forcing:

- a resolution date;
- a measurable outcome;
- probability updates;
- a public history of forecasts.

But platform forecasts still depend on question wording and participant selection. The chapter need not teach forecasting theory; it can borrow one principle:

> A prediction that has no date and no observable condition for success can remain “right” forever.

That directly supports the draft's falsifiability question.

## 12. AGI and doom misconceptions — evidence-based corrections

### “AGI is just around the corner.”
Incomplete. Some experts and executives forecast short timelines; others forecast much longer timelines or reject common definitions. Ask what AGI means and what evidence/timeline is attached.

### “AGI is impossible.”
Also not established. There is no scientific result proving that broadly capable machine intelligence is impossible. A sceptical view about current methods is a forecast/technical argument, not a theorem.

### “Everyone in AI agrees AI is an existential risk.”
False. Surveys and public debate show wide disagreement in probabilities, mechanisms and priorities.

### “Nobody serious worries about long-term risk.”
False. The 2026 International AI Safety Report, major researchers, national institutes and international processes all treat frontier risks as legitimate research topics.

### “Companies only talk about risk to make their products sound powerful.”
An incentive hypothesis, not a universal explanation. It may apply in some communications, but many independent researchers also study catastrophic risk. Evaluate the actual argument.

### “Current AI is conscious.”
Not established by scientific evidence. Consciousness is not directly measurable in machines and definitions are contested.

### “AI definitely can never be conscious.”
Also stronger than current evidence supports. Butlin et al. found no current system satisfied their proposed consciousness indicators while arguing there was no obvious technical barrier in principle. **[Preprint/technical assessment]**  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708

**Chapter boundary:** detailed consciousness discussion belongs primarily in Chapter 4; Chapter 11 only needs enough to show how certainty exceeds evidence in both directions.

## 13. Suggested generic examples for print

### Generic doom example

> “A post claims a superintelligent system will end civilisation within a named year, but provides no definition of the milestone, no chain of events, no probability and no condition that would count as being wrong.”

Teaching point: this is a **forecast with missing resolution criteria**, not the same as a technical risk paper.

### Generic serious-risk example

> “An international scientific assessment says some frontier risks are uncertain but potentially severe, explains what evidence exists, and explicitly identifies evidence gaps.”

Teaching point: uncertainty can be part of responsible analysis rather than weakness.

## 14. What the eventual writer should avoid

- Do not treat “doom” as a single camp.
- Do not reduce disagreement to personality or motives.
- Do not use an expert survey as a referendum on truth.
- Do not describe a non-zero probability as a prediction that the event will occur.
- Do not imply that inability to prove catastrophe means zero risk.
- Do not imply that possibility of catastrophe means a particular timeline is established.
- Do not let AGI debate overwhelm the practical claim-evaluation chapter.
