# 1. Misinformation

Research the relationship between generative AI and misinformation in detail.

Distinguish between:

- **misinformation** (false information shared without intent to deceive);
- **disinformation** (false information deliberately spread to deceive);
- **malinformation** (true information used to cause harm), where useful;
- AI **generating** false content on purpose at someone's request;
- AI **accidentally** producing false content (hallucination, already covered in Chapter 4);
- AI-generated content farms and low-quality automated websites;
- fake reviews;
- AI-generated fake news sites;
- coordinated influence operations using AI;
- AI-generated content polluting search results;
- and AI chatbots repeating or amplifying false claims found online.

Investigate the evidence carefully.

Actively research the debate over whether generative AI has **substantially changed** the misinformation landscape or whether its effect has so far been **smaller than feared**. Serious researchers have argued both positions. For example, some have argued that the main bottleneck for misinformation was never the cost of producing false content but the difficulty of getting people to see and believe it. Others have documented measurable growth in AI-generated content, sites, and operations.

Research:

- documented AI-enabled influence operations, including any reported by AI companies themselves in threat reports;
- elections held since generative AI became widely available, and what independent researchers concluded about AI's actual impact on them;
- research on whether AI-generated persuasive text is more, less, or equally persuasive than human-written text;
- research on whether people can distinguish AI-generated from human-written text;
- research on AI-generated fake reviews and product listings;
- research on AI content farms and their business models (for example, advertising revenue);
- what search engines, social platforms, and marketplaces have done in response;
- Australian developments, including any proposed or abandoned misinformation legislation and the role of the relevant regulator;
- and what practical habits genuinely help individuals.

Do not assume the answer is either "AI has flooded the world with misinformation" or "AI misinformation fears are overblown."

Record where the evidence currently stands and where it is uncertain.

# 4. Bias

Research bias and discrimination in AI systems.

The draft's explanation is that AI learns patterns from its training data, and if that data reflects unfair patterns, AI can reproduce or amplify them.

Research whether that explanation is complete. Investigate other sources of bias, such as:

- unrepresentative or incomplete training data;
- historical data that reflects past discrimination;
- the choice of what the system is trained to predict (for example, using cost as a proxy for health need);
- proxy variables (postcode, name, school, gaps in employment);
- measurement bias;
- labelling decisions made by people;
- design and deployment choices;
- feedback loops (for example, predictive systems that send more scrutiny to already over-scrutinised groups);
- automation bias, where people over-trust the system's output;
- and the context in which a system is used.

Research different **domains** where AI bias has been documented or seriously alleged:

- hiring and recruitment screening;
- lending and credit;
- insurance;
- policing and criminal justice risk assessment;
- facial recognition accuracy across demographic groups;
- healthcare algorithms;
- welfare and government decision-making;
- education (including AI-writing detectors and non-native English speakers);
- advertising delivery (for example, which people are shown job or housing ads);
- generative AI text and image outputs (stereotypes in generated images and text).

For each major documented case, record:

- what the system was;
- who deployed it;
- what was found;
- who found it (independent researchers, journalists, regulators, courts, the company itself);
- what the methodology was;
- whether the finding was disputed, and by whom;
- what happened afterwards;
- and what it genuinely demonstrates.

Research the debate about **what "fair" means**. Some researchers have shown that different reasonable definitions of algorithmic fairness can be mathematically incompatible, so a system cannot satisfy all of them at once. Find a beginner-appropriate way to explain why "just remove the bias" is harder than it sounds, without making the reader feel the problem is hopeless.

Research **automated decision-making in government**, including cases where the harm came from an automated system that was not necessarily "AI" in the modern sense. Consider whether such cases are useful to the chapter or would confuse the reader about what AI is. For Australia, research whether the Robodebt scheme and the resulting Royal Commission are relevant, how accurately they can be described as an "algorithm" or "automation" problem rather than an AI problem, and what lessons the Royal Commission drew about automated decision-making.

Research the Australian legal position, including:

- anti-discrimination law and how it applies to automated decisions;
- the Australian Human Rights Commission's work on AI and human rights;
- privacy reforms relating to automated decisions;
- and any guidance from regulators.

Actively research the draft's claim that AI output "can sound neutral and objective while still reflecting biased patterns." Find evidence about automation bias and about how people perceive algorithmic decisions compared with human decisions.

# Proportion and calibration

This chapter must avoid both sensationalism and dismissiveness.

# Contrasting viewpoints

Chapter 9 concerns issues where serious people disagree about the size, urgency, and correct response to each risk.

Do not manufacture artificial "both sides" symmetry.

Instead:

- identify the main positions;
- explain the reasoning behind each;
- identify what evidence supports each;
- identify where evidence is weak;
- distinguish moral arguments from legal arguments;
- distinguish documented present harm from projected future harm;
- distinguish individual anecdotes from systematic measurement;
- distinguish company claims from independent research;
- distinguish advocacy research from independent academic research;
- identify where two apparently conflicting claims can both be true.

Examples:

> AI has made convincing fake content cheap to produce.

and:

> The measurable effect of AI-generated misinformation on election outcomes has so far been limited.

These are not necessarily contradictory.

Likewise:

> A single AI request uses a small amount of energy.

and:

> AI's total energy demand is growing fast enough to affect electricity planning.

may both be true.

Likewise:

> AI development is concentrated in a small number of very large companies.

and:

> Competition and price falls among model providers have been rapid.

may both be true at different layers of the industry.

Capture this nuance.

# Research philosophy

- AI is dangerous;
- AI is safe;

- technology companies are villains;

> Here is what people disagree about.

> Here is what needs to be handled collectively, not just individually.

That is more valuable than producing a predetermined narrative.
