# Chapter 9 Research — Scams and Fraud

Research date: 2026-10-01

## Executive research takeaways

- AI is best described as **amplifying existing scam mechanics** — impersonation, urgency, trust, social engineering, investment fraud, romance fraud — while weakening several old warning signs.
- The strongest Australian scam-loss figures cover **all scams**, not AI-enabled scams. Do not relabel them as “AI scam losses.”
- In 2025, Australians made 481,523 scam reports across the datasets combined by the National Anti-Scam Centre; 274,577 involved financial losses totalling **$2.18 billion**. This is reported loss, not a complete measure of all scam harm, and it is not AI-specific.
- The draft's line that “the core defence hasn't changed” is partly right: **independent verification, resisting urgency, and not sending money on an unexpected request remain foundational**. But some old cues have become less useful — bad grammar, recognising someone's voice, or seeing a familiar face on a video call.
- A better framing is: **the principles of defence are familiar, but the evidence you can trust has changed.**

# 1. Australian scam picture

The National Anti-Scam Centre's 2025 Targeting Scams Report combines data from Scamwatch, ReportCyber, the Australian Financial Crimes Exchange, IDCARE and ASIC.

For 2025 it reports:

- 481,523 combined scam reports;
- 274,577 reports involving financial loss;
- $2.18 billion in reported losses;
- losses up 7.8% from 2024;
- losses still 29.7% below the 2022 peak of $3.1 billion;
- median reported loss in the combined/Scamwatch discussion fell compared with 2024.

### Evidence type

Official reported data. It captures reports received by participating organisations, **not every scam in Australia**. Under-reporting and differences in datasets matter.

### Important labelling

> **Do not write:** “AI scams cost Australians $2.18 billion in 2025.”
>
> **Accurate:** “Australians reported $2.18 billion in losses to scams in 2025. Authorities separately warn that scammers are increasingly using AI, but the national total is not an AI-only figure.”

# 2. What AI changes in scam operations

## 2.1 Better-written phishing and impersonation messages

Generative tools can quickly produce fluent messages, adjust tone, translate between languages and produce many variants.

### Old cue weakened

“Scam messages have spelling and grammar mistakes.”

That was never a reliable rule; it is weaker now. A perfectly written email can still be malicious.

## 2.2 Personalisation at lower cost

Public information can be summarised into convincing context: employer names, recent events, family relationships, hobbies or professional language.

The risk is not that AI magically knows everything about a target. It can lower the labour required to turn available information into a personalised script.

## 2.3 Voice cloning

Scamwatch warns that scammers can use AI to imitate voices and that only a short sample may be needed by current tools.

### Old cue weakened

“I know my daughter's voice” or “I recognise my boss.”

Voice familiarity is now useful context, but not strong authentication for a money transfer or disclosure of credentials.

## 2.4 Video impersonation

Synthetic video can strengthen social proof during a call or meeting. The most useful real-world case is the 2024 Hong Kong corporate fraud described below.

## 2.5 Fake celebrity endorsements

Australian regulators continue to warn about online investment scams that use public figures' images, generated/altered video or fabricated news-style articles.

## 2.6 Romance / relationship scams and long-con investment fraud

AI can support translation, scripting, profile imagery and round-the-clock message generation. These techniques automate parts of a scam; the psychological mechanics — relationship-building, trust, isolation and escalating requests — are older.

## 2.7 Synthetic identity fraud

AI-generated faces/documents/voice can contribute to identity-fraud workflows. For a beginner chapter, explain the concept without operational detail.

## 2.8 Sextortion / generated sexual imagery

Synthetic intimate images can be used to threaten or extort. Keep this at policy/protection level; do not include exploitative detail.

# 3. Real-world case — deepfake corporate video conference

Hong Kong authorities described a January 2024 case in which an employee at a multinational company was deceived by a pre-recorded video conference in which fraudsters impersonated company personnel with deepfake technology. The employee transferred approximately **HK$200 million**.

Hong Kong authorities also reported another case in May 2024 involving a loss of nearly HK$4 million.

### What this demonstrates

- video presence is not authentication;
- scammers can combine phishing with synthetic media;
- high-value organisations need independent payment controls, not just “recognise the executive”;
- the real vulnerability is procedural: an unusual money request bypassed verification.

### Evidence type

Government public warning describing investigated cases.

**Source:** Government of the Hong Kong SAR, 26 June 2024.  
https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/26/P2024062600192p.htm

### Printed-book adaptation

Generalise it to: “An employee joined what appeared to be a video meeting with senior colleagues and authorised transfers. The apparent colleagues were synthetic.” Preserve the approximate loss only if the author wants a concrete figure and clearly identifies it as a Hong Kong case.

# 4. Australian regulator evidence of AI-assisted investment fraud

ASIC's 2026 public warnings describe scammers using AI to generate fake endorsements, websites and promotional material at scale.

In August 2026 ASIC warned that scammers were using AI to create “vast webs of deception” and reported that impersonations of prominent people in FY2025–26 were associated with **$7.4 million in reported losses** in the subset being discussed. ASIC also reported removing more than 19,400 online scams in the financial year, a broader takedown figure that should not be presented as 19,400 AI scams.

### Evidence type

Regulator operational/reporting data. Useful for current patterns, but care is needed to distinguish:

- all online scam takedowns;
- impersonation reports;
- cases explicitly involving AI;
- total scam losses.

# 6. What still works — and what no longer deserves much trust

| Defence / cue | Status | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verify through a known, separate channel | Strong | Breaks the attacker's control of the conversation. |
| Call the person on a number already saved / independently obtained | Strong | Particularly useful against voice/video impersonation. |
| Slow down when urgency is applied | Strong | Urgency is a classic social-engineering device; AI does not change that. |
| Require normal payment approval procedures | Strong | Organisational process beats recognition of a face/voice. |
| Never send money merely because a message/video looks convincing | Strong | Appearance is not authentication. |
| Family code phrase / safe word | Potentially useful | Helpful if kept private and not discoverable; not a complete defence if the phrase is exposed. |
| Bad grammar means scam | Weak/outdated | AI and professional scam operations can produce excellent language. |
| Familiar voice means genuine | Weak | Voice cloning weakens this cue. |
| Face on video means genuine | Weak | Synthetic/pre-recorded video can be used. |
| Caller ID means genuine | Weak | Caller IDs and sender names can be spoofed. |
| A blue tick / platform verification alone means genuine | Weak | Accounts can be compromised and lookalikes can be convincing. |

# 7. The draft claim: “the core defence hasn't changed”

## Supported part

The strongest protective principles remain familiar:

- stop;
- verify;
- use a separate channel;
- resist urgency;
- follow payment/security process;
- do not disclose credentials or transfer money merely because someone sounds authoritative.

## Qualification needed

AI has weakened cues people previously relied upon:

- fluent language;
- a recognisable voice;
- apparent live video;
- realistic profile photography;
- personalised knowledge.

### Better formulation

> The foundations of scam protection have not changed — slow down and verify independently — but several old shortcuts for deciding what looks genuine no longer deserve much trust.

# 8. Older adults and other disproportionately harmed groups

The chapter should be careful not to say “older people are gullible.” Scam harm varies by scam type and loss amount. Older adults can face disproportionate financial harm in some categories because they may have larger savings, may be targeted with particular narratives, or may be socially isolated; younger cohorts can have high rates of exposure to other scam types.

Use Australian National Anti-Scam Centre demographic breakdowns for any age-specific claim and label them by year.

Other groups can also face disproportionate harm, including:

- First Nations communities;
- culturally and linguistically diverse communities;
- people experiencing loneliness or crisis;
- small businesses with payment-authority workflows;
- employees with access to accounts or sensitive information.

Do not imply a universal victim profile.

# 9. Scams Prevention Framework — Australia

Australia's **Scams Prevention Framework Act 2025** created a cross-sector framework. As at the research date, designated regulated sectors include banks, telecommunications providers and digital platforms, with staged rules and obligations.

The ACCC is the general regulator, with ASIC and ACMA performing sector roles.

### Why this belongs in Chapter 9

It illustrates that scam prevention is not solely an individual responsibility. Banks, telcos, digital platforms and regulators can disrupt payments, accounts, advertisements and communications at scale.

### Recheck before publication

Implementation is staged through 2026–27 and details can change.

**Source:** ACCC, Scams Prevention Framework.  
https://www.accc.gov.au/by-industry/scams/scams-prevention-framework

# 10. Reporting pathways — Australia

The later chapter or companion website could give a compact “what to do now” box.

## If money was sent

1. Contact the bank/payment provider immediately using a trusted channel.
2. Report the scam to Scamwatch / National Anti-Scam Centre pathways.
3. Where relevant, report cybercrime via ReportCyber.
4. Preserve evidence without continuing to engage the scammer.
5. If identity documents/accounts are compromised, use identity-recovery services such as IDCARE and follow government identity-protection guidance.

Useful starting points:

- Scamwatch: https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/
- ReportCyber: https://www.cyber.gov.au/report-and-recover/report
- IDCARE: https://www.idcare.org/

# 12. Claims that need calibration

## “AI has caused scam losses to explode.”

Not established from aggregate scam-loss data alone. Losses fluctuate for many reasons and official datasets rarely identify cleanly what share was AI-enabled.

## “Voice cloning only needs a few seconds.”

Some current tools and authorities say short samples can be sufficient, but required quality/length varies by model, recording conditions, language and desired similarity. Say **“some systems can work from short samples”** rather than inventing one universal threshold.

## “AI scams are impossible to spot.”

Overstated. Synthetic content can be difficult to detect visually or aurally, but scam behaviour still produces procedural warning signs: unusual requests, urgency, payment changes, secrecy, moving platforms, demands for credentials, cryptocurrency/gift cards, and resistance to independent verification.

## “Only gullible people get scammed.”

False and counterproductive. Scam methods exploit normal human trust and contextual pressure. Victim-blaming discourages reporting and learning.

# 13. A realistic generic scam scenario for the book

This can satisfy the plan's requirement for a non-attributed example while being grounded in recurring documented patterns.

> A person receives an urgent call that sounds like a family member. The caller says there has been an accident and asks for money immediately. Rather than relying on the voice, the person ends the call and phones the family member using the number already saved in their contacts. The family member is safe. Whether the original voice was AI-generated or simply an impersonator no longer matters: independent verification breaks the scam.

### Why this is preferable

- does not imply that every family-emergency scam uses AI;
- demonstrates a protective behaviour;
- does not teach fraud technique;
- avoids depending on “spot the deepfake” skills;
- generalises across voice cloning, caller-ID spoofing and human impersonation.

# 14. Editorial opportunities

## Checklist: Before sending money

- Was the request expected?
- Is urgency being used to bypass normal process?
- Have I independently contacted the person/organisation?
- Am I using contact details I already trust?
- Has the bank-account/payment destination changed?
- Am I being asked to keep the payment secret?
- Would I make the same decision if I could not hear/see the person asking?

## Diagram

“AI changes the disguise, not the social-engineering skeleton”:

old scam mechanics -> AI improves language/identity imitation/personalisation -> victim sees more credible signal -> independent verification breaks the chain.
