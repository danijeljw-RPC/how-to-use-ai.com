# Chapter 9 Research — Additional Findings Outside the Core Nine

Research date: 2026-10-01

The approved nine categories should remain the chapter's core. This file records additional risks that are sufficiently documented to matter, then recommends where each belongs so interesting side topics do not overwhelm Chapter 9.

---

# 1. AI companions, relational chatbots and emotional dependence

## Why this is now difficult to ignore

AI assistants/companions can feel relational because they:

- converse in natural language;
- remember details;
- respond continuously;
- personalise tone;
- simulate empathy or intimacy.

Potential harms discussed in current research/regulation include:

- inappropriate content;
- emotional dependence;
- reinforcement of harmful beliefs;
- manipulative engagement design;
- disclosure of sensitive information;
- children treating systems as trusted confidants.

## Strong Australian evidence

In 2026 eSafety published a survey of **1,950 Australian children aged 10–17**, conducted February–March 2026.

Among children who had used an AI assistant or companion:

- 20% reported potentially inappropriate or harmful interactions;
- 32% reported sharing personal or potentially sensitive information;
- 54% reported using assistants/companions for personal or social reasons;
- 20% used them daily or more frequently.

**Source:** eSafety, *Talking to machines: Children's experiences with AI assistants and companions*.  
https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/talking-to-machines-childrens-experiences-with-ai-assistants-and-companions

### Evidence type

Australian survey evidence; self-report.

## eSafety transparency notices

eSafety issued information notices to four AI companion providers in October 2025 and published findings concerning child-safety systems and practices.

**Source:**  
https://www.esafety.gov.au/industry/basic-online-safety-expectations/ai-services/findings-october-2025

## International evidence is mixed

The 2026 International AI Safety Report says evidence about psychological/social effects is emerging and mixed: some studies associate heavy companion use with loneliness/dependence/reduced human interaction, while other studies find positive or no measurable effects. It does not support a blanket claim that companionship chatbots necessarily cause psychological harm.

**Source:**  
https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** brief sidebar or sentence under privacy / “other risks worth watching,” especially for parents and young people.  
**Companion website:** dedicated, updateable page.  
**Later chapters/books:** deeper human-computer interaction treatment if desired.

Do not turn Chapter 9 into a mental-health chapter.

# 2. Harmful medical, mental-health, legal or financial advice

General-purpose models can provide plausible but wrong advice. Earlier chapters already cover hallucination/verification; Chapter 9 could mention the **stakes** increase when advice affects health, safety, legal rights or money.

The 2026 International AI Safety Report documents reliability failures including misleading medical advice and notes that errors can cause physical, psychological, financial, legal or reputational harm.

**Source:**  
https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-extended-summary-policymakers

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** one cross-reference at most, because Chapters 3–4 already teach verification.  
**Companion site:** practical “high-stakes advice” page.  
**Later book:** organisational deployment of AI in regulated domains.

# 3. Children and young people as a cross-cutting risk group

Children are not a separate risk mechanism but can be disproportionately affected by:

- synthetic intimate imagery;
- companions/relational chatbots;
- data/privacy choices;
- misinformation;
- AI-writing detectors;
- educational monitoring/proctoring;
- inappropriate generated content.

## Recommendation

Use “who is disproportionately affected?” callouts within relevant core risks rather than adding a tenth “children” section.

Australian eSafety sources make this highly relevant to the target audience.

# 4. Security risks inside AI assistants and agents

Examples include:

- prompt injection;
- malicious content causing an agent to follow hidden instructions;
- data exfiltration through connected tools;
- insecure extensions/connectors;
- excessive permissions;
- agent actions executed with insufficient confirmation.

These become more important as AI systems can browse, email, edit files, purchase or execute workflows.

## Why it matters

This is different from “criminal uses AI to attack someone.” The AI product itself can become part of the attack surface.

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** at most a brief “emerging risk” reference under privacy/security.  
**Book 4/5:** detailed treatment belongs in technical system-building/security books.  
**Companion website:** current consumer advice about permissions/connectors.

NIST's Generative AI Profile is a useful non-product-specific source:  
https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence

# 5. Cyberattacks assisted by AI

AI can assist with coding, reconnaissance, social engineering and vulnerability work. The same capabilities have legitimate defensive/engineering uses.

The evidence is strongest that AI can improve parts of existing workflows; claims that AI has autonomously transformed cybercrime at scale require careful current evidence.

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** do not add a major cyber section. Mention AI-enhanced phishing under scams.  
**Later technical book:** security implications of coding agents, prompt injection and automated exploitation.  
**Chapter 11:** hype-versus-reality could examine extreme claims.

# 6. Over-reliance, cognitive offloading and deskilling

Earlier chapters already warn about over-reliance. Newer research increasingly examines whether persistent AI assistance changes skill retention and decision-making.

The 2026 International AI Safety Report treats autonomy/skill effects as an emerging area and notes limited long-term evidence.

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** avoid repetition; one cross-reference only.  
**Chapter 6/7:** already appropriate.  
**Companion website:** update as longitudinal evidence improves.

# 7. Concentration of errors / common-model dependency

If many organisations depend on the same model/provider, a single model flaw, outage, harmful behaviour or policy change can propagate widely.

The International AI Safety Report has highlighted that flaws in widely used general-purpose systems can have broad effects.

### Distinct from monopoly

This is an **operational systemic-risk** concept, not simply market concentration. Even a competitive market could develop shared dependency on common underlying infrastructure/models.

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** optional sentence in concentration section.  
**Book 3/4/5:** resilience, vendor risk and multi-provider architecture belong later.

---

# 8. AI-generated child sexual abuse material

This is a serious and documented online-safety/law-enforcement concern.

## Scope rule

Research and writing should remain strictly at:

- law;
- regulatory response;
- prevention;
- platform safety;
- reporting;
- child protection.

No descriptive or operational detail is appropriate.

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** if mentioned, include only within deepfake/child-safety discussion, not a standalone section.  
**Companion website:** link to eSafety/ACCCE resources.

Relevant current eSafety AI-companion findings:  
https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/esafety-report-shows-ai-companions-are-putting-children-at-risk

# 9. Labour conditions behind AI systems

Human workers perform:

- data labelling;
- content moderation;
- evaluation;
- safety review;
- data preparation.

Concerns include pay, working conditions and exposure to distressing material.

This is important to the full social cost of AI but would pull Chapter 9 away from its approved scope unless handled very briefly.

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** probably omit.  
**Book 3 / website:** labour/supply-chain ethics topic.  
**Chapter 11:** possible counterpoint to narratives of “fully automated” AI.

---

# 10. Long-term catastrophic / loss-of-control risk

The research brief explicitly assigns AGI panic/doom debates largely to Chapter 11.

The 2026 International AI Safety Report distinguishes:

- misuse;
- malfunctions;
- systemic risks;

and discusses frontier/catastrophic possibilities alongside documented current harms.

## Recommendation

**Chapter 9:** define the boundary only:

> This chapter focuses on documented or near-term risks ordinary people can understand in current systems. Longer-term claims about AGI, loss of control and catastrophic futures belong in Chapter 11's hype-versus-reality treatment.

Do not let speculative risk crowd out scams, privacy, bias and other documented harms.

# 11. A taxonomy source that supports broader thinking

The MIT AI Risk Repository is a living meta-review/taxonomy combining risks from many frameworks. As of the current site it contains more than 1,700 coded risks from 74 frameworks and groups them into domains such as:

- discrimination & toxicity;
- privacy & security;
- misinformation;
- malicious actors/misuse;
- human-computer interaction;
- socioeconomic/environmental;
- AI system safety/failures/limitations.

It also classifies causal dimensions such as whether harms arise from humans or AI, intentionally/unintentionally, pre/post-deployment.

**Source:**  
https://airisk.mit.edu/risks

### Why useful for this project

It confirms that the plan's nine categories are **a curated beginner subset**, not a comprehensive taxonomy. That is appropriate for Book 1.

### Why not copy its structure

The repository is designed for researchers/policymakers, not beginner chapter pacing. Its taxonomy is too broad and technical to import wholesale.

# 12. AI Incident Database

The AI Incident Database catalogues reported incidents involving AI and provides classifications/taxonomies.

**Source:**  
https://incidentdatabase.ai/

### Usefulness

- source leads for documented harms;
- seeing recurring incident categories;
- avoiding invented examples.

### Limitations

Incident databases are not prevalence databases. Inclusion depends on reporting, curation and definition; counts should not be treated as population incidence rates.

## Recommendation

Use as a research lead / companion-site resource, not a headline statistic.

---

# 13. International AI Safety Report 2026 as a calibration source

The 2026 report, led by Yoshua Bengio and authored by a large international group, organises general-purpose AI risks into:

- misuse;
- malfunctions;
- systemic risks.

It repeatedly distinguishes observed real-world harm from emerging/uncertain future risk and notes limited evidence in some areas.

**Source:**  
https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026

### Recommendation

Use as a high-level synthesis/check against selective sourcing, not as the only authority.
