# Chapter 9 Research — Regulation and Individual Protection

Research date: 2026-10-01

## Executive research takeaways

The plan's framing — “informed users and responsible regulation” — is useful but should be made more precise.

- Some risks are meaningfully reduced by individual behaviour: scam verification, careful disclosure of private information, source checking.
- Some risks are only partly controllable by individuals: discriminatory automated decisions, surveillance, market concentration, data-centre infrastructure, platform design.
- Australia does **not** have “no laws for AI.” Existing privacy, consumer, criminal, online-safety, anti-discrimination, financial-services and other laws already apply, and AI-specific governance is developing alongside them.
- “Responsible regulation” is not one settled recipe. Serious disagreements concern whether to regulate the technology itself or specific high-risk uses, whether existing law is enough, how to avoid entrenching incumbents, and how duties should be divided among developers, deployers, platforms and users.

# 1. A better model than “personal responsibility versus regulation”

Use three layers:

## Layer 1 — Individual safeguards

Examples:

- verify an unexpected money request;
- do not upload unnecessary sensitive information;
- check the source of a dramatic clip;
- challenge an automated high-stakes decision where a review path exists.

## Layer 2 — Organisational safeguards

Examples:

- payment authorisation controls;
- privacy impact assessments;
- human review / appeal processes;
- bias testing;
- access controls;
- incident reporting;
- appropriate procurement.

## Layer 3 — Collective / structural safeguards

Examples:

- privacy law;
- anti-discrimination law;
- consumer law;
- online-safety rules;
- competition policy;
- sector regulation;
- criminal law;
- infrastructure/energy planning.

### Why this is better

It avoids teaching readers that every harm can be prevented by “being careful.”

# 3. Voluntary AI governance guidance in Australia

Australia's 2024 Voluntary AI Safety Standard evolved in October 2025 into the **Guidance for AI Adoption**, which the government describes as a simplified set of six practices for responsible AI governance.

### Why the distinction matters

The original “10 guardrails” remain useful history, but the chapter should not present them as the newest Australian guidance without qualification.

**Source:** Department of Industry, Science and Resources.  
https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/voluntary-ai-safety-standard

### Editorial point

An AI-specific Act is not required for every AI-caused harm to be regulated. Existing law can regulate conduct regardless of whether AI was used.

# 5. Scams Prevention Framework — structural response

Australia's Scams Prevention Framework is being introduced in stages.

As at 2026-10-01:

- regulated services include designated banking, telecommunications and digital-platform services;
- entities in those sectors had to be AFCA members from **1 September 2026**;
- most obligations are scheduled to apply from **31 March 2027**;
- the framework requires regulated entities to take action to prevent, detect, disrupt, report and respond to scams;
- ACCC is general regulator and digital-platform sector regulator;
- ASIC regulates banks under the framework;
- ACMA regulates telecommunications providers;
- codes/rules are still being developed/implemented.

### Evidence type

Current regulatory implementation status.

**Source:** ACCC, Scams Prevention Framework.  
https://www.accc.gov.au/about-us/scams-prevention-framework

**Recheck before publication.** This is a high-priority recheck because implementation is ongoing.

# 6. Privacy / automated decision transparency — structural response

From **10 December 2026**, specified APP entities must include information in their privacy policies where a computer program makes or substantially/directly informs a decision that could significantly affect an individual's rights or interests and uses personal information.

At the research date (1 October 2026), this commencement date is still future.

# 7. Deepfake / image-based abuse response in Australia

Australia now has:

- Commonwealth criminal-law provisions covering specified non-consensual transmission of sexual material, including deepfake-related reforms from 2024;
- eSafety image-based abuse reporting/removal mechanisms;
- state/territory offences and remedies that vary by jurisdiction;
- online-safety powers and industry obligations.

### Reader-facing reporting route

For image-based abuse, eSafety says people can report relevant material directly; procedures differ where blackmail/sextortion is involved and depending on whether the affected person is under 18.

# 8. EU AI Act — useful comparison, not Australian law

The EU AI Act uses a risk-based regulatory architecture with:

- prohibited practices;
- transparency duties for some AI-generated/manipulated content and interactive systems;
- rules for general-purpose AI;
- high-risk system obligations;
- staged commencement/enforcement.

As of 2026-10-01, the official EU AI Act Service Desk states:

- general provisions/prohibitions began applying in February 2025;
- GPAI rules began applying in August 2025;
- the majority of rules and relevant enforcement started **2 August 2026**;
- transparency rules apply from that date, with a transition for certain pre-existing systems;
- new prohibitions relating to generating/manipulating non-consensual sexual deepfakes and CSAM are scheduled for **2 December 2026**;
- Annex III high-risk system rules are scheduled later under the amended timeline.

**Source:** EU AI Act Service Desk.  
https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/eu-ai-act-implementation-timeline

Article 5 prohibited-practices text:  
https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5

**Recheck before publication.** EU implementation dates have changed and can change again.

# 9. United States — useful comparison at principles level

The US remains a mixture of:

- federal agency enforcement/guidance;

# 11. International principles

The OECD AI Principles were adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024. They promote AI that is innovative and trustworthy and include values and policy recommendations concerning human rights, transparency, robustness/accountability and enabling policy environments.

### Why useful

They illustrate that “responsible AI” is not purely a company slogan; there are intergovernmental frameworks. But principles are not the same thing as enforceable law.

**Source:**  
https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/ai-principles.html

# 12. Serious disagreements about regulation

A balanced research package should preserve these positions without choosing a winner.

## Position A — AI-specific rules are necessary

Reasoning:

- general laws can be hard to apply to opaque, fast-changing systems;
- some harms occur before traditional enforcement catches up;
- high-risk uses need testing, documentation and accountability before deployment;
- synthetic content/general-purpose models create cross-sector problems.

## Position B — regulate outcomes/uses, not “AI” broadly

Reasoning:

- the same model can be low-risk in one use and high-risk in another;
- technology-wide rules can become obsolete;
- focus should be on actual harmful conduct and high-stakes decisions.

## Position C — existing law already covers much of the harm

Reasoning:

- fraud is fraud even when AI writes the email;
- discrimination remains discrimination when an algorithm is used;
- privacy law can apply to AI data practices;
- consumer law already prohibits misleading conduct.

Qualification: existing legal coverage does not prove enforcement, remedies or institutional capability are sufficient.

## Position D — badly designed regulation can entrench incumbents

Reasoning:

- compliance costs may be easier for large firms to absorb;
- licensing/compute requirements can raise entry barriers;
- regulatory complexity can reduce open-source/small-company competition.

## Position E — providers/deployers should bear more responsibility

Reasoning:

- users cannot inspect model training, data or infrastructure;
- developers/deployers control design, testing and deployment safeguards.

## Position F — responsibility should follow control and context

Reasoning:

- model provider may not know downstream use;
- deployer chooses the high-stakes context;
- platform controls distribution;
- user can misuse a legitimate tool.

### Editorial takeaway

“Responsible regulation” should be written as **a need for enforceable, proportionate safeguards matched to risk and responsibility**, not as though one regulatory model has been settled globally.

# 13. Protective behaviours by risk category

| Risk | Individual action that can help | What individuals cannot solve alone |
|---|---|---|
| Misinformation | verify source/context; lateral reading; pause before sharing | platform incentives, organised influence operations, provenance infrastructure |
| Deepfakes | verify source; use separate channel for high-stakes requests | non-consensual content ecosystems, platform removal, criminal enforcement |
| Scams | slow down; callback on known channel; use payment controls | scam ads/accounts, mule networks, telecom spoofing, cross-border enforcement |
| Bias | request reasons/review; document decision | system design, training data, institutional discrimination, audit access |
| Surveillance | minimise unnecessary data; use available privacy controls | public/workplace systems deployed by organisations/government |
| Privacy | reduce sensitive input; review settings; secure accounts | provider data architecture, breaches, legal powers, data-broker ecosystem |
| Copyright | use normal IP diligence for consequential commercial uses | training legality, collective licensing, court interpretations |
| Environment | choose efficient use where practical; avoid waste | grid build-out, data-centre siting, provider efficiency/disclosure |
| Concentration | use alternatives/interoperable/open tools where practical | chip/cloud market structure, mergers, defaults, competition enforcement |

# 14. A consolidated “what readers can actually do” checklist

## Verify before acting

- unexpected request for money or credentials -> independently verify;
- dramatic clip -> find source and corroboration;
- AI-generated factual claim that matters -> check a reliable source.

## Limit unnecessary exposure

- do not paste sensitive information merely because the chatbot feels conversational;
- remove identifying detail when it is not needed;
- consider whether information belongs to someone else.

## Challenge consequential automation

- ask whether automation materially informed a decision where appropriate;
- request a review/reason through available channels;
- preserve records.

## Report harm

Australian starting points:

- scams: https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/
- cybercrime: https://www.cyber.gov.au/report-and-recover/report
- image-based abuse / serious online abuse: https://www.esafety.gov.au/report
- privacy complaint: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-complaints
- discrimination/human-rights complaints: https://humanrights.gov.au/complaints

## Know when the problem is structural

The correct personal response to monopoly, discriminatory deployment or data-centre infrastructure is not “be more careful.” Those problems require organisational and collective action.

# 15. Do awareness and education help?

Research on media literacy, phishing education and misinformation interventions generally suggests that well-designed training can improve specific skills, but effects vary and can decay. The chapter should avoid implying that one awareness lesson makes someone scam-proof.

For this book, the strongest educational design is likely to be **procedural habits**, because they remain useful as the content changes:

- separate-channel verification;
- lateral/source checking;
- slowing down under urgency;
- data-minimisation questions;
- review/challenge processes.

These are more durable than memorising a list of visual “AI tells.”

# 16. Myth vs reality candidates for Chapter 9

## Myth: “There are no laws about AI.”

**Reality:** Existing law already applies to many AI-related harms, while AI-specific rules and guidance are still developing. Coverage and enforcement gaps remain.

## Myth: “Regulation will prevent AI harms.”

**Reality:** Regulation can set duties, remedies and enforcement powers, but cannot eliminate fraud, bias or misuse. Design, organisational controls and individual behaviour also matter.

## Myth: “People just need to be more careful.”

**Reality:** Personal habits help with some risks. Others are structural and are controlled by organisations, infrastructure owners and governments.

## Myth: “The EU AI Act is the law everywhere.”

**Reality:** It is EU law with some extraterritorial implications, not the Australian legal framework.
