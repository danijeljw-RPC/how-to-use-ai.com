# Chapter 9 Research Package — The Problems Nobody Should Ignore

**Research date:** 1 October 2026  
**Book:** *How To Use AI.com* — Book 1  
**Chapter:** 9 — The Problems Nobody Should Ignore  
**Status:** Research package only. This package does **not** write Chapter 9.

## Purpose

This package was produced against the approved Chapter 9 plan and the current Chapter 9 template/draft. The plan remains authoritative for intended scope, structure, reader state, and learning outcomes. The existing chapter draft is treated as a starting template to test, not as an authoritative factual source.

The research is deliberately broader and deeper than the final chapter should be. Its purpose is to give the later chapter-writing system genuine editorial choices: stronger evidence, better examples, counterarguments, proportion, Australian context, practical protective behaviours, and clearly identified uncertainties.

## Core editorial finding

The plan's central stance — **AI can be useful and genuinely risky at the same time** — is broadly compatible with the evidence. The main qualification is that the phrase “informed users and responsible regulation” can imply that individual knowledge is enough. It is not. Some risks can be reduced materially by individual behaviour (especially scams and careless disclosure of data). Others arise mainly from institutional decisions, market structure, infrastructure, or systems deployed on people who may have no meaningful ability to opt out.

A stronger beginner-level framing is therefore:

> Some AI risks can be reduced by what you do. Others depend on what organisations, platforms, regulators and governments do. Being informed helps you tell the difference.

This does not replace the approved takeaway; it is a research-backed qualification for the eventual writer to consider.

## Evidence discipline

Numerical claims in this package are labelled where practical as one of:

- **Observed historical data**
- **Official reported data** — often incomplete because reporting systems undercount incidents
- **Survey evidence**
- **Independent academic finding**
- **Meta-analysis/systematic review**
- **Company operational data**
- **Estimate/modelled estimate**
- **Forecast/projection**
- **Scenario model**
- **Regulator enforcement data**
- **Investigative/secondary reporting**

Do not convert a reported-loss figure into “total losses”, a forecast into a historical fact, a laboratory capability result into measured real-world prevalence, or a company disclosure into independently verified evidence.

## High-priority findings for the later writer

1. **Deepfake detection advice needs updating.** A 2024 meta-analysis of 56 papers and 86,155 participants found overall human detection of high-quality deepfakes was not significantly above chance. The chapter should emphasise source checking, lateral verification and separate-channel verification rather than “look for odd hands/blinking”.
2. **Deepfakes are not mainly a politics story.** Image-based sexual abuse, scams, impersonation and harassment are major present-day harms. Australian eSafety enforcement and reporting make this particularly relevant to a general reader.
3. **Scam statistics must not be mislabelled as AI-scam statistics.** Australia recorded $2.18 billion in reported scam losses in 2025 across scam types. Authorities document increasing AI use, but there is no equivalent reliable national total isolating losses caused specifically by AI.
4. **Bias is not simply “biased training data”.** It can enter through target choice, proxy variables, measurement, labels, deployment, feedback loops and human over- or under-trust. The 2019 healthcare cost-proxy example is unusually good for beginners.
5. **Surveillance and privacy are related but distinct.** AI changes the economics and scale of identifying, searching and profiling people. Australian Clearview AI and Bunnings matters provide concrete local examples.
6. **“There are no laws about AI” is wrong.** Australia uses technology-neutral privacy, consumer, discrimination, online-safety, criminal, sectoral and other laws, while adding AI-specific institutions/guidance. Coverage and remedies remain uneven.
7. **Copyright remains genuinely unsettled.** Some cases now have rulings or settlements, but they are fact-specific. The later chapter should not tell readers that training is simply legal or illegal, nor assume tool terms eliminate third-party risk.
8. **Environmental numbers need scope labels.** Per-prompt measurements can be small for efficient text inference, while aggregate data-centre demand is materially affecting electricity planning. Image/video generation, long reasoning and agentic workflows can differ substantially from a short text prompt.
9. **Concentration and competition can coexist.** Frontier development depends on concentrated chips, clouds, capital and distribution, while model competition, open-weight systems and falling serving costs can simultaneously reduce some barriers. Both facts matter.
10. **The draft's individual-vs-society risk diagram is too binary.** Most risks cross both levels. Alternative taxonomies are supplied in `01-core-research.md` and `11-editorial-opportunities.md`.

## Major cautions

### Time-sensitive law and policy

Legal status, regulatory proposals, privacy settings, platform policies, scam figures, litigation, AI market structure and energy forecasts can change quickly. Items marked **Recheck before publication** should be verified close to the manuscript lock date.

### Australian-first does not mean Australian-only

Australia is given primary attention because it is the author's jurisdiction and likely initial audience. US, EU and UK material is used when it explains a major global platform, an important court dispute, a useful regulatory contrast, or evidence not available locally.

### “AI-enabled” is not the same as “caused by AI”

Many harms in this chapter predate generative AI: fraud, misinformation, discrimination, surveillance, privacy violations, market concentration and environmental externalities. In many cases AI changes cost, speed, scale, accessibility or persuasiveness rather than inventing a new harm.

### Old automated systems are not automatically “AI”

Robodebt and some other algorithmic-decision cases are relevant governance lessons, but should not be relabelled as modern AI incidents. The later writer should use them, if at all, as examples of why automated decisions require legality, review, transparency and human accountability.

### Source limitations

Company threat reports are useful for how the provider observes misuse of its own systems but are not independent measurements of the whole ecosystem. Advocacy sources can document harms and affected communities but may use advocacy framing. Regulator reports can be authoritative about enforcement while still relying on under-reported complaint data. Academic studies can be rigorous but narrow or become obsolete as models change.
