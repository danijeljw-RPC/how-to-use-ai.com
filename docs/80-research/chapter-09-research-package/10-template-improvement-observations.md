# 1. Introduction — Taking Risk Seriously Without Panic

## Observation 1.1 — framing is strong but needs one structural qualification

**Current topic:** AI has genuine risks requiring informed users and responsible regulation.  
**Weak/incomplete:** The wording can imply that the two relevant actors are individuals and regulators. Many mitigations sit between them: employers, banks, platforms, schools, model providers and other organisations.  
**Research suggests:** Distinguish individual, organisational and structural safeguards.  
**Evidence:** Australia's Scams Prevention Framework, OAIC governance guidance, NIST AI RMF, MIT mitigation taxonomy.  
**Affects:** balance, usefulness, conceptual accuracy.  
**Possible action for later writer:** Keep the approved Key Idea, but make clear elsewhere that personal vigilance cannot solve structural harms.

## Observation 1.2 — define the chapter's evidence test

**Weak/incomplete:** The chapter currently jumps straight into individual risk categories.  
**Research suggests:** A short early rule — “possible, happening, common, severe” are different questions — would unify the chapter and prevent sensationalism.  
**Affects:** coherence, proportion, reader literacy.  
**Possible diagram/table:** capability vs prevalence vs impact.

# 2. Misinformation

## Observation 2.1 — production scale is stated more confidently than impact

**Current claim:** AI can produce convincing false text at a speed/scale that used to require a team.  
**Weak/incomplete:** Directionally plausible, but “production” is only one stage. Distribution, reach, belief and behavioural effect are separate bottlenecks.  
**Research suggests:** Add calibration from CETaS 2024 election research and AI company threat reports.  
**Strong evidence:** 2024 UK/EU/French elections documented AI misuse but no evidence of meaningful result impact in that study.  
**Affects:** proportion, factual precision, balance.

## Observation 2.2 — distinguish misinformation/disinformation and hallucination

**Weak/incomplete:** The section risks blending deliberate false-content generation with accidental model error already covered in Chapter 4.  
**Research suggests:** One sentence distinguishing misinformation, disinformation and model hallucination is enough.  
**Affects:** clarity, chapter boundaries.

## Observation 2.3 — fake reviews/content farms are useful but need evidence role

**Weak/incomplete:** Current examples are generic.  
**Research suggests:** FTC fake-review rule and NewsGuard content-farm tracking can make the phenomenon concrete.  
**Caveat:** Content-farm count is not audience-impact evidence.  
**Affects:** depth, usefulness.

# 3. Deepfakes

## Observation 3.1 — the current section underweights non-consensual sexualised imagery

**Weak/incomplete:** Current text focuses generally on fake quotes/photos/video.  
**Research suggests:** Australian eSafety reporting/enforcement shows synthetic intimate imagery, including school-age cases, is a major current harm deserving explicit treatment.  
**Strong evidence:** eSafety school reports, Federal Court penalty, “nudify” enforcement, Commonwealth 2024 legislation.  
**Affects:** factual completeness, audience relevance, harm calibration.

## Observation 3.2 — “look/sound surprising, verify elsewhere” is directionally good; strengthen it

**Current Watch Out:** be sceptical of dramatic/emotional content and check independent reporting.  
**Research suggests:** Keep this. Add that unaided visual inspection is not reliable enough to be the main defence.  
**Evidence:** 2024 meta-analysis of 56 papers / 86,155 participants.  
**Affects:** usefulness and current capability accuracy.

## Observation 3.3 — provenance is a useful optional nuance

**Research suggests:** If Chapter 8 already covered C2PA, use only the practical limitation: provenance can help establish origin/edit history, not truth.  
**Affects:** balance, avoiding magical “authenticity detector” framing.

## Observation 3.4 — liar's dividend is missing

**Research suggests:** Briefly note that widespread awareness of fakes can let genuine evidence be dismissed as synthetic.  
**Affects:** depth and societal-risk understanding.  
**Scope:** one paragraph or callout, not a major subsection.

# 4. Scams

## Observation 4.1 — “the core defence hasn't changed” needs refinement

**Current claim:** Technology improved; protection rule did not need to.  
**Supported:** Separate-channel verification, slowing down and refusing unexpected money requests remain foundational.  
**Missing:** Old authenticity cues have weakened: grammar, known voice, familiar face on video.  
**Research suggests replacement concept:** principles are familiar, trusted evidence has changed.  
**Affects:** accuracy, usefulness.

## Observation 4.2 — use one verified number, carefully labelled

**Opportunity:** The draft contains no figures because of a plan caution about unverified statistics.  
**Research suggests:** A verified Australian statistic would strengthen seriousness and local relevance.  
**Candidate:** $2.18b reported scam losses in 2025 across combined Australian reporting sources.  
**Critical label:** all reported scams, **not AI-enabled scam losses**.  
**Affects:** credibility, depth, evidence literacy.

## Observation 4.3 — generic scenario can be grounded in real cases

**Research suggests:** Build the plan's non-attributed scenario from recurring family-emergency/voice-clone patterns: urgent call -> end call -> ring known number -> verify.  
**Affects:** practical utility.

## Observation 4.4 — include structural protection

**Missing:** Scams are not only an individual problem.  
**Research suggests:** Briefly mention Australian Scams Prevention Framework and roles of banks/telcos/platforms.  
**Affects:** balance; prevents victim responsibility framing.

# 5. Bias

## Observation 5.1 — training-data explanation is too narrow

**Current claim:** biased patterns in training data can be reproduced/amplified.  
**Correct but incomplete:** target choice, proxies, measurement, labels, thresholds, deployment and feedback loops also create bias.  
**Best replacement example:** 2019 healthcare cost-proxy study.  
**Affects:** factual accuracy and depth.

## Observation 5.2 — high-stakes examples need specificity

**Current Watch Out:** hiring, lending, insurance.  
**Research suggests:** Add one concrete, rigorously sourced case instead of naming sectors only. Healthcare proxy or AI-writing detector would be beginner-friendly.  
**Affects:** clarity, usefulness.

## Observation 5.3 — “neutral/objective” idea can be made stronger

**Research suggests:** Explain that a numeric score can look objective while embedding subjective decisions about what to measure and optimise.  
**Affects:** conceptual understanding.

## Observation 5.4 — fairness is not one metric

**Missing:** “remove bias” sounds technically straightforward.  
**Research suggests:** One short explanation that reasonable fairness goals can conflict. No mathematics required.  
**Affects:** avoiding oversimplification.

## Observation 5.5 — Robodebt is potentially useful but must not be called AI

**Research suggests:** If used, label as automated-government decision-making, not generative AI. Use it for governance/review principles only.  
**Affects:** factual accuracy.

# 6. Surveillance

## Observation 6.1 — “dramatically cheaper and easier” needs a deployed example

**Current claim:** AI makes surveillance dramatically cheaper/easier.  
**Research supports mechanism:** automation makes video/audio/data search scalable.  
**Weakness:** abstract without evidence.  
**Stronger evidence:** Clearview AI searchable face database; Bunnings retail FRT; algorithmic workplace monitoring research.  
**Affects:** depth, evidence.

## Observation 6.2 — separate four questions

**Research suggests:** possible vs deployed vs legal vs publicly accepted.  
**Why:** Current section could lead readers to infer every technically possible surveillance use is widespread.  
**Affects:** proportion.

## Observation 6.3 — include a legitimate-purpose trade-off

**Best example:** Bunnings: repeat theft/violence and staff safety were real concerns; privacy proportionality/notice remained legally significant.  
**Affects:** balance, avoids caricature.

## Observation 6.4 — emotion recognition can demonstrate weak scientific foundations

**Opportunity:** A compact example of technology marketed as inference exceeding what the evidence supports.  
**Evidence:** Barrett et al. 2019 scientific review; EU AI Act restrictions.  
**Scope:** optional; may be companion website if chapter is crowded.

# 10. Monopolisation by Large Technology Companies

## Observation 10.1 — title/wording implies a settled conclusion

**Current section:** “Monopolisation by Large Technology Companies.”  
**Plan requires this topic**, so section title may need to remain.  
**Research suggests body text should distinguish:** market concentration, vertical integration, infrastructure bottlenecks, partnerships and distribution power.  
**Affects:** balance, economic precision.

## Observation 10.2 — “relatively little competition” is too broad

**Counterevidence:** rapid model-provider competition, open-weight systems, narrowing US-China performance gap, falling prices in some model classes.  
**Concentration still real:** chips/fabrication/cloud/frontier capital/distribution.  
**Better concept:** competition can be intense at one layer while infrastructure remains concentrated.  
**Affects:** factual balance.

## Observation 10.3 — “values/restrictions built in” is legitimate but should avoid insinuation

**Research suggests:** Frame as governance/default decisions made by providers — moderation, access, pricing, privacy, features — that become consequential when downstream products depend on a few providers.  
**Affects:** tone, credibility.

## Observation 10.4 — Australian competition evidence is available

**Use:** ACCC 2025 Digital Platform Services Inquiry final report and AI industry snapshot.  
**Affects:** local relevance and authority.

# 11. Existing diagram

## Current diagram concept

“Risks primarily to individuals” versus “risks primarily to society.”

## Problem

Most categories cross both:

- deepfakes: direct victim + information trust;
- scams: direct victim + payment/platform systems;
- bias: individual decision + structural inequality;
- privacy: individual disclosure + population surveillance;
- environment: collective + local communities;
- concentration: society/economy + user choice/prices.

## Better alternatives for later writer

### Alternative A — mechanism

- **people using AI to cause harm**: misinformation, deepfakes, scams, some surveillance;
- **AI/automation making mistakes or unfair decisions**: bias, harmful outputs, privacy leakage;
- **how AI is built/deployed/owned**: privacy, copyright, environment, concentration.

### Alternative B — control

- **risks you can personally reduce**;
- **risks organisations must manage**;
- **risks requiring collective/regulatory action**.

### Alternative C — evidence status

- **documented present harms**;
- **demonstrated capabilities with uncertain prevalence**;
- **system-level/projected effects**.

### Recommendation

Do not pick during research. Alternative B is probably easiest for a beginner and aligns with the chapter's practical purpose.

# 13. Myth vs Reality section

The current closing paragraph performs a Myth vs Reality function but is prose-heavy.

Research suggests a clearer set of paired claims:

- **Myth:** AI risks are all hypothetical.  
  **Reality:** fraud, image-based abuse, discriminatory automated outcomes and privacy enforcement cases are documented.
- **Myth:** AI has already destroyed elections/truth.  
  **Reality:** AI-enabled political manipulation is real, but measured impact has sometimes been much smaller than feared.
- **Myth:** careful users can avoid every harm.  
  **Reality:** some risks are structural.
- **Myth:** there are no AI laws.  
  **Reality:** existing laws already apply and AI-specific frameworks are developing.
- **Myth:** one detector can tell you what is AI-generated.  
  **Reality:** detection is probabilistic and fragile.

This would more directly satisfy the plan's denial-versus-panic requirement.
