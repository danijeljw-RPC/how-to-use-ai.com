# Chapter 9 Research — Strong Real-World Examples

Research date: 2026-10-01

This file deliberately selects **strong, teachable examples**, not the most shocking incidents available. Each example should help the eventual writer explain mechanism, proportion, protection, legal response or uncertainty.

---

## Example 1 — 2024 UK/EU/French elections: less measurable AI impact than feared

**Risk category:** Misinformation / deepfakes  
**What happened:** CETaS at the Alan Turing Institute reviewed viral AI-enabled disinformation during the 2024 UK general election and European/French election periods. It identified 16 confirmed viral UK cases and 11 across EU/French elections and found no evidence in its analysis that AI materially affected the election results.  
**Source:** Sam Stockwell, CETaS, 19 September 2024.  
https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/publications/ai-enabled-influence-operations-threat-analysis-2024-uk-and-european-elections  
**What it demonstrates:** Capability is not the same as real-world impact; limited outcome impact can coexist with trust/hate/confusion harms.  
**Why useful:** Excellent antidote to both panic and dismissal.  
**Chapter fit:** Misinformation; Myth vs Reality.  
**Printed book:** Strong.  
**Website:** Strong; link to methodology.  
**Anonymise/generalise:** No; institutional research.  
**Limitations:** Focused on sufficiently viral/documented cases, not a complete census of all content.  
**Dates quickly:** Medium; still useful historically.

## Example 2 — AI company disruptions of influence operations

**Risk category:** Misinformation / malicious misuse  
**What happened:** OpenAI has published repeated threat reports describing networks using its tools for drafting, translation, research and other influence-operation tasks. Its October 2025 report said more than 40 networks had been disrupted since reporting began in 2024 and emphasised that actors commonly add AI to existing playbooks.  
**Source:** OpenAI, October 2025.  
https://openai.com/global-affairs/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-october-2025/  
**What it demonstrates:** AI-enabled misuse is real, but often augments existing operations rather than creating entirely new distribution capacity.  
**Why useful:** Shows company threat intelligence without treating company claims as population-wide measurement.  
**Chapter fit:** Misinformation.  
**Printed book:** Good if source caveat is explicit.  
**Website:** Strong; current threat reports can be updated.  
**Limitations:** Provider sees only activity in its systems; commercial/self-reporting incentives.  
**Dates quickly:** High.

## Example 3 — AI-generated fake reviews become explicitly regulated in the US

**Risk category:** Misinformation / consumer deception  
**What happened:** The US FTC finalised a rule banning specified fake reviews/testimonials, explicitly encompassing AI-generated fake reviews.  
**Source:** Federal Trade Commission, 14 August 2024.  
https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials  
**What it demonstrates:** AI amplifies an old consumer-deception problem; regulators can apply/update rules around the conduct.  
**Why useful:** Familiar to ordinary readers who rely on online reviews.  
**Chapter fit:** Misinformation; regulation.  
**Printed book:** Strong.  
**Website:** Strong.  
**Limitations:** US law, not Australian law. Australian Consumer Law also prohibits misleading/fake review practices, but use an Australian source for local law.  
**Dates quickly:** Low/medium.

## Example 4 — Hong Kong deepfake video-conference fraud

**Risk category:** Deepfakes / scams  
**What happened:** Hong Kong authorities described a January 2024 corporate fraud in which a worker attended a pre-recorded video conference featuring deepfake impersonations of company personnel and transferred about HK$200 million. A later 2024 case involved nearly HK$4 million.  
**Source:** Government of the Hong Kong SAR, 26 June 2024.  
https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/26/P2024062600192p.htm  
**What it demonstrates:** Seeing familiar colleagues on video is no longer adequate high-stakes authentication.  
**Why useful:** Concrete, memorable, and leads directly to a practical defence: independent payment verification.  
**Chapter fit:** Deepfakes / scams.  
**Printed book:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Good.  
**Anonymise/generalise:** Generalise private employee identity; organisation need not be named.  
**Limitations:** High-value business fraud is not representative of everyday scam frequency.  
**Dates quickly:** Low.

**Chapter fit:** Deepfakes; legal response.  
**Printed book:** Strong, handled without graphic detail.  
**Website:** Strong with support/reporting links.

**Dates quickly:** Low.

## Example 7 — Human deepfake detection is unreliable in aggregate

**Risk category:** Deepfakes / protective behaviour  
**What happened:** A 2024 meta-analysis synthesised 56 papers and 86,155 participants. Overall detection sensitivity was not significantly above chance; overall accuracy estimates varied by analysis/modality and interventions could improve performance.  
**Source:** *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* 16 (2024), 100538.  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2024.100538  
**What it demonstrates:** “Just look closely” is a weak core safety strategy.  
**Why useful:** Changes reader behaviour toward source verification rather than visual confidence.  
**Chapter fit:** Deepfakes; Watch Out.  
**Printed book:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Could become a verification exercise.  
**Limitations:** High heterogeneity; deepfake technology and detection interventions evolve.  
**Dates quickly:** Medium.

## Example 8 — Australian scam losses: a number that must not be mislabelled “AI scams”

**Risk category:** Scams / evidence calibration  
**What happened:** The National Anti-Scam Centre reported 481,523 combined scam reports in 2025; 274,577 involved financial losses totalling $2.18 billion.  
**Source:** ACCC / National Anti-Scam Centre, 30 March 2026.  
https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/continued-action-critical-to-combat-fraud-as-annual-scam-losses-exceed-2-billion  
**What it demonstrates:** Scam harm is large; aggregate scam statistics do not identify the AI-enabled share.  
**Why useful:** Teaches readers how headline numbers get distorted.  
**Chapter fit:** Scams; evidence box.  
**Printed book:** Strong if explicitly labelled “all reported scams.”  
**Website:** Update annually.  
**Limitations:** Reported losses undercount actual harm and combine several reporting bodies; not AI-specific.  
**Dates quickly:** High.

## Example 10 — Healthcare algorithm: the wrong proxy creates racial bias

**Risk category:** Bias  
**What happened:** A 2019 *Science* study found a widely used US health-management algorithm under-identified Black patients for extra care because it predicted healthcare cost as a proxy for health need. Correcting the disparity would have raised the Black share selected at the threshold from 17.7% to 46.5%.  
**Source:** Obermeyer et al., *Science* (2019).  
https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax2342  
**What it demonstrates:** Bias is not only “bad training data”; target/proxy choice matters.  
**Why useful:** Perhaps the strongest beginner example in the entire bias section.  
**Chapter fit:** Bias.  
**Printed book:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Good explanatory diagram.  
**Limitations:** US healthcare and a specific algorithm; do not generalise exact figures to other systems.  
**Dates quickly:** Low as a historical case.

## Example 11 — Gender Shades, paired with current NIST testing

**Risk category:** Bias / facial analysis  
**What happened:** The 2018 Gender Shades study exposed large intersectional performance disparities in commercial gender classification; NIST continues current testing of demographic effects in face-recognition systems.  
**Sources:**  
https://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/buolamwini18a.html  
https://pages.nist.gov/frvt/html/frvt_demographics.html  
**What it demonstrates:** Overall accuracy can hide subgroup performance; audits changed industry discussion.  
**Why useful:** Pairing historical and current sources avoids treating 2018 error rates as 2026 performance.  
**Chapter fit:** Bias / surveillance.  
**Printed book:** Strong.  
**Website:** Strong, link to current NIST results.  
**Limitations:** Gender classification is not identical to identity matching; old commercial systems have changed.  
**Dates quickly:** NIST current data: high; historical study: low.

## Example 12 — AI-writing detectors and non-native English writers

**Risk category:** Bias / education  
**What happened:** A 2023 study of seven detectors found an average 61.3% false-positive rate on a set of 91 TOEFL essays written by non-native English writers, while results differed sharply for the comparison native-English dataset.  
**Source:** Liang et al., *Patterns* (2023).  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779  
**What it demonstrates:** A detector can mistake linguistic predictability for AI authorship and create unfair accusations.  
**Why useful:** Immediate student relevance.  
**Chapter fit:** Bias.  
**Printed book:** Excellent with model-age caveat.  
**Website:** Current detector test/research updates.  
**Limitations:** 2023 detectors; exact performance is not a universal current rate.  
**Dates quickly:** Medium/high.

## Example 15 — Bunnings facial recognition: real safety goal, real privacy constraints

**Risk category:** Surveillance / privacy  
**What happened:** Bunnings used FRT in more than 60 stores to address repeat theft/violence. In February 2026 the ART affirmed Privacy Commissioner findings on APP 1 and APP 5 and examined proportionality, alternatives and consent exceptions. OAIC updated FRT guidance in July 2026.  
**Sources:**  
https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/oaic-statement-on-administrative-review-tribunals-bunnings-decision  
https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/privacy-commissioner-publishes-updated-guidance-on-facial-recognition-in-retail-spaces  
**What it demonstrates:** A legitimate safety purpose does not automatically make an invasive technology proportionate or compliant.  
**Why useful:** Contains an actual trade-off rather than villain/victim caricature.  
**Chapter fit:** Surveillance / privacy.  
**Printed book:** Excellent.  
**Website:** Excellent with current guidance.  
**Limitations:** Legal details should be rechecked.  
**Dates quickly:** Medium.

## Example 16 — Training-data extraction from language models

**Risk category:** Privacy  
**What happened:** Researchers demonstrated scalable extraction of memorised training data from production language models in 2023, building on earlier evidence that models can memorise and reproduce rare sequences including personal data.  
**Source:** Nasr et al.  
https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035  
**What it demonstrates:** Model memorisation/privacy leakage is a real technical class of risk, not merely “the provider might read your prompt.”  
**Why useful:** Adds technical grounding without needing technical detail.  
**Chapter fit:** Privacy.  
**Printed book:** Brief mention only.  
**Website:** Better for deeper explanation.  
**Limitations:** Specific exploit/model versions may no longer work; do not imply current systems can be exploited the same way.  
**Dates quickly:** Medium.

## Example 19 — Google publishes a per-prompt environmental measurement

**Risk category:** Environmental costs  
**What happened:** Google reported that its measured median Gemini Apps text prompt in May 2025 used about 0.24 Wh, 0.03 g CO2e and 0.26 mL water under its methodology.  
**Source:** Google, 21 August 2025.  
https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference/  
**What it demonstrates:** Short text inference can be individually small on an efficient system; provider disclosure can improve the evidence base.  
**Why useful:** Helps counter dramatic per-query memes while preserving aggregate concerns.  
**Chapter fit:** Environment.  
**Printed book:** Good if labelled company/product-specific.  
**Website:** Better; figures can be updated.  
**Limitations:** Company operational data, methodology-specific, not universal, not image/video/agent workloads.  
**Dates quickly:** High.

## Example 23 — Australian children and AI companions

**Risk category:** Additional finding: AI companions / children / privacy  
**What happened:** eSafety surveyed 1,950 Australian children aged 10–17 in February–March 2026. Among children who had used an AI assistant or companion, 20% reported potentially inappropriate or harmful interactions and 32% had shared personal or potentially sensitive information.  
**Source:** eSafety, *Talking to machines: Children's experiences with AI assistants and companions* (2026).  
https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/talking-to-machines-childrens-experiences-with-ai-assistants-and-companions  
**What it demonstrates:** Relational/assistant AI creates a current child-safety and privacy question beyond the plan's nine headings.  
**Why useful:** Strong Australian evidence for deciding whether to add a brief sidebar/cross-reference.  
**Chapter fit:** Additional findings, possibly privacy/children.  
**Printed book:** Brief only, to avoid scope expansion.  
**Website:** Excellent for a dedicated parent resource.  
**Limitations:** Self-report survey; “potentially harmful” is a defined research category, not proof of clinical harm. Wider evidence on psychological outcomes is mixed.  
**Dates quickly:** Medium/high.

## Example 24 — C2PA provenance is not truth

**Risk category:** Deepfakes / verification technology  
**What happened:** C2PA's own explainer makes clear that content provenance records can document origin/edit history but do not establish the factual truth of the depicted claim.  
**Source:** C2PA Explainer.  
https://c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.2/explainer/Explainer.html  
**What it demonstrates:** A protective technology can be useful without being a “truth detector.”  
**Why useful:** Prevents the chapter replacing one false certainty with another.  
**Chapter fit:** Deepfake protection.  
**Printed book:** Excellent one-sentence caveat.  
**Website:** Good demo comparing provenance/no provenance.  
**Limitations:** Adoption/coverage remains incomplete.  
**Dates quickly:** Medium.
