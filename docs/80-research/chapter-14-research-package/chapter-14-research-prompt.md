# Important note about the plan's research constraints

The Chapter 14 plan states that "no new external research [is] required" because the chapter "explicitly avoids specific predictions or statistics that would need verification or would date quickly."

**This research task deliberately supersedes that note, while respecting the reason behind it.**

Avoiding hard predictions does not mean avoiding evidence. A chapter that says "here is a plausible direction, and here is why" needs to know **what is actually happening now**, **how mature each direction really is**, **what has been promised before and not delivered**, and **what the genuine obstacles are**. Without that, the chapter's claims of plausibility are themselves unsupported, which would fail the very evaluation method the chapter invites readers to apply.

Therefore, research should focus on:

- the **current, documented state** of each direction;
- **measured trends** (with their limitations), clearly separated from forecasts;
- **past predictions** in each area and how they turned out;
- the **specific unresolved problems** that determine whether and how each direction develops;
- and **what signals** a reader could watch for in future to judge progress for themselves.

The final chapter should still avoid confident predictions. The research should make its caution **informed**, not vague.

# 1. Agents

The plan defines an AI agent, in plain terms, as **software that can take multi-step action toward a goal, not just respond to a single request**, building on Chapter 3's brief mention of automation.

Research:

- how leading AI developers, researchers, and standards bodies define "agent," and why definitions differ;
- the distinction some developers draw between predefined **workflows** (fixed steps) and **agents** (systems that decide their own steps);
- what agents can reliably do today, with evidence from independent evaluations and benchmarks of agents performing web, computer, and multi-step tasks;
- measured trends in the length and complexity of tasks AI agents can complete, including the methodology, limitations, and criticism of any trend measurement;
- current consumer-facing agent features (for example, AI assistants that browse the web, operate a computer, book or buy things, or manage email and calendars), described generically with dates;
- current workplace uses of agents (for example, in software development and customer service), with evidence of real performance;
- documented failures and incidents involving agents (for example, agents taking unintended actions, deleting data, making purchases, or being manipulated);
- **security risks** specific to agents, especially **prompt injection** (instructions hidden in web pages, emails, or documents that hijack an agent), and why this is considered an unsolved problem;
- questions of **permission, oversight, and accountability**: what "human in the loop" or checkpoints mean in practice, who is responsible when an agent makes a mistake, and how consent and authorisation work;
- protocols and standards that allow agents to connect to other tools and services, explained only at a conceptual level suitable for beginners;
- economic and industry forecasts about agents, clearly labelled as forecasts, including sceptical forecasts about project cancellations or "agent washing";
- and how Chapter 11's evaluation method applies to agent claims.

Evaluate the draft's **mental model**: today's chat tools are like a capable assistant who acts only when asked and does one thing at a time, while agents are early, imperfect steps toward an assistant who carries out a short list of related tasks and checks in at the right moments. Research whether this is accurate and whether a better analogy exists.

Evaluate the draft's example (checking a calendar, finding a suitable time, drafting an email, and sending it with approval checkpoints). Research whether this is already available, how reliably it works, and whether a more current or more illustrative example exists.

# Why predictions about AI are hard

The plan includes a section explaining **why the chapter avoids hard predictions**. Research this thoroughly, because it is the chapter's most distinctive feature and ties the whole book together.

Investigate:

- the historical record of AI predictions, including confident early predictions by AI pioneers, the "AI winters," and more recent predictions that proved too early or too late;

- and scenario-based thinking as an alternative to prediction (describing several possible futures rather than one).

Research how the chapter could explain these ideas in a way that makes readers **better at judging future claims**, rather than simply telling them that nobody knows.

# Contrasting viewpoints

Chapter 14 concerns questions where serious people disagree strongly about pace, scale, benefit, and risk.

Do not manufacture artificial "both sides" symmetry.

Instead:

- identify the main positions;
- explain the reasoning behind each;
- identify what evidence supports each;
- identify where evidence is weak;
- distinguish measured trends from extrapolations;
- distinguish demonstrations from deployments;
- distinguish company announcements from independent evidence;
- distinguish forecasts from scenarios;
- identify where two apparently conflicting claims can both be true.

Examples:

> AI agents can complete increasingly long and complex tasks in controlled evaluations.

and:

> AI agents remain unreliable and insecure for many real-world tasks.

These are not necessarily contradictory.

Likewise:

> Driverless ride-hailing services now operate commercially in some cities.

and:

> Predictions that self-driving cars would be common everywhere by now were wrong.

are both true.

Likewise:

> Some AI capabilities have arrived faster than experts predicted.

and:

> Many confident AI predictions have proved far too early.

are both part of the historical record.

Capture this nuance.

Research perspectives from:

- AI researchers with different views on pace and risk;
- economists and economic historians;
- technology forecasters;
- domain experts in education, healthcare, and transport;
- regulators;
- AI companies (noting commercial interest);
- civil-society organisations;
- and sceptics of both rapid-transformation and nothing-will-change narratives.

# Quotations

Use direct quotations sparingly.

# Research philosophy

Do not approach this task trying to prove that:

- AI will transform everything soon;
- AI is overhyped and nothing much will change;
- agents are the future;
- agents are a fad;
- robots are coming;
- robots are decades away;
- AI will fix education or healthcare;
- AI will harm education or healthcare;
- the future is predictable;
- nothing useful can be said about the future.

Approach these as questions to investigate.

The research package should contain enough evidence for the later writer to say:

> Here is what each direction actually is.

> Here is where it genuinely stands today.

> Here is what is holding it back.

> Here is what people predicted before, and what happened.

> Here is what to watch for so you can judge for yourself.

> Here is why human choices, not only technology, will shape what comes next.

That is more valuable than producing a predetermined narrative.
