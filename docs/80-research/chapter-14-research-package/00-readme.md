# Chapter 14 Research Package — Where AI Goes Next

**Research date:** 1 October 2026  
**Book:** *How-To-Use-AI.com* — Book 1  
**Chapter:** 14 — *Where AI Goes Next*  
**Purpose:** Research package for a later writing system. This package does **not** write Chapter 14.

## Authoritative scope

The approved Chapter 14 plan remains authoritative for chapter purpose, reader state, learning outcomes, and required directions. The existing chapter draft is treated as a template to test and improve, not as established fact.

The chapter's intended stance is unusually important:

> Describe plausible directions, why they are plausible, where they stand now, what is holding them back, and what readers should watch — without pretending to know exactly what happens next.

The research supports retaining that stance. The strongest evidence does **not** support either a simple "AI will transform everything immediately" narrative or a simple "this is mostly hype" narrative. Different directions are at radically different maturity levels.

## Major research conclusions

1. **"Future AI" is already partly deployed, but unevenly.** Industrial robotics, warehouse automation, Australian mining automation, medical imaging AI, generative-AI use in schools, and limited Level-4 robotaxi services are real deployments. General-purpose personal agents, humanoid workers and broad Level-5 autonomy remain much less mature.

2. **Capability, deployment and adoption must be separated.** A lab result can improve quickly while deployment remains constrained by reliability, security, regulation, economics, infrastructure, public acceptance, liability, organisational change and training.

3. **Agents are real, but the word is overloaded.** Credible developers explicitly distinguish predictable workflows from systems that decide their own steps. The security problem is not theoretical: indirect prompt injection / agent hijacking is an active research and standards problem.

4. **Robotics is a useful antidote to software-only futurism.** Mature automation is often specialised, bounded and unglamorous. Amazon reported one million deployed robots in 2025. Australian mining and logistics provide strong examples of automation at scale. Humanoid robots remain a separate and much less mature category.

5. **Education evidence is mixed in exactly the way Chapter 14 needs.** Well-designed tutoring systems can help; unrestricted generative-AI assistance can improve practice performance while reducing later unassisted learning. Australian school systems are moving from bans and pilots toward purpose-built systems and governance.

6. **Healthcare is one of the clearest examples of AI progressing through regulation rather than around it.** The US FDA reported more than 1,600 authorised AI-enabled medical devices by September 2026, heavily concentrated in radiology. Australia regulates qualifying AI software as medical devices through the TGA. Randomised trials support some applications, while external-validation failures remain an important warning.

7. **Transport demonstrates why prediction is hard.** Commercial driverless ride-hailing exists in selected US cities, yet broad self-driving predictions were repeatedly premature. Australia still treats highly/fully automated driving as trial-stage and is building a national safety-law framework.

8. **Personal assistants are converging with agents.** Memory, personal context, tool use and background task execution are increasingly combined, but privacy and trust costs rise with capability. Earlier "AI device" launches such as Rabbit R1 are useful hype-vs-product case studies.

9. **The chapter should explicitly teach readers to watch signals rather than dates.** Independent evaluations, real deployment at scale, regulation, insurance/liability arrangements, transparent outcome data, falling costs, and adoption by cautious institutions are more durable indicators than launch demos or executive claims.

10. **Australian policy now gives the chapter an especially useful agency theme.** Australia has a National AI Plan, an Australian AI Safety Institute, an APS AI Plan, healthcare reforms, school frameworks and automated-vehicle law work. These make the point that societies shape deployment through institutions and choices.

## Maturity vocabulary recommended for the eventual chapter

Use a small, explicit vocabulary:

- **Research:** credible result, not yet a product or operational deployment
- **Demo:** deliberately staged demonstration
- **Pilot / trial:** real users or environment, constrained scope
- **Limited deployment:** genuine service/product, but restricted geography, users or conditions
- **Widespread deployment:** operational at meaningful scale across many sites/users
- **Mature infrastructure:** routine, established and no longer dependent on novelty

This vocabulary aligns naturally with Chapter 11's demo/product/research distinction while adding enough granularity for Chapter 14.

## Important cautions

- Company operational data can establish what a company says it deployed, but not automatically prove comparative safety or effectiveness.
- Forecasts and investment totals are evidence of expectations and incentives, not evidence that an outcome will occur.
- Benchmarks can measure progress while still failing to represent messy real-world operation.
- Regulatory approval means a product met applicable regulatory requirements for an intended use; it does not mean "AI is generally safe" or that the product is superior to every alternative.
- Fast-moving figures should be framed as dated snapshots.
- Avoid using "AI" as though it were one coherent technology with one maturity curve.

## Overall editorial recommendation

The chapter should not become a catalogue of futuristic sectors. Its strongest possible structure is:

1. What this direction means.
2. What already exists.
3. What is still difficult.
4. What people predicted before.
5. What signals would indicate genuine progress.

That repeatable structure would make the chapter useful long after individual product names and deployment counts change.
