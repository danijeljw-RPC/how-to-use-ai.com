# Contrasting Viewpoints and Genuine Disagreements

## 1. "AI as normal technology" versus rapid transformation

### Normal-technology view
Narayanan and Kapoor argue that even transformative technology diffuses through institutions slowly and unevenly. Capability alone does not determine outcomes.

**Strong evidence/logic**
- adoption depends on complementary infrastructure;
- organisations change slowly;
- law and social systems constrain deployment;
- people retain control over many deployment decisions.

### Faster-transformation view
Other researchers/scenario writers argue that AI capability may improve fast enough to automate increasingly complex cognitive work and potentially parts of AI R&D itself.

**Strong evidence/logic**
- rapid benchmark improvements;
- measurable growth in agent task horizons;
- fast consumer adoption;
- enormous infrastructure investment.

### What can both sides agree on?
Current capability is improving rapidly; actual societal transformation depends on deployment and adoption.

### Editorial stance
Do not select a winner. Show assumptions.

## 2. Agents are improving rapidly / agents remain unreliable

Both are true.

**Progress evidence**
- browser/computer-use products;
- coding agents;
- METR task-horizon trend;
- enterprise deployment.

**Limitation evidence**
- prompt injection;
- long-horizon error accumulation;
- permission/accountability issues;
- benchmarks do not equal open-world reliability.

The apparent contradiction disappears when reliability threshold and task environment are specified.

---

## 3. Robotaxis are real / self-driving predictions were overconfident

Both are true.

Robotaxis operate commercially in constrained geographies. Level-5 general driving is not solved. Australia remains in a trial/regulatory-preparation stage for high/full automation on public roads.

The correct question is not "Do self-driving cars exist?" but:
> Under what conditions, in what jurisdictions, with what fallback and safety evidence?

---

## 4. AI tutoring can help / AI can harm learning

Both are true.

The evidence suggests design matters:
- structured tutoring can scaffold;
- answer-provision can create dependence;
- immediate task performance can hide weak retention.

This is an ideal example of why generic claims about "AI in education" are low quality.

---

## 5. AI is already in medicine / AI cannot replace doctors

Both can be true.

AI-enabled medical devices are numerous and regulated. Imaging and workflow tools have real deployments. That does not establish general medical autonomy.

The unit of analysis should be **task**, not profession.

---

## 6. AI adoption is historically fast / institutional change can be slow

Bick/Blandin/Deming support rapid US adoption relative to PCs/internet. Yet adoption does not imply deep workflow transformation.

An employee trying ChatGPT once a week is adoption. Rebuilding a hospital, school system or regulated profession around AI is institutional transformation.

---

## 7. Regulation slows technology / regulation enables trusted deployment

Both effects can occur.

Regulation can:
- delay launch;
- raise compliance cost;
- restrict unsafe uses.

But it can also:
- create rules for liability;
- establish evidence requirements;
- increase public trust;
- make procurement possible;
- standardise expectations.

Medical devices and automated vehicles are useful examples.

---

## 8. Open models increase access / open models create governance challenges

Open-weight models can:
- lower costs;
- support local/on-premises use;
- reduce dependence on a few providers;
- allow research and customisation.

They can also:
- be harder to withdraw once released;
- complicate safety enforcement;
- make powerful capabilities broadly reproducible.

This is a better fit for the companion website/later books than Chapter 14 proper.

---

## 9. AI may accelerate science / AI may also slow or distort science

Potential benefits:
- hypothesis generation;
- weather prediction;
- structure prediction;
- data analysis;
- literature synthesis.

Risks:
- low-quality generated papers;
- reproducibility issues;
- automation bias;
- concentration of compute/data;
- researchers spending time validating generated output.

Chapter 14 can mention both without adding a new full section.

---

## 10. AI capability may continue accelerating / progress may plateau

The 2026 International AI Safety Report explicitly treats multiple trajectories as plausible.

Reasons progress could continue:
- scaling;
- algorithmic improvement;
- specialised hardware;
- synthetic data / tool use;
- agentic workflows.

Reasons it could slow:
- data/compute/energy constraints;
- diminishing returns;
- benchmark saturation;
- reliability bottlenecks;
- economic constraints;
- regulation;
- lack of useful deployment contexts.

No single extrapolation should be written as fact.

---

## Practical editorial rule

When two claims appear contradictory, add the missing variable:

- **where?**
- **at what reliability?**
- **for which task?**
- **under what regulation?**
- **measured by whom?**
- **capability, deployment or adoption?**

That technique should be used throughout the chapter.
