# Editorial Possibilities

These are editorial devices, not factual claims.

## 1. Best diagram: Capability → Deployment → Adoption

### Concept
Three large stages:

**Capability**
"Can it do it?"

↓ bottlenecks: reliability, security, cost

**Deployment**
"Can it operate safely and legally in the real world?"

↓ bottlenecks: integration, training, liability, infrastructure

**Adoption**
"Do people and organisations actually use it routinely?"

### Why it works
It explains why:
- benchmarks can leap while society changes slowly;
- some narrow systems become normal before general systems;
- a demo and a widespread technology can both be "real" but very different.

### Where it breaks down
Stages can loop; deployment produces data that improves capability, and adoption can be forced or subsidised.

## 2. Core comparison table

| Direction | What exists now | Main obstacle | Signal to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agents | Multi-step browser/coding/work agents | reliability + prompt injection | independent long-task success + delegated authority standards |
| Robotics | industrial/warehouse/mining automation | general physical robustness/cost | autonomous paid operation outside controlled settings |
| Education | general chatbots + purpose-built school tools | learning design + assessment/privacy | long-term learning outcomes |
| Healthcare | regulated imaging + workflow AI | external validation + accountability | prospective outcomes/post-market data |
| Transport | Level-2 widespread; Level-4 geofenced services | generalisation + regulation/economics | expansion + independent safety data |
| Personal assistants | conversation + memory + tools | trust/privacy/permissions | audited cross-app action reliability |

This table can carry a large amount of value in little space.

## 4. Agent analogy

### Draft analogy
Chat assistant waits for each request; agent carries out a short list.

### Improved analogy
**Assistant with a keycard**

A chatbot can suggest what to do. An agent may also have permission to open doors: calendar, email, browser, files, payments.

The key question becomes:
> Which doors should it be able to open without asking?

### Where it breaks
Human assistants understand organisational norms and consequences better than current systems.

---

## 5. Robotics analogy

### "Chess versus kitchen"
Computers conquered formal games before general household manipulation.

Useful for Moravec's paradox.

### Where it breaks
Modern robotics can do much more than the classic analogy implies, and "kitchen" combines perception, planning, dexterity and safety.

## 9. Website interactive: "Demo, deployment or adoption?"

Present real headlines and ask readers to classify:
- vendor demo;
- trial;
- regulator approval;
- commercial deployment;
- adoption data;
- forecast.

Then reveal answer and reasoning.

## 11. Evidence box: "A regulated AI future already exists"

Short facts:
- FDA AI-enabled medical-device register;
- TGA AI regulation;
- NTC automated vehicle law;
- Australian school framework.

Concept: the future of AI is partly a future of standards, evidence and institutions — not only more capable models.

---

## 12. "What would convince me?" boxes

For each direction, include a one-line falsifiable signal.

Example:
**Humanoids:** "I would update toward near-term widespread deployment if multiple companies sustain paid, mostly autonomous deployments across ordinary workplaces with published intervention and safety data."

This models intellectual updating rather than prediction.

---

## 13. Avoid these editorial devices

- generic robot silhouette/futuristic city art;
- countdown-to-AGI graphics;
- exponential curves without definitions;
- a single "AI maturity" score covering all sectors;
- company roadmap timelines presented as future facts;
- "good future vs bad future" binary diagrams.

They push the chapter toward speculative futurism rather than evidence.
