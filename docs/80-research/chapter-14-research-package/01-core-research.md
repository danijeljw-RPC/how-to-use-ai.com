# Core Research — Framing, Human Agency and Missing Directions

## 1. Testing the chapter's central framing

### "AI is not the end of human relevance. It is the beginning of a different technological era."

The first sentence is fundamentally a normative framing, not an empirical proposition that can be proven. It works best when "human relevance" is interpreted as a rejection of technological determinism rather than as a prediction about employment or consciousness.

The second sentence — "the beginning of a different technological era" — is defensible if written carefully. Evidence supporting it includes unusually rapid adoption of generative AI, rapid improvement on technical benchmarks, major private investment, and integration of AI into consumer software, education, medicine, government and robotics.

However, credible scholars disagree about whether AI should be framed as historically exceptional.

### AI as a general-purpose / transformative technology

The 2026 Stanford AI Index describes rapid growth in capabilities, organisational adoption and investment. Bick, Blandin and Deming's nationally representative US surveys found generative AI adoption was unusually rapid compared with the personal computer and internet when measured from mass-market launch.

This supports describing AI as a potentially broad enabling technology.

**Sources**
- Stanford HAI, *The 2026 AI Index Report* (2026): https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report
- Bick, Blandin & Deming, *The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI*, Management Science (2026): https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.02523

### AI as "normal technology"

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that AI can be transformative while still being "normal technology": a tool whose effects are mediated by adoption, institutions, regulation, organisational change and human choices. They reject treating AI as a separate species or as an autonomous historical force.

This perspective is particularly compatible with Chapter 14 because it allows cautious optimism without claiming slow progress.

**Source**
- Narayanan & Kapoor, *AI as Normal Technology*, Knight First Amendment Institute (15 Apr 2025): https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology

### Faster-change / discontinuity perspectives

The other credible end of the debate argues that capability improvements may compound quickly, especially when systems can automate parts of AI research and software engineering. Scenario exercises such as "AI 2027" are best treated as scenarios rather than forecasts: their value is in exploring causal chains and stress-testing assumptions, not in presenting dates as established facts.

**Editorial lesson:** The chapter does not need to adjudicate "normal technology" versus rapid transformation. It can show that both arguments depend on assumptions about capability growth, deployment, bottlenecks and institutional response.

### Recommended wording principle

Prefer:

> AI may become a general-purpose technology whose effects spread through many parts of society, but the pace and form of that change will depend on much more than model capability.

Avoid:

> AI is definitely the next electricity / internet / industrial revolution.

The latter is a prediction disguised as an analogy.

## 2. What makes a direction "plausible"?

A practical evidence-based test for the chapter:

A direction is more plausibly important when several of these are present:

1. **Repeated technical progress**, not one benchmark.
2. **Independent evaluation**, not only vendor demos.
3. **Operational deployment**, even if limited.
4. **Clear demand or economic incentive**.
5. **A regulatory pathway** rather than a legal dead end.
6. **Infrastructure and supply chains** capable of supporting it.
7. **Evidence that users or institutions continue using it after pilots**.
8. **Falling cost / improving reliability**.
9. **Integration into existing workflows**, not just standalone novelty.
10. **Known obstacles that are being measured**, rather than hand-waved away.

This framework can become a recurring box or table.

## 3. Capability versus deployment versus adoption

This is one of the strongest conceptual additions available.

### Capability
What the technology can do in an evaluation, laboratory or controlled environment.

### Deployment
Whether a functioning product/service is actually operating in the world.

### Adoption
Whether people or organisations use it routinely and reorganise behaviour around it.

These can move at very different speeds.

Examples:

- A robot can demonstrate dexterous movement before factories can economically deploy it.
- A model can achieve high benchmark scores before hospitals can safely integrate it.
- A driverless vehicle can operate commercially in geofenced areas while national road systems remain overwhelmingly human-driven.
- A school can provide AI access before researchers know the long-term effect on learning habits.

**Editorial value:** this distinction operationalises Chapter 11's scepticism without defaulting to pessimism.

## 4. Human agency and technological determinism

The chapter's closing message should lean more on **human agency** than "human relevance."

Technology does not determine a single social outcome. Design choices, business models, laws, professional standards, procurement, user behaviour, labour institutions and public norms shape what is deployed and how.

### Current Australian examples

Australia's National AI Plan, released 2 December 2025, explicitly combines three goals:
- capture opportunities;
- spread benefits;
- keep Australians safe.

The Australian AI Safety Institute now analyses emerging capabilities and risks, conducts/participates in model evaluations, and supports regulators.

The Australian Public Service AI Plan gives public servants access to tools and training while introducing accountability, transparency and governance mechanisms.

Education ministers maintain a national framework for generative AI in schools.

The TGA regulates qualifying AI software as medical devices according to intended purpose.

The National Transport Commission is developing a national Automated Vehicle Safety Law.

These are direct examples of institutions shaping how a technology enters society rather than merely waiting for capability to dictate outcomes.

**Sources**
- Australian Government, *National AI Plan* (2 Dec 2025): https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/national-ai-plan
- Australian AI Safety Institute: https://www.industry.gov.au/science-technology-and-innovation/technology/artificial-intelligence/ai-safety-institute
- APS AI Plan 2025: https://www.digital.gov.au/policy/ai/australian-public-service-ai-plan-2025
- Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools: https://www.education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools
- NTC Automated Vehicle Program: https://www.ntc.gov.au/transport-reform/automated-vehicle-program

### Durable reader lesson

The useful response to uncertainty is not passive waiting. Ordinary people exercise agency by:
- choosing whether and where to use AI;
- demanding transparency from providers;
- protecting personal information;
- participating in workplace consultation;
- influencing school policies as parents/students/educators;
- supporting or challenging laws;
- choosing products based on safety, privacy and interoperability;
- retaining skills they do not want to outsource.

This connects Chapter 14 directly to Chapters 12 and 13 without repeating them.

## 5. Directions missing from the plan

### AI in science and research — strong candidate for brief inclusion

AI-supported weather forecasting, protein structure prediction, materials discovery and mathematical/scientific reasoning are sufficiently important to merit at least a short mention.

Why it matters:
- It is different from "AI doing office work".
- It gives a non-consumer example of AI helping humans expand knowledge.
- It avoids making the future chapter revolve entirely around assistants and automation.

Recommended placement: brief "Other directions worth watching" box or paragraph, not a full section.

### Government and public services — strong Australian website/chapter sidebar

The APS is moving beyond ad hoc pilots toward government-wide access, training and secure tooling. Services Australia has an Automation and AI Strategy 2025–27. This is useful evidence that AI adoption is organisational, governed and gradual.

Recommended placement: short example under human agency or "what adoption actually looks like."

### Smaller/on-device models — brief inclusion

On-device processing matters because it can reduce latency, cloud cost and some privacy exposure. It does not remove privacy risk automatically: devices still contain sensitive data and may synchronise with cloud services.

Recommended placement: personal assistants.

### Open-weight models — later books / website

Important for competition, sovereignty and engineering choices but too technical for Chapter 14 unless mentioned in one sentence about how AI may become cheaper and more local.

### Energy and compute constraints — connect to Chapter 9

The IEA estimated data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity use, with rapid growth. This is a real physical constraint and policy issue.

Recommended placement: one concise reminder that future AI is constrained by physical infrastructure, energy, water, chips and grids. Do not re-run Chapter 9.

**Source**
- IEA, *Energy and AI* (2025): https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai

### Regulation and governance — not a "future technology", but a major shaping force

The chapter should not present regulation as an afterthought. In health, education, transport and government it directly affects what counts as deployment.

### Possibility that progress slows

The 2026 International AI Safety Report explicitly treats multiple paths to 2030 as plausible: slowdown/plateau, continuation, or dramatic acceleration. That is exceptionally useful for the chapter because it legitimises uncertainty without implying ignorance.

**Source**
- International AI Safety Report 2026: https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publications

## 6. Core framing recommendation

A stronger core idea than "human relevance" alone:

> AI capability matters, but capability is not destiny. What happens next will be shaped by what the technology can do, what can be deployed reliably, what people actually adopt, and what societies choose to permit, encourage or reject.

This is not proposed chapter prose; it is the research-backed conceptual frame the later writer can use.
