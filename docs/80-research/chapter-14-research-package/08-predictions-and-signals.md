# Predictions, Forecasting and Signals to Watch

## Why this is the chapter's most important research thread

Chapter 14 should not merely say "nobody knows." That is true but unhelpful.

A better lesson is:

> We cannot know exact outcomes, but we can get better at separating evidence, forecasts and scenarios, and we can watch the conditions that would make a direction more or less likely.

---

## 1. AI prediction has a poor record in both directions

AI history contains:
- ambitious predictions that arrived far later than expected;
- periods of disappointment and reduced funding ("AI winters");
- capabilities that arrived sooner than many observers expected.

The book should deliberately include both.

### Why "prediction failed" is not enough

A failed date may reflect:
- technical bottlenecks;
- insufficient compute/data;
- cost;
- regulation;
- market rejection;
- infrastructure;
- organisational adoption;
- a breakthrough in a different direction.

Forecasts should be examined for their assumptions, not mocked only because the date was wrong.

---

## 2. Expert surveys are evidence of belief, not evidence of outcome

AI Impacts' 2023 Expert Survey on Progress in AI surveyed 2,778 researchers from six top AI venues.

Responses shifted substantially compared with earlier surveys and showed very wide disagreement.

This is useful because it demonstrates:
- expert views are not monolithic;
- timeline distributions move after new evidence;
- definitions materially affect answers.

**Sources**
- AI Impacts survey page: https://aiimpacts.org/surveys/
- 2023 survey background: https://wiki.aiimpacts.org/ai_timelines/predictions_of_human-level_ai_timelines/ai_timeline_surveys/2023_expert_survey_on_progress_in_ai

### Editorial rule

Never write "experts predict X" from the median of one survey without:
- the question wording;
- response population;
- date;
- spread/disagreement.

## 3. Forecasting research

Philip Tetlock's work on expert political judgment and later forecasting research is relevant at a conceptual level:

Useful forecasters tend to:
- update beliefs;
- break big questions into smaller ones;
- use probabilities;
- avoid ideological certainty;
- track outcomes.

The chapter does not need to teach forecasting science, but can borrow the posture.

A key caveat from later literature is that forecasting accuracy depends on question type and time horizon; short, well-defined forecasts are easier than long-range open-ended predictions.

**Background source**
- discussion in PNAS: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4136586/

## 4. Scenarios versus forecasts

A scenario asks:

> What would the world look like if these assumptions held?

A forecast asks:

> How likely is this outcome by a specified time?

This distinction is essential for material such as rapid-AI scenarios.

A dramatic scenario can be useful even when its probability is unknown. The writer should label it accordingly.

## 5. International AI Safety Report: explicit uncertainty

The 2026 International AI Safety Report is unusually useful because it explicitly states that between now and 2030, AI progress could:
- slow/plateau;
- continue at roughly current rates;
- accelerate dramatically.

This is exactly the calibration Chapter 14 needs.

**Source**
- International AI Safety Report 2026: https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publications
- Australian Government summary: https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/international-ai-safety-report-2026

## 6. Adoption can be fast even when transformation is slow

Bick, Blandin and Deming found generative-AI adoption in the US spread faster than PCs or internet adoption from comparable mass-market launch points.

This does not mean economic transformation occurs at the same speed.

Adoption of a tool can be rapid while:
- workflow redesign is slow;
- organisations lack policy/training;
- gains are uneven;
- complementary investments take years.

**Source**
- https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.02523

This is an excellent example of two apparently conflicting claims both being true:
- "GenAI adoption is historically fast."
- "Societal transformation may still be gradual."

## 7. Amara's Law

Commonly stated:

> We tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run.

The saying is widely attributed to Roy Amara, but its exact origin is poorly documented and similar wording predates the label.

Recommendation:
- use only if the writer can tolerate attribution uncertainty;
- preferably paraphrase the idea and note it is commonly attributed to Amara;
- do not present it as an empirically proven law.

## 8. Signals to watch instead of dates

### General signals

#### Independent evaluation
A result reproduced by independent researchers is stronger than a vendor demo.

#### Deployment at scale
A pilot becoming a routine service is stronger evidence than a launch announcement.

#### Outcome data
Did the system improve learning, health, safety, cost or productivity?

#### Regulation
Approvals and legal frameworks show institutions believe a pathway to deployment exists.

#### Insurance and liability
When insurers, courts and contracts can price responsibility, a technology is becoming operationally mature.

#### Cost
A capability that works at $1,000 per task is different from one that works at $1.

#### Human intervention rate
How often must someone rescue or correct it?

#### Geographic/environmental expansion
Can a system leave a controlled setting?

#### Professional standards/training
When professions rewrite training and standards around a technology, adoption is becoming structural.

#### Incident reporting
Transparent incident data is a sign of mature governance, even when incidents themselves are negative.

## 9. Direction-specific signals

### Agents
- 80%+ reliability on long tasks;
- prompt-injection resistance;
- delegated-authority standards;
- audit/reversibility;
- enterprise usage beyond pilots.

### Robotics/humanoids
- autonomous productive hours;
- cost per useful hour;
- intervention rate;
- safety certification;
- multi-site paid deployment.

### Education
- unassisted learning retention;
- teacher workload;
- equity outcomes;
- assessment redesign;
- privacy/safety metrics.

### Healthcare
- regulator approvals;
- prospective trials;
- external validation;
- patient outcomes;
- post-market surveillance.

### Transport
- regulator-approved Level-4 expansion;
- independent crash data;
- operating-domain expansion;
- insurance/liability frameworks.

### Personal assistants
- granular permissions;
- trustworthy memory;
- cross-app action reliability;
- privacy enforcement;
- retention/usefulness.

## 11. Recommended chapter exercise

Give the reader a future claim, e.g.:

> "Humanoid robots will replace warehouse workers within five years."

Ask them to identify:
1. What is the evidence: demo, pilot or deployment?
2. What must improve?
3. What costs/constraints matter?
4. What would falsify the claim?
5. What signal would make them update toward it?
6. Who benefits if they believe it now?

This directly reuses Chapter 11 without repeating the whole method.
