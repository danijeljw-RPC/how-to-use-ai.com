# Education — Research Notes

## Core conclusion

The draft's claim that personalised tutoring is a plausible growth area is supportable, but the evidence now allows a much stronger and more specific treatment:

> AI can help learning when it is designed to support the learning process; unrestricted answer-giving can improve immediate task performance while weakening later independent performance.

This is more useful than a generic "promising but concerns exist" formulation.

---

## Historical tutoring evidence and Bloom's 2-sigma claim

Benjamin Bloom's 1984 "2 sigma problem" is often summarised as one-to-one tutoring producing performance about two standard deviations above conventional classroom instruction.

The result is historically important but should not be treated as a universal modern estimate of tutoring effects. Later tutoring research generally finds positive effects but effect sizes vary by subject, tutor, design, student population and implementation.

**Primary historical source**
- Benjamin S. Bloom, *The 2 Sigma Problem: The Search for Methods of Group Instruction as Effective as One-to-One Tutoring*, Educational Researcher 13(6), 1984. DOI: 10.3102/0013189X013006004

**Editorial use:** explain why personalised tutoring has long been attractive, then immediately state that "2 sigma" should not be used as a promise for AI tutoring.

## Strong negative/qualifying evidence: unrestricted generative AI

Bastani et al. studied high-school mathematics and distinguished unrestricted GPT access from a more structured GPT tutor.

The key durable finding is the difference between **performance while assisted** and **learning retained without assistance**.

Students can appear more capable while the tool is present without acquiring the same underlying skill.

That finding is highly relevant to Chapter 12's durable-skills message.

**Source**
- H. Bastani et al., *Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics*, PNAS 122(26), 2025: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560616/

### Editorial caution
Do not turn one study into "AI harms learning." The lesson is about design and dependence, not a universal effect.

## Australian school policy

### National framework

Australia's Framework for Generative AI in Schools was created in 2023 and reviewed in 2024; Education Ministers endorsed the review in June 2025.

Its six principles:
1. Teaching and learning
2. Human and social wellbeing
3. Transparency
4. Fairness
5. Accountability
6. Privacy, security and safety

The framework is deliberately adaptive, with reviews intended to keep pace with change.

**Source**
- Australian Department of Education: https://www.education.gov.au/schooling/resources/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools

### South Australia: EdChat

South Australia's Department for Education developed EdChat with Microsoft.

By February 2025:
- it had been trialled with more than 10,000 students and 2,000 staff;
- it was rolled out to all public-school teachers.

In September 2025 the state announced rollout to all public secondary students.

This is a very strong Australian example because it shows government choosing a purpose-built, governed system rather than simply telling students to use public chatbots.

**Sources**
- SA Department for Education, 13 Feb 2025: https://www.education.sa.gov.au/department/media-centre/our-news/ai-tool-rolled-out-to-support-teachers
- SA Department for Education, 15 Sep 2025: https://www.education.sa.gov.au/department/media-centre/our-news/ai-tool-to-roll-out-to-all-public-high-schools

### NSW: NSWEduChat

NSW announced in September 2025 that its purpose-built NSWEduChat would roll out to public-school students from Year 5 after a 50-school trial.

**Source**
- NSW Department of Education, 23 Sep 2025: https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/roll-out-of-purpose-built-ai-education-tool-to-all-public-school

NSW's 2026 HSC reforms limiting take-home assessment are useful evidence that institutions may adapt assessment itself rather than relying on AI detection.

## AI-writing detectors

The evidence base has repeatedly found reliability problems, including false positives and sensitivity to writing style/language background.

The durable message should be:

> Detecting AI-written text from the text alone is not a dependable foundation for high-stakes accusations.

This makes assessment redesign, process evidence and supervised assessment more defensible than "AI detector says 82%".

**Recent overview**
- Giray, Roe & Espiritu, *AI writing detectors are ineffective, unreliable and harmful*, English Teaching: Practice & Critique, 2026: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1175870826000130

**Caution:** the cited 2026 paper is an argumentative review/essay rather than a single definitive detector benchmark. Pair with primary detector studies if the final chapter uses detailed accuracy claims.

## Teachers: support versus replacement

Current policy and deployment evidence strongly supports framing AI as teacher support before teacher replacement.

Practical uses:
- lesson planning;
- adapting materials;
- administrative summarisation;
- feedback drafting;
- policy lookup.

The important distinction is whether AI:
- increases teacher capacity while preserving professional judgement;
or
- becomes a substitute for interaction, diagnosis of learning needs and accountability.

Australian deployments explicitly retain educators within the system.

## Equity

AI tutoring could narrow gaps if high-quality support becomes cheaper and universally available.

It could widen gaps if:
- paid systems are much better than free ones;
- some students have better devices/connectivity;
- home support differs;
- models work less well for some dialects/languages;
- schools differ in training and governance.

The strongest chapter treatment is conditional rather than predictive.

## Children, privacy and emotional reliance

Australia's eSafety Commissioner reported in 2026 that 78% of surveyed Australian children aged 10–17 had used an AI assistant; among children using assistants/companions, many used them for personal or social reasons.

This is relevant to education because school use and personal-assistant use blur.

**Source**
- eSafety Commissioner, *Talking to machines: Children’s experiences with AI assistants and companions*, 2026: https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/talking-to-machines-childrens-experiences-with-ai-assistants-and-companions

Use carefully: this is survey evidence about use/experience, not evidence that AI causes wellbeing outcomes.

## Historical comparison: technology repeatedly "transforms education"

Educational television, personal computers, internet access, MOOCs and tablets were all accompanied by strong transformation claims.

The recurring lesson is:
- technology can alter access and practice;
- pedagogy, incentives, teacher training, assessment and institutional structure determine realised impact.

This is a strong example of capability versus adoption.

## Maturity assessment

| Application | Maturity |
|---|---|
| General chatbots used by students | Widespread adoption |
| Purpose-built school chatbots in Australia | Deployment / scaling |
| Teacher planning/support | Deployment |
| AI tutoring with structured pedagogy | Research to limited deployment |
| Automated high-stakes assessment | Contested / constrained |
| AI-writing detection for misconduct | Deployed but evidentially unreliable |
| Teacher replacement | Not supported as a mature direction |

## Signals to watch

- long-term learning outcomes, not only assignment scores;
- randomised trials across diverse schools;
- effect when AI is removed during assessment;
- teacher workload outcomes;
- access/equity differences;
- privacy incidents;
- changes to assessment design;
- independent evaluation of state-run school tools.
