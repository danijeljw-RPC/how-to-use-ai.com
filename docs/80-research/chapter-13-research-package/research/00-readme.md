# Chapter 13 Research Package — Building Your Personal AI Toolkit

**Research date:** 2026-10-01  
**Primary jurisdiction:** Australia  
**Book:** *How To Use AI.com*, Book 1  
**Chapter:** 13 — Building Your Personal AI Toolkit

## Purpose

This package is research for a later writing system. It is **not Chapter 13** and should not be pasted into the book as prose.

The approved Chapter 13 plan remains authoritative for scope, reader state, learning outcomes, and the chapter's role in the book. The existing chapter draft/template is useful as a map of the current thinking, but the research below deliberately tests its claims, identifies gaps, and supplies stronger evidence and examples.

The core editorial constraint remains intact:

> The printed chapter should teach durable categories, questions, habits, and warning signs rather than recommend, rank, or endorse named AI products.

Real products are nevertheless researched here because durable advice about privacy, pricing, subscriptions, permissions, data retention, feature convergence, and product churn cannot be grounded without checking how actual services work. Product-specific material is dated and separated for possible use on the companion website.

## Executive findings

1. **The core framing survives research, but needs one qualification.** "AI literacy matters more than loyalty to any specific tool" is defensible if "literacy" means the ability to understand capabilities, limitations, risks, and context — not merely knowing prompts — and if the chapter does **not** imply that people should constantly switch products. Switching can carry practical costs: lost history, memories, integrations, custom settings, files, learned habits, and organisational approvals.

2. **The six categories remain useful teaching labels, but they no longer map neatly to six separate products.** Current general-purpose assistants increasingly combine chat, search/research, image generation/editing, voice, file analysis, and coding. Productivity suites and operating systems also embed AI into software people already use. The chapter should therefore say that the six categories describe **jobs/capabilities**, not six subscriptions a reader needs.

3. **A task-first decision model is stronger than a catalogue-first model.** "What am I trying to do?" is the most durable starting point. The reader can then map the task to a capability category, check whether a tool they already have can do it, and only then consider another service.

4. **The draft evaluation checklist is directionally good but incomplete.** It should be supported by a memorable short check for ordinary low-risk use and a deeper check before paying, uploading sensitive material, connecting accounts, or relying on a tool for consequential work. Privacy, permissions, company identity, cancellation, age suitability, accessibility, export/deletion, and source/reliability behaviour all matter.

5. **"Private", "temporary", "incognito", "training off", "memory off", and "deleted" are not synonyms.** Current provider documentation shows these controls can affect different things: model training, visible history, memory/personalisation, safety retention, human review, legal retention, file storage, shared links, and third-party connector data. This is one of the strongest practical lessons for the chapter.

6. **Consumer and organisational tiers can have materially different data rules.** Representative providers document different default training and retention behaviour for consumer products versus commercial/business products. Readers should not infer workplace protections from experience with a personal account, or vice versa.

7. **Australia's legal position is moving.** The Competition and Consumer Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Act 2026 has been enacted, including reforms covering subscription contracts, but its substantive provisions are not yet operative; the Act commences **1 July 2027**. The Children's Online Privacy Code must be finalised and registered by **10 December 2026**, so its final form requires a publication-time recheck.

8. **Subscription problems are not hypothetical.** Australian consumer research documents difficulty cancelling subscriptions, forgotten trials, and continued payments for unwanted services. This evidence is broader than AI, but it directly supports the chapter because AI tools are commonly sold through recurring consumer subscriptions.

9. **AI-themed malicious apps, extensions, and sites are documented, including inside official app/extension stores.** Security-vendor and platform reports establish that these patterns exist. They do **not** establish that most AI apps are scams or provide population prevalence; the chapter should keep that distinction explicit.

10. **Children deserve a specific check, not a generic warning.** eSafety's 2026 Australian survey of 1,950 children aged 10–17 found high use of AI assistants, personal/social uses, sensitive-information sharing, and some potentially harmful interactions. A beginner-oriented toolkit chapter should tell parents to check age suitability, privacy, relational/companion design, and safety controls before treating an AI service as "just another app".

11. **Accessibility is a substantive benefit, not a marketing footnote.** Current tools can describe images/scenes, support voice interaction, caption/transcribe, translate, and assist reading/writing. However, accessibility providers and product documentation also warn that generated descriptions can be wrong and should not substitute for safety-critical mobility or navigation aids.

12. **The chapter should avoid treating a "wrapper" as inherently suspicious.** Many legitimate products are intentionally built on third-party foundation models. The relevant questions are whether the product adds useful value, accurately describes what it is, handles data transparently, charges clearly, and provides support/security appropriate to the access it requests.

## Evidence discipline used in this package

- Primary regulator, legislation, provider-policy, platform-policy, and first-party security documentation is preferred for claims about law, settings, current features, prices, and provider commitments.
- Independent consumer organisations and academic research are used for testing, behaviour, consumer impact, and conceptual frameworks.
- Security-vendor research is used to establish documented attack/scam patterns, with explicit caution that vendor telemetry is not population prevalence.
- Provider usage data is treated as provider operational data, not representative of all AI users.
- Product-specific settings and prices are dated **2026-10-01** unless a different date is stated.
- Time-sensitive items are marked **Recheck before publication**.
- Where evidence is incomplete, the note says so rather than converting an inference into a fact.

## Boundaries with other chapters/books

- **Chapter 7 — AI at Work:** owns workplace policy, confidential information, employer-approved tools, and organisational verification. Chapter 13 should only remind the reader that work accounts/policies differ from personal use.
- **Chapter 8 — AI and Creativity:** owns copyright/licensing/commercial-use detail. Chapter 13 can include "check commercial-use terms if the output will be sold/published" without reopening the full debate.
- **Chapter 9 — Problems Nobody Should Ignore:** owns AI used by scammers, surveillance, broad privacy harms, and systemic risks. Chapter 13 owns the buyer-side question: "Is this AI product/app/subscription legitimate and sensible to install or pay for?"
- **Chapter 11 — AI Hype vs Reality:** owns the deeper method for testing claims. Chapter 13 can reuse the habit: ask what is actually demonstrated and seek independent evidence.
- **Chapter 14 — Where AI Goes Next:** owns future-facing agents. Chapter 13 should mention only present-day action-taking assistants where permissions and prompt-injection risks affect tool evaluation today.
- **Book 2:** owns advanced personal workflows and productivity systems.
- **Book 4/5:** own building integrations, agents, APIs, model architecture, and developer-level security.

## Recommended handoff instruction to the later writer

Use this package as evidence and option space, not as a mandate to include everything. Preserve the plan's beginner-friendly scope. Prefer durable principles in the printed book; move named-product settings, screenshots, current prices, and rapidly changing examples to the companion website. Do not convert provider marketing claims into independent findings. Do not turn documented scam cases into a claim that most AI products are fraudulent.
