# Editorial Opportunities — Chapter 13

**Status:** Editorial possibilities derived from the research, not proposed final prose.  
**Research date:** 2026-10-01

The aim is to make Chapter 13 useful without turning it into a catalogue, legal guide, security chapter, or advanced workflow chapter.

## 1. A task-first decision diagram

### Concept it would explain

The six categories describe capabilities, not six products or subscriptions.

### Possible flow

```text
What am I trying to do?
        |
        v
Which capability fits the task?
(chat / image / research / productivity / coding / voice)
        |
        v
Can a tool I already use do it well enough?
       / \
     yes  no
      |    |
 test it   evaluate another tool
 safely         |
                v
     need -> trust -> data/access -> reliability -> cost/exit
```

### Why it may work for a beginner

It starts from an ordinary problem rather than a technology label. It also counters the false impression that AI literacy means collecting multiple subscriptions.

### Where it breaks down

Many tasks span categories. A research task may use chat, web search, document analysis and voice in one session. The diagram should therefore allow overlaps rather than imply a rigid taxonomy.

### Print/website suitability

- **Print:** strong candidate for the planned category diagram.
- **Website:** could become a simple interactive chooser, with dated example products kept off the printed page.

## 2. A six-category comparison table

### Suggested dimensions

| Capability | Useful for | Typical beginner task | Main thing to verify | Common risk/limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chat | drafting, explaining, brainstorming | clarify a concept | factual reliability | fluent errors |
| Image | generating/editing visuals | rough visual concept | licensing/accuracy | misleading details/bias |
| Research | finding/synthesising sourced information | compare background sources | source quality | citation does not guarantee support |
| Productivity | work inside documents/email/calendars | summarise a document | account/data access | broad access to existing information |
| Coding | explain/write/debug code | understand an error | execution/testing | insecure or incorrect code |
| Voice | hands-free interaction/transcription | speak instead of type | recording/data handling | transcription/recognition errors |

This is deliberately generic. The later writer should adapt wording to the existing chapter voice and earlier-chapter boundaries.

### Why it may work

It makes every category answer the same reader questions instead of giving six disconnected mini-definitions.

### Where it breaks down

The categories overlap. A footnote/callout should make that explicit.

## 3. A two-layer evaluation method

The research supports a **short everyday check** and a **deeper check** before money, sensitive data, or connected accounts are involved.

### Short everyday check — editorial possibility

The exact final wording is for the chapter writer, but the memorable concepts are:

1. **Need:** What job am I trying to do?
2. **Trust:** Who made this, and am I in the genuine app/site?
3. **Data:** What am I about to give it?
4. **Result:** Can I judge or verify what it gives back?
5. **Cost:** What will it cost after the trial, and how do I leave?

### Deeper check — editorial possibility

Use when the reader is about to:

- pay for a recurring plan;
- upload private or sensitive information;
- connect email, cloud files, calendar, contacts or other accounts;
- install a browser extension;
- rely on the service for consequential work;
- let it take actions; or
- allow a child/teen to use it.

Additional questions:

- Is my content used to improve/train models?
- Is that optional and what is the default?
- How long is content retained?
- Can humans review it?
- Does deletion cover chats, memories, uploaded files and shared links?
- What permissions/account scopes are requested?
- Can I export my data/history?
- How is cancellation performed?
- Does uninstalling end billing? (Often not.)
- Is the age requirement appropriate?
- Does the tool meet relevant accessibility needs?
- For work: is it approved by the employer and does the account tier have different protections?

### Why the two-layer model is useful

A single giant checklist is unlikely to be remembered. A five-question first pass gives the reader a routine; the longer check appears only when the downside of a poor choice increases.

## 4. Evidence box: "Five privacy questions that are not the same question"

### Concept

Readers often collapse privacy into one binary: private/not private. Provider documentation shows separate controls and policies.

### Candidate distinctions

- **Training:** may my content be used to improve models?
- **Retention:** how long is it kept even if not used for training?
- **Memory/personalisation:** does the service extract and reuse information in future interactions?
- **Human review:** can authorised people review some interactions?
- **Sharing/access:** can connected services, recipients of shared links, third-party apps, or legal processes access copies?

A sixth dimension could be **deletion/export** if space permits.

### Why it works

It directly corrects misconceptions such as "training off means nothing is stored" and "deleting a chat deletes every copy" without requiring product names.

### Website extension

A current provider-by-provider matrix could show how each service answers those questions, dated and linked to primary policies.

---

## 5. Evidence box: "Official app store does not mean risk-free"

### Research basis

Documented fleeceware/fake chatbot apps reached official app stores; a malicious AI-themed browser extension was distributed through an official extension store before removal.

### Durable lesson

Platform review is a useful layer of protection, not a substitute for checking the developer, permissions, subscription terms and independent reputation.

### Caution

Do not imply official stores are generally unsafe, and do not derive prevalence from a handful of security reports.

## 6. A subscription-maths micro-example

### Concept

Weekly pricing can obscure annual cost.

### Possible demonstration

Rather than use a real product price, show an invented arithmetic example clearly labelled as illustrative:

- `$9.99/week` sounds like a small purchase;
- over 52 weeks, that is about `$519/year`.

The chapter could tell the reader to convert weekly/monthly prices to an annual figure before deciding.

### Why it works

It gives a concrete defence against price framing without depending on any current vendor.

### Where it breaks down

Annualising assumes the user keeps the service for a full year and ignores discounts/cancellation. Say what the calculation is for: comparison, not prediction.

### Website opportunity

A subscription annualiser could convert weekly/monthly prices and optionally compare several services without ranking them.

## 7. A permission-proportionality exercise

### Reader exercise

Pick one AI-enabled app already installed and review its permissions.

Ask:

- Why would this feature need the microphone?
- Why would it need the photo library?
- Does it need all files or only selected files?
- Does a browser extension need access to every page, or only the site where it operates?
- Can access be changed later?

### Lesson

A permission is not suspicious merely because it is powerful; the question is whether the requested access is proportionate to the promised function.

### Website/screenshots

Platform-generic walkthroughs can be paired with up-to-date Apple, Android and browser instructions.

## 8. A "known-answer smoke test" demonstration

### Reader exercise

Before trusting a new tool for something unfamiliar, give it a small task the reader already understands and inspect:

- whether it follows the instruction;
- whether it invents facts;
- whether cited sources actually support the answer;
- whether it acknowledges uncertainty;
- whether it behaves consistently after a follow-up.

Then explicitly state the limitation: success on a familiar/easy task does **not** prove reliability on unfamiliar/harder tasks.

### Link to earlier chapters

This naturally reinforces Chapters 3 and 4 without reopening hallucination theory.

## 9. A source-check mini-demonstration

### Concept

"Research AI" can still produce a citation that exists but does not support the sentence attached to it.

### Exercise

For one sourced answer:

1. open one cited source;
2. locate the underlying claim;
3. check whether the source says the same thing;
4. check its date and original context;
5. note whether the source is primary, independent or a company making a claim about itself.

### Why it works

It converts the abstract warning "AI can make things up" into an actionable research habit.

## 10. A clean-exit checklist

### Concept

Tool evaluation should include leaving, not only joining.

### Editorial ingredients

- cancel the recurring subscription;
- export anything worth keeping;
- delete chats/files/account if desired and understand what deletion covers;
- remove saved memories/personalisation where separate;
- revoke connected-account access;
- remove browser-extension permissions;
- uninstall the app/extension;
- check the next statement/billing cycle.

### Useful platform fact

Google Play's support documentation explicitly says uninstalling an app does not cancel its subscription. That is a strong generic lesson even if the product name never appears in the book.

## 11. Parent/teen evaluation card

### Concept

For a child or teenager, a parent's evaluation needs extra questions beyond ordinary app legitimacy.

### Candidate dimensions

- minimum age / parental consent;
- whether the service behaves like a tool or a relationship/companion;
- what personal information it encourages the child to share;
- whether chats can become sexual, violent, self-harm-related or otherwise inappropriate;
- reporting/blocking/parental controls;
- memory and emotional-personalisation features;
- whether the child understands that the system is generated software, not a confidant with human judgement.

### Research basis

Use eSafety's Australian 2026 child research and current companion-service transparency work. Avoid converting survey findings into claims about every product.

### Scope boundary

Keep this concise in Chapter 13. Extended family guidance belongs on the companion website or a dedicated resource.

---

## 12. Accessibility evaluation card

### Concept

"Can I use it?" is as important as "is it capable?"

### Candidate checks

- keyboard/screen-reader compatibility;
- voice input/output;
- captions/transcripts;
- adjustable text/display;
- image/scene description;
- language support;
- exportable text/transcripts;
- whether critical descriptions are clearly caveated as fallible.

### Research basis

Vision Australia material plus platform accessibility documentation demonstrates both genuine usefulness and limitations.

### Caution

Never present generated visual description as a replacement for orientation/mobility or other safety-critical aids when the provider itself warns against that use.

## 13. "Capability, access, data, payment" framework

A useful cross-cutting editorial model is to separate four questions that advertising often bundles together:

1. **Capability:** Can the system do the task?
2. **Access:** What permissions/accounts/data must it reach to do it?
3. **Data treatment:** What happens to what it receives?
4. **Payment:** What does it cost now, later, and to exit?

### Why it may work

A tool can be highly capable but require excessive access for the reader's use case. A free tool may have acceptable data handling; a paid tool may still retain data. An official product may still be poor value. Separating these dimensions prevents shortcut reasoning.

## 14. Analogy: a toolkit, with limits

### Possible analogy

An AI toolkit is closer to a household/toolbox than a collection hobby: choose tools for jobs you actually have; one multipurpose tool may cover several jobs; specialised tools can be worthwhile when the job demands it.

### Why it works

It supports the chapter title and anti-catalogue philosophy.

### Where it breaks down

Physical tools do not normally ingest personal information, change their terms, learn from use, connect to accounts, or bill monthly. The analogy should be brief and explicitly limited.

---

## 15. Analogy: choosing a service, not buying a permanent appliance

### Possible analogy

Many AI products are more like choosing a mobile/streaming/cloud service than purchasing a once-and-done object: price, terms, features and retention can change; leaving may require exporting data and cancelling billing.

### Why it works

It explains why ongoing evaluation matters.

### Where it breaks down

Some AI features run locally/on-device and some software is not subscription-based. Do not imply every tool is a cloud service.

## 16. Evidence boxes worth considering

Possible compact evidence callouts for the chapter writer:

- **Australian children and AI:** selected eSafety findings, clearly identified as a 2026 survey of 1,950 Australian 10–17-year-olds.
- **Subscription cancellation:** selected CPRC result, clearly identified as consumer research broader than AI.
- **Privacy:** OAIC's caution around entering personal, especially sensitive, information into publicly available generative AI.
- **Official-store risk:** one documented fleeceware example, without prevalence language.
- **Changing products:** one generic sentence supported by documented rebrands/model retirements rather than a named list in print.

The strongest evidence box is one that changes what the reader does, not merely one with a dramatic statistic.

## 17. Screenshot opportunities — companion website rather than print

Because interfaces change rapidly, screenshots are best maintained online. Useful walkthroughs:

1. finding a service's training/improvement toggle;
2. locating retention/deletion documentation;
3. reviewing app permissions on iOS/Android;
4. reviewing browser-extension permissions;
5. checking which third-party accounts are connected;
6. finding app-store subscriptions and cancellation controls;
7. distinguishing uninstall from cancellation;
8. exporting/deleting account data;
9. identifying the genuine developer/publisher entry in an app store;
10. checking whether a shared conversation link is still active.

Every screenshot should display an **as-of date** and a link to the current first-party instructions.

## 18. Companion-site interactive ideas

### A. "Check this AI app" walkthrough

A guided form asks the reader for no sensitive data; it prompts them to inspect:

- developer/company;
- official website/store listing;
- purpose;
- permissions;
- data policy;
- billing period and annualised cost;
- cancellation route;
- export/deletion;
- age/accessibility fit.

It should return questions to investigate, **not a safety score or endorsement**.

### B. Current capability/category guide

Maintain dated examples of products that currently span multiple categories. Purpose: illustrate convergence, not rank them.

### C. Privacy-setting cards

For major services, maintain dated links to:

- training controls;
- retention;
- memory/personalisation;
- temporary/private modes;
- export/deletion;
- business-versus-consumer terms.

### D. Subscription-cost normaliser

Convert weekly/monthly/yearly advertised prices to comparable annual/monthly equivalents. Include taxes/credits caveat.

### E. Permission audit guide

Platform-specific current screenshots for iOS, Android and mainstream browsers.

### F. Change log

Track meaningful changes to provider privacy/data practices, product rebrands/retirements, and Australian consumer/privacy rules. This turns the website into the current layer while the book carries durable principles.

## 20. Suggested editorial sequence (not chapter prose)

A particularly coherent route through the approved structure would be:

1. **Start with the task, not the brand.**
2. Explain the six capabilities and immediately state that one product may cover several.
3. Ask whether an existing tool already covers the need.
4. Introduce the short evaluation method.
5. Expand privacy/access/cost only when stakes rise.
6. Demonstrate low-stakes testing.
7. Show documented traps: fake app/site/extension, deceptive subscription, excessive access.
8. Add Australia-specific recourse/context without turning into legal advice.
9. Finish with literacy over loyalty, qualified by: staying with a useful service is perfectly rational; literacy is the ability to reassess it.

This sequence preserves the plan while making the chapter more practical and evidence-led.
