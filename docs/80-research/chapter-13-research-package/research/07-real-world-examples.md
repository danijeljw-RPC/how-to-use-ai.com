# 07 — Selected Real-World Examples

This file contains **18 stronger examples** selected for practical usefulness. They are not all intended for the printed chapter. Named products/cases are primarily evidence from which the writer can derive generic lessons; current product-specific detail is better suited to the companion website.

## Example 1 — A familiar AI product changes name

**Category:** product change / durability of tool lists  
**What happened:** Google renamed Bard to Gemini in February 2024.  
**Source:** Google, 2024-02-08: https://blog.google/intl/en-africa/products/explore-get-answers/bard-becomes-gemini-try-ultra-10-and-a-new-mobile-app-today/  
**What it demonstrates:** a product can remain active while its public name changes.  
**General lesson without naming product:** printed lists of AI brands can age even if the underlying service survives.  
**Why it matters:** directly grounds the chapter's refusal to become a catalogue.  
**Plan fit:** Introduction — Categories, Not a Catalogue; Core Takeaway.  
**Print suitability:** Yes, preferably generic ("a major assistant rebranded within a year of launch").  
**Website suitability:** Yes, named and dated.  
**Recreatable check:** Search a current provider's changelog/history.  
**Caveat:** a rebrand does not mean the product disappeared.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Low as a historical example.

## Example 3 — Turning off training does not delete chat history

**Category:** privacy / misconception  
**What happened:** OpenAI's current Data Controls documentation says switching off model-improvement use prevents new conversations being used for training, while saved chats can remain in history.  
**Source:** OpenAI Help, accessed 2026-10-01: https://help.openai.com/en/articles/7730893-data-controls-in-chatgpt  
**What it demonstrates:** training and storage are separate concepts.  
**General lesson:** "don't train on this" does not mean "don't store this".  
**Plan fit:** How to Evaluate; Experimenting Safely; Myth vs Reality.  
**Print suitability:** Yes, generic.  
**Website suitability:** Yes, with screenshots/current setting path.  
**Reader can recreate:** Yes, compare provider training and retention pages.  
**Caveat:** settings change; do not hard-code menu paths in print.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Settings yes; concept no.

## Example 4 — Deleting a chat may not delete a separately saved file

**Category:** privacy / deletion misconception  
**What happened:** OpenAI's current retention documentation says a file saved separately in Library is not deleted merely because the chat that used it is deleted.  
**Source:** https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8983778-chat-and-file-retention-in-chatgpt  
**What it demonstrates:** an AI account can contain several related data objects with different lifecycles.  
**General lesson:** when leaving a tool, check chats, files, projects, memories and shared links separately.  
**Plan fit:** Evaluation; safe experimentation; clean exit.  
**Print suitability:** Yes, generic.  
**Website suitability:** Yes, product-specific walkthrough.  
**Caveat:** Library availability and behaviour can change.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Specific feature yes.

**Plan fit:** privacy/data; evaluation.

**Website suitability:** Named detail yes.

## Example 7 — Consumer and commercial data terms differ

**Category:** privacy / trade-off  
**What happened:** Anthropic's current consumer documentation permits model-improvement use under specified opt-in/safety-review conditions, while its commercial documentation says commercial inputs/outputs are not used to train models by default. Similar consumer/business distinctions exist at other providers.  
**Sources:**
- Consumer: https://privacy.anthropic.com/en/articles/10023580-is-my-data-used-for-model-training
- Commercial: https://privacy.anthropic.com/en/articles/7996868-is-my-data-used-for-model-training
**What it demonstrates:** one brand can have materially different rules by account/product type.  
**General lesson:** check the exact product/tier you are using; do not generalise from a personal account to a work account.  
**Plan fit:** privacy; work-use reminder.  
**Print suitability:** Yes, generic.  
**Website suitability:** Yes.  
**Caveat:** business terms may be contract-specific.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Exact terms yes.

## Example 8 — A legal hold overrides normal deletion expectations

**Category:** privacy / legal response  
**What happened:** In 2025 litigation, OpenAI was required to preserve some consumer/API content beyond its usual retention. An October 2025 update said the forward indefinite-retention obligation had ended, while limited historical April–September 2025 data remained preserved.  
**Source:** OpenAI, updated 2025-10-22: https://openai.com/index/response-to-nyt-data-demands/  
**What it demonstrates:** legal obligations can override ordinary retention/deletion rules.  
**General lesson:** provider deletion promises usually contain legal/security exceptions; deletion is not absolute against court/legal duties.  
**Plan fit:** privacy and data in practice.  
**Print suitability:** Generic principle yes; case could be a short evidence box.  
**Website suitability:** Yes.  
**Caveat:** source is the litigant/provider's description of the litigation; if used prominently, corroborate with court reporting/documentation close to publication.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Case status can change; historical principle remains.

## Example 9 — Shared AI conversations appeared in search results

**Category:** privacy / sharing  
**What happened:** In 2025 a discoverability option for shared ChatGPT conversations resulted in some shared pages being indexed/searchable. OpenAI removed the feature. Current shared-link documentation states shared pages are not intended for indexing but warns that anyone with a personal-account shared link can access/forward it.  
**Sources:**
- Current policy: https://help.openai.com/en/articles/7925741-sharing-conversations-and-scheduled-tasks-in-chatgpt
- 2025 reporting: https://www.searchenginejournal.com/openai-is-pulling-shared-chatgpt-chats-from-google-search/552671/
**What it demonstrates:** sharing and privacy are separate from normal private chat storage.  
**General lesson:** treat share links as content disclosed to anyone who gets the link; "not indexed" is not access control.  
**Plan fit:** privacy; safe experimentation; misconception.  
**Print suitability:** Strong generic example if carefully worded.  
**Website suitability:** Strong named case.  
**Caveat:** do not imply all private chats were indexed; the incident concerned shared/discoverable links.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Historical case stable; current link controls can change.

## Example 10 — Official app stores can still contain poor-value/deceptive AI apps

**What it demonstrates:** app-store presence reduces some installation friction/risk but is not a guarantee of identity/value/fair subscription design.  
**General lesson:** verify developer identity and subscription price even in an official store.  
**Plan fit:** scam/subscription traps.  
**Print suitability:** Yes, generic/historical.  
**Website suitability:** Yes.  
**Caveat:** security-vendor report; selected examples are not prevalence.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Product names yes; pattern no.

## Example 11 — A working AI extension can still steal account access

**Sources:**

**What it demonstrates:** functionality and safety are separate; an extension can do what it promises while also abusing access.  
**General lesson:** browser extensions deserve scrutiny because their permissions may expose browsing/account data.  
**Plan fit:** scam/permissions/safe experimentation.  
**Print suitability:** Generic warning plus one historical case.  
**Website suitability:** Yes.  
**Caveat:** older incident; seek a fresher case if available at publication.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Case old but pattern durable.

## Example 13 — Australian subscription cancellation friction is widespread

**General lesson:** check cancellation and renewal before signing up, especially for trials.  
**Plan fit:** evaluation; subscription traps.  
**Print suitability:** Strong Australian evidence.  
**Website suitability:** Yes.  
**Caveat:** covers subscriptions generally, not AI subscriptions specifically.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Moderate; still useful as baseline.

## Example 16 — Australian children share sensitive information with AI

**Category:** children / privacy / safe experimentation  
**What happened:** eSafety's 2026 representative survey of 1,950 Australian children aged 10–17 found 78% had used AI assistants, 8% AI companions; among users of assistants/companions, 32% reported sharing personal or potentially sensitive information and 20% reported potentially inappropriate/harmful interactions.  
**Source:** https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/talking-to-machines-childrens-experiences-with-ai-assistants-and-companions  
**What it demonstrates:** children may use assistants for personal/social purposes even when the product is positioned as functional.  
**General lesson:** parents should evaluate age suitability, privacy and relational design, not just the app's advertised task.  
**Plan fit:** safe experimentation; vulnerable readers.  
**Print suitability:** Very strong Australian example.  
**Website suitability:** Yes.  
**Recreatable check:** parent/carer can review current terms/privacy/safety settings with child.  
**Caveat:** survey reports self-reported experiences; distinguish assistants from companions and note their boundaries blur.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Statistics remain historical evidence; current services/safeguards change.

## Example 17 — AI can provide accessibility value while warning that it can be wrong

**Category:** accessibility / trade-off  
**What happened:** Current Google accessibility documentation describes Guided Vision as providing camera-based audio descriptions/object/text assistance while explicitly warning it can make mistakes and should not be used as a mobility/navigation or obstacle-detection aid. Vision Australia also demonstrates/monitors AI-enabled adaptive technologies for blind/low-vision users.  
**Sources:**
- Google Guided Vision: https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/18365638?hl=en
- Vision Australia webinar: https://www.visionaustralia.org/community/news/2026-06-23/accessible-writing-tools-and-technology-webinar-recording
**What it demonstrates:** a real benefit and a real limitation can coexist.  
**General lesson:** choose AI accessibility features for the task, but maintain non-AI safety methods where errors could cause harm.  
**Plan fit:** inclusion/accessibility; evaluation.  
**Print suitability:** Strong example.  
**Website suitability:** Yes.  
**Caveat:** product capabilities change; do not imply one product represents all accessibility AI.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Product specifics yes.

## Example 18 — More connected AI features can require a reduced-risk mode

**Category:** security / agentic tools / permissions  
**What happened:** OpenAI introduced Lockdown Mode in 2026 for elevated-risk users, restricting/disabling certain connected capabilities to reduce exposure to malicious content and prompt-injection-like attack paths.  
**Source:** https://openai.com/index/introducing-lockdown-mode-and-elevated-risk-labels-in-chatgpt/  
**What it demonstrates:** action-taking/connected AI has a different risk profile from isolated chat.  
**General lesson:** the more an assistant can read and do, the more valuable narrow permissions and confirmation controls become.  
**Plan fit:** safe experimentation; evaluation of permissions.  
**Print suitability:** Generic principle yes; named feature likely website-only.  
**Website suitability:** Strong current example.  
**Caveat:** provider's own security design/description; deeper agent security belongs in Chapter 14/later books.  
**Likely to date quickly:** Feature implementation yes; principle no.
