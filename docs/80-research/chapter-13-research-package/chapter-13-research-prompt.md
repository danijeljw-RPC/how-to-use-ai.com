# Important note about the plan's constraints

The Chapter 13 plan makes two deliberate choices:

1. The chapter **must not name, rank, or recommend specific AI products** in the printed book, because products change, rebrand, get acquired, or disappear faster than a book's shelf life, and because the project's structure decision says the chapter "should not become a giant tool list."

**This research task deliberately supersedes the second point, but respects the first.**

The chapter's practical advice (what to check before trying a tool, how to experiment safely, how to spot scams and subscription traps) is only trustworthy if it is grounded in how real tools, real privacy settings, real scams, and real consumer protections actually work. Therefore:

- research real, representative products, settings, policies, and scam cases in order to **derive durable, product-independent lessons**;
- for every product-specific finding, record the **general principle** it illustrates, so the printed chapter can state the principle without naming the product;

- record the **date** of every product term, price, setting, or policy, because these change frequently;

# How to evaluate a new AI tool

The draft's evaluation checklist asks:

- What specific task do I need it for?
- Is there a free or low-cost way to try it first?
- What happens to the information I put into it?
- Is the pricing clear?
- Would I still want this if the free trial ended today?

Research this checklist rather than simply accepting it.

Investigate:

- consumer-protection guidance on evaluating apps and digital subscriptions (for example, from the ACCC, CHOICE, and international consumer bodies);
- privacy-regulator guidance on choosing and using AI products (for example, the OAIC's guidance on using commercially available AI products);
- published evaluations of AI apps' privacy and security practices by independent organisations;
- what questions experts recommend asking about data handling, including:
  - Is my data used to train the model?
  - Can I opt out, and is the opt-out on or off by default?
  - How long is my data kept?
  - Can humans at the company read my conversations?
  - Where is the data stored, and under which country's law?
  - Can I delete my data, and what does deletion actually cover?
  - Is my data shared with third parties or used for advertising?
  - Does the tool connect to my email, files, calendar, or other accounts, and what can it access?
- what questions matter about **accuracy and reliability** (for example, whether the tool shows sources, whether it separates facts from generated text, whether it warns about limitations);
- what questions matter about **the company** (who makes the tool, how long it has existed, whether contact and support details are genuine, whether it is a known developer);
- what questions matter about **age suitability** and children's use;
- what questions matter about **accessibility**;
- what questions matter about **commercial use** of outputs (Chapter 8 covers this for creative work; record only what Chapter 13 needs);
- and what questions matter for **work use** (Chapter 7 covers company policy; record only what Chapter 13 needs).

Research whether the checklist should be shorter (for memorability) or longer (for completeness), and whether a two-tier approach (a short everyday checklist and a longer checklist for paid or sensitive use) would work better.

Record these as research-backed editorial possibilities. Do not write the final checklist.

# Avoiding scams, fake apps, and subscription traps

The draft names five red flags:

1. Urgency-driven upsells
2. Thin wrappers around a free or cheap underlying service
3. Unclear pricing
4. Excessive permissions
5. Fake reviews or inflated claims

Research each, and research additional patterns.

## Scams and malicious products

Investigate:

- **fake AI apps** in app stores that imitate well-known AI products;
- **malicious browser extensions** posing as AI tools that steal account sessions, passwords, or data;
- **malware distributed through fake AI tool websites or advertisements** (for example, sites offering free AI video or image generation that deliver malicious software);
- **fake AI investment platforms and "AI trading bots"** (Chapter 9 may cover these under scams; record the boundary);
- **"get rich with AI" courses and schemes**;
- **phishing that imitates AI providers** (fake billing notices, fake account warnings);
- **fake customer-support numbers** for AI services;
- and **AI companion apps** that use manipulative design or collect sensitive data.

For each pattern, research documented cases, authoritative warnings (for example, from Scamwatch, the Australian Cyber Security Centre, app stores, security researchers, and international consumer regulators), and practical advice for recognising it.

## Subscription traps and dark patterns

Investigate:

- "fleeceware": apps with free trials that convert to expensive recurring charges, and security researchers' documentation of this in AI apps;
- weekly subscriptions that look cheap but cost more than annual plans;
- difficult cancellation processes;
- pre-ticked renewals and automatic upgrades;
- confusing credit or token systems where it is hard to know what a task will cost;
- usage limits that are not clearly disclosed;
- in-app purchases in AI apps aimed at children;
- and "dark patterns" in subscription design more generally.

Research the Australian consumer-protection position, including:

- the Australian Consumer Law and the ACCC's approach to subscription traps and misleading pricing;
- any proposed or enacted prohibition on unfair trading practices, including subscription traps, and its current status;
- how to cancel a subscription and dispute a charge (for example, through app stores or card providers);
- and consumer-organisation research on subscription traps.

Research comparable international developments (for example, "click to cancel" rules in the United States and their legal status, and UK and EU subscription rules) only where they help explain the issue.

## Thin wrappers

Research this concept carefully and fairly.

- Define what a "wrapper" is in plain language (a product built on top of another company's AI model).
- Research the fact that **many legitimate, useful products are built this way**, and that being a wrapper is not in itself a scam.
- Identify when a wrapper becomes a problem: charging significantly more than the underlying service without adding value, hiding which model it uses, making false claims about proprietary technology, or passing user data to other companies without clear disclosure.
- Research how a beginner could tell the difference, if at all.

Ensure the chapter can warn about thin wrappers **without implying that all AI start-ups or all apps built on another company's model are dishonest**.

## Excessive permissions

Research:

- what permissions AI apps and browser extensions commonly request;
- which permissions are reasonable for which features (for example, microphone access for a voice tool);
- which are red flags;
- and how to review and revoke permissions on common phone and computer platforms, described generically.

## Fake reviews and inflated claims

Research:

- fake reviews in app stores and on websites;
- regulatory action against fake reviews in Australia and elsewhere (for example, rules on fake and AI-generated reviews);
- how Chapter 11's evaluation method applies to product claims;
- and how to check a tool's reputation using independent sources.

Actively research the draft's claim that "most AI products aren't scams" and the statement that scams and traps are "common enough to be worth actively checking for." Find evidence that allows these statements to be made with appropriate precision, or suggest better phrasing.

# Source quality and bias

For important sources, note relevant limitations.

### AI provider's own policy or help page
Useful for:
- what the provider says it does with data;
- current settings.

Possible limitation:
- policies change;
- wording may be vague;
- policy is not the same as independent verification.

### Security vendor report
Useful for:
- emerging malicious apps and extensions.

Possible limitation:
- commercial interest in highlighting threats;
- detection counts are not prevalence.

### Consumer organisation
Useful for:
- independent testing;
- consumer-focused guidance.

Possible limitation:
- advocacy role;
- snapshot in time.

### App store or platform policy
Useful for:
- rules for developers;
- refund processes.

Possible limitation:
- rules are not always enforced.

### Government or regulator
Useful for:
- law;
- guidance;
- enforcement.

Possible limitation:
- proposals may not yet be law.

Do not discard a source because it has an interested party.

Instead, describe what it can and cannot establish.

This chapter's subject changes faster than any other in the book.

Clearly mark information that may date rapidly, including:

- product names and features;
- product categories and convergence;
- prices and tiers;
- privacy settings and defaults;
- data-retention periods;
- terms of service;
- scam patterns;
- app-store rules;
- consumer-law reforms;
- privacy-law reforms.

Include source dates and access dates.

Where useful, add:

> **Recheck before publication**

to research items likely to change.

For every time-sensitive item, also record the **durable principle** it illustrates, so the printed chapter can remain accurate after the specific detail changes.

# Quotations

Use direct quotations sparingly.

Prefer summarising a source's substantive finding.

Where a quotation is unusually useful (for example, exact wording from a privacy policy that illustrates a general lesson):

- preserve it exactly;
- keep it short;
- record the source and date;
- record the page/section where possible;
- and explain why the quotation matters.

# Do not invent evidence

Do not invent:

- citations;
- URLs;
- statistics;
- quotations;
- product terms;
- privacy settings;
- prices;
- scam cases;
- security incidents;
- regulatory actions;
- consumer-law provisions;
- examples;
- case studies.

If something cannot be verified reliably, state that clearly.

If sources conflict, record the disagreement.

# Starting leads to verify

The following leads are offered as **starting points only**. They come from the commissioning side's general awareness, not from verified research. Some may be inaccurate, incomplete, outdated, or superseded. Verify each against primary sources before relying on it, record its current status, and discard any that do not hold up.

- OAIC guidance on privacy and the use of commercially available AI products (2024).
- Scamwatch and Australian Cyber Security Centre warnings about fake AI apps and AI-themed scams.
- Security-research reports on "fleeceware" subscription apps, including AI chatbot apps.
- Reports of a malicious browser extension posing as a ChatGPT tool that hijacked Facebook business accounts (2023).
- Reports of malware distributed through fake AI video- and image-generator websites and advertisements (2024–2025).
- Mozilla Foundation's *Privacy Not Included* review of AI companion and "romantic" chatbot apps (2024).
- Incidents in which shared AI chat links became discoverable through search engines, and the provider's response.
- Court orders affecting retention of AI chat logs in US litigation.
- Changes to default training-data settings by major AI providers, and how users were notified.
- The ACCC's position on subscription traps and the Australian Government's consultation on an unfair trading practices prohibition.
- The US Federal Trade Commission's "click to cancel" rule and its later legal status.
- US Federal Trade Commission rules on fake reviews, including AI-generated reviews (2024).
- eSafety Commissioner guidance and codes relating to AI companions and children, and age limits in AI providers' terms.
- CHOICE (Australian consumer organisation) investigations of AI products or AI features.
- Published studies of what the general public actually uses AI chatbots for.
- Academic definitions of AI literacy (for example, Long and Magerko, CHI 2020) and UNESCO AI competency frameworks.
- The AI-literacy obligation in Article 4 of the EU AI Act.
- Examples of AI products discontinued or substantially changed within a short time, and of AI features bundled into existing subscriptions with price increases.
- On-device AI processing in major phone platforms and the privacy claims made for it.
- Prompt-injection risks in AI browser assistants and AI tools connected to email.

# Depth

The research can be extensive.

Do not artificially restrict it to the expected length of the eventual Chapter 13.

It is preferable for the chapter-writing AI to receive substantially more high-quality research than it ultimately uses.

Do not pad the package with repetitive information.

Prioritise:

1. accuracy;
2. primary sources (especially providers' own terms and regulators' own guidance);
3. recent evidence, with dates;
4. practical usefulness to beginners;
5. durable principles that survive product changes;
6. strong documented examples of scams, traps, and privacy issues;
7. Australian consumer and privacy protections;
8. protection of vulnerable readers;
9. clear separation of printed-book material from website material;
10. clear distinction between evidence and opinion;
11. material that gives the eventual writer genuine editorial choices.

---

# Research philosophy

Do not approach this task trying to prove that:

- AI tools are safe;
- AI tools are dangerous;
- free tools are enough;
- paid tools are necessary;
- AI companies can be trusted with your data;
- AI companies cannot be trusted with your data;
- consumers can protect themselves from everything;
- consumers are powerless.

Approach these as questions to investigate.

The research package should contain enough evidence for the later writer to say:

> Here are the kinds of AI tools that exist, and what each is for.

> Here is how to decide whether you need one at all.

> Here is what to check before you try or pay.

> Here is what happens to what you type.

> Here is how to spot a scam or a trap.

> Here is why understanding beats brand loyalty.

That is more valuable than producing a predetermined narrative.
