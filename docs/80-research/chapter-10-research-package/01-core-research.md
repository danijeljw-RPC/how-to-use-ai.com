# Core Research Mapped to the Chapter 10 Plan

## 1. Introduction — The Question Everyone Actually Wants Answered

The strongest opening is not a yes/no prediction but a distinction:

> AI affects **tasks first**, and jobs through the way those tasks are bundled, reorganised, priced and managed.

This is more accurate than asking whether an occupation is simply "safe" or "at risk."

The reader needs to understand three layers:

1. **Capability:** can an AI system perform or assist with a task?
2. **Adoption:** is an employer or worker actually using it?
3. **Labour-market effect:** does that use change hours, hiring, wages, headcount or job quality?

A large amount of public debate collapses these layers.

### What current evidence supports

As of 2026 there is no credible basis for saying that generative AI has produced economy-wide mass unemployment. A revised Stanford Digital Economy Lab analysis using ADP payroll data through June 2026 explicitly reports no widespread economy-wide displacement, while identifying a substantial divergence for workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations.

This is a useful framing because both statements can be true:
- aggregate effects remain limited;
- concentrated effects can already matter greatly to particular people.

Source:
- Stanford Digital Economy Lab, *Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence*, revised 12 Aug 2026.  
  https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/

## 2. Some Jobs Change, Some Disappear, Many Evolve, New Ones Emerge

The four-part framing is broadly defensible, but it should be presented as a **taxonomy of possible outcomes**, not as four outcomes already measured in equal amounts.

### Some jobs change

Strongly supported. Danish administrative-data research finds widespread workplace adoption and creation of new AI-related tasks without measurable average effects on earnings or hours in the first two years. This is direct evidence of work changing before aggregate labour outcomes move.

Source:
- Anders Humlum & Emilie Vestergaard, *Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI*, NBER WP 33777, revised Mar 2026.  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777

### Some jobs disappear

Historically well supported for particular occupations and tasks. For generative AI specifically, current evidence of whole occupations disappearing remains much thinner than evidence of reduced demand for some task categories.

The later chapter should be careful with "disappear." Better wording may be:
- positions are not refilled;
- hiring slows;
- contractor demand falls;
- a task category collapses;
- fewer workers are needed for the same output.

That is often how displacement appears before an occupation literally vanishes.

### Many jobs evolve

This is the best-supported present-tense description. It matches ILO and Jobs and Skills Australia findings that task transformation is more likely than complete occupational automation for most exposed work.

Sources:
- ILO, *Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure*, 20 May 2025.  
  https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure
- Jobs and Skills Australia, *Australia's AI Transition: Jobs, Skills and the Future of Work*, 2025.  
  https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study

### New jobs emerge

Historically very well supported; AI-specific evidence is real but should not be exaggerated.

David Autor and colleagues find that the majority of current U.S. employment is in job specialties introduced after 1940. The important qualification is that the composition of new work changed: since 1980, new work has been concentrated more in high-paid professional and lower-paid service work than in middle-paid production and clerical work.

Source:
- Autor, Chin, Salomons & Seegmiller, *New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940–2018*, NBER WP 30389.  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389

This does **not** prove that future AI-created work will absorb the people displaced by AI. New work may:
- appear in different places;
- require different education;
- arrive years later;
- go to a different generation of workers.

## 3. Historical Technology Shifts, Honestly

History supports two propositions simultaneously:

1. technological change can create new industries, tasks and occupations;
2. specific workers can suffer large and lasting losses even when the economy eventually gains.

A particularly strong empirical anchor is displaced-worker research. Jacobson, LaLonde and Sullivan found long-term earnings losses averaging about 25% per year among high-tenure workers displaced from distressed firms in their sample.

Source:
- Jacobson, LaLonde & Sullivan, *Earnings Losses of Displaced Workers*, American Economic Review 83(4), 1993.  
  Summary: https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v83y1993i4p685-709.html

This is ideal evidence for the planned **Watch Out** callout. It prevents the chapter from turning "history eventually creates work" into "displacement is harmless."

### Engels' pause

Robert Allen's account of the British Industrial Revolution shows a long period in which output per worker rose while real wages stagnated, before wages later rose with productivity.

Source:
- Robert C. Allen, *Engels' Pause: Technical Change, Capital Accumulation, and Inequality in the British Industrial Revolution*, 2009.  
  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eeh.2009.04.004

Editorial use: a simple demonstration that productivity gains and worker gains need not arrive together.

## 4. Augmentation vs. Replacement

The distinction is essential, but the current draft is too clean.

### Evidence for augmentation

Customer support:
- 5,179 agents;
- average productivity gain about 14%;
- gains around 34% for novice/lower-skill workers;
- minimal effects for highly experienced workers.

Source:
- Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, *Generative AI at Work*, QJE 2025 / NBER WP 31161.  
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161

Professional writing:
- 453 college-educated professionals;
- ChatGPT reduced completion time 40%;
- output quality rose 18%;
- lower-performing workers benefited more.

Source:
- Noy & Zhang, *Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence*, Science 381, 2023.  
  https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586

### Evidence that augmentation is not universal

Experienced open-source developers:
- 16 developers;
- 246 tasks in mature repositories they knew well;
- early-2025 AI tools increased completion time by 19%;
- participants nevertheless believed AI had made them faster.

Source:
- METR, *Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity*, 10 Jul 2025.  
  https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/

The study is narrow and should not be generalised to all software development. It is nevertheless an excellent antidote to "AI always makes users more productive."

### Real usage data

Anthropic's initial Economic Index classified 57% of Claude.ai interactions as augmentation and 43% as automation. But later data showed much higher automation shares in Claude Code and API usage.

Sources:
- Anthropic Economic Index, Feb 2025.  
  https://www.anthropic.com/news/the-anthropic-economic-index
- Anthropic, coding analysis, Apr 2025.  
  https://www.anthropic.com/news/impact-software-development
- Anthropic, geography/API analysis.  
  https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-geography

Implication: the draft should not say simply that augmentation is "more common today" without specifying dataset and interface. Automation appears more prevalent in agentic and API contexts.

## 5. A Practical Self-Assessment

The current effort-heavy versus judgement-heavy framework is useful as a beginner heuristic, but it needs two corrections.

### Correction 1: judgement is not binary

AI can assist some judgement tasks, especially where judgement depends on:
- pattern recognition;
- comparison against explicit criteria;
- synthesis of large documents;
- generating alternatives.

"Judgement-heavy" should therefore mean **greater dependence on contextual responsibility, tacit knowledge, trust, stakes or accountability**, not "AI cannot help."

### Correction 2: effort-heavy tasks may be apprenticeship

Junior work often contains apparently routine tasks because those tasks teach:
- domain vocabulary;
- error patterns;
- institutional context;
- how to distinguish normal from unusual cases.

Automating all of these tasks can create a career-ladder problem.

A stronger self-assessment should ask about:
- repeatability;
- stakes of error;
- need for physical presence;
- relationship/trust;
- accountability;
- tacit/contextual knowledge;
- whether the task is a training mechanism;
- whether AI output is easy or difficult to verify.

See `07-adaptability-and-self-assessment.md`.

## 6. Why Adaptability Matters More Than Any Specific Skill

This remains a good chapter-level takeaway, with a necessary qualification:

> Adaptability improves a person's options, but does not guarantee protection from structural displacement.

The chapter should avoid implying:
- displaced people failed to adapt;
- retraining always works;
- every worker has equal access to time, money, education or mobility.

The better framing is:
- individual adaptability matters;
- employer choices matter;
- labour-market institutions matter;
- timing and geography matter;
- policy can alter who bears transition costs.

### Practical actions that fit Chapter 10

Without drifting into Chapter 12:
- identify which tasks are already changing;
- learn employer AI policy;
- test AI on low-risk work and measure whether it actually helps;
- retain the ability to verify outputs;
- notice which tasks build judgement rather than merely consume time;
- strengthen domain knowledge and relationships;
- watch hiring patterns in the reader's own field rather than relying on viral predictions.

## 7. Myth vs Reality — evidence-backed candidates

### Myth: "If 40% of jobs are exposed, 40% of jobs will disappear."
Reality: exposure is capability overlap. It does not encode adoption, organisational choice, complementarity, demand effects or task bundling.

### Myth: "AI makes everyone more productive."
Reality: strong positive effects have been measured in some writing and support tasks, while experienced developers in one RCT were slower.

### Myth: "There is no evidence of job effects yet."
Reality: there is no broad mass-displacement signal, but there is emerging subgroup and platform evidence.

### Myth: "History proves it will all work out."
Reality: history shows both long-run adaptation and severe transition costs.

### Myth: "Only low-skilled work is exposed."
Reality: generative AI exposure is often high in educated, digitised, professional work; this differs from many earlier automation waves.

## 8. Core Takeaway — recommended factual qualification

The plan's line:

> People using AI will often outperform people refusing to use it.

can survive, but the research suggests the surrounding text should make clear that:

- "often" is doing important work;
- it applies most strongly where AI is capable on the relevant task;
- using AI badly can reduce performance;
- professional restrictions or confidentiality can make non-use rational;
- individual productivity does not automatically produce wage growth or job security.

A more defensible interpretation is:

> Where AI performs well on a task and can be used safely, workers who learn to use it effectively may gain a productivity or capability advantage. That advantage is neither universal nor equivalent to career security.

The final wording remains an editorial decision.
