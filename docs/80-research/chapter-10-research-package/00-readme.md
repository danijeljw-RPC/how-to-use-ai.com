# Chapter 10 Research Package — Will AI Replace Jobs?

**Research date:** 2026-10-01  
**Book:** *How-To-Use-AI.com* — Book 1  
**Chapter:** 10 — *Will AI Replace Jobs?*

## Purpose

This package supports a later AI system writing Chapter 10. It does **not** write the chapter.

The approved Chapter 10 plan remains authoritative for scope, reader state, learning outcomes and structure. The existing chapter draft is treated as a template to test, not as a factual authority.

The research was commissioned specifically to strengthen four weaknesses that a conceptual treatment alone would leave exposed:

1. confusing **AI exposure** with observed job loss;
2. treating task-level productivity experiments as evidence about whole careers;
3. using history as automatic reassurance;
4. presenting "adaptability" as an individual solution without acknowledging institutions, employers and transition costs.

## Bottom line from the evidence

The best-supported high-level position is neither "AI will take all jobs" nor "AI will merely help everyone do their existing jobs."

Current evidence supports a more specific account:

- Generative AI can substantially improve speed and quality on some bounded tasks.
- Gains vary by worker, task and workflow; AI can also make people slower or worse when the task lies outside the model's effective frontier.
- Exposure is a measure of what technology *could plausibly affect*, not an estimate of jobs that will disappear.
- Jobs are bundles of tasks. The same occupation can contain automatable, augmentable and distinctly human tasks.
- Administrative data does not currently show economy-wide mass displacement caused by generative AI.
- There is, however, credible emerging evidence of labour-market effects in specific groups and markets, especially young workers in highly AI-exposed U.S. occupations and some categories of online freelance work.
- Historical technological change has created new work, but transition costs can be severe and persistent for displaced workers and regions.
- Australia-specific evidence currently leans toward job transformation and augmentation rather than wholesale replacement, while adoption remains uneven.
- "People using AI will often outperform people refusing to use it" is defensible only as a **conditional** claim. It should not be written as a universal law.

## Critical terminology

**Task** — a unit of work performed within a job.

**Job / occupation** — a bundle of tasks plus responsibilities, relationships, accountability, context and often physical presence.

**Exposure** — the degree to which AI capabilities overlap with tasks in an occupation. Exposure does not by itself tell us whether a worker gains, loses, changes tasks or is displaced.

**Augmentation** — AI assists a person, with the person retaining an important role in directing, interpreting, checking or acting.

**Automation** — AI performs a task that a person previously performed. Automation can occur without eliminating a whole job.

**Replacement / displacement** — labour demand for a worker or role falls enough that a person, position or category of work disappears.

**Complementarity** — AI increases the productivity or value of human work.

**Substitution** — AI reduces demand for a particular human contribution.

## Major cautions for the eventual writer

Do not write:

- "25% of jobs will be replaced" when the source measures exposure.
- "AI makes workers 40% faster" without naming the task and study population.
- "AI has not affected jobs" simply because aggregate unemployment has not surged.
- "AI is already causing mass unemployment" when current broad evidence does not support it.
- "Technology always creates more jobs than it destroys."
- "Prompt engineer is the new career everyone should learn."
- lists of "safe" and "doomed" professions.
