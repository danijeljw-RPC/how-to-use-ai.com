# Tasks, Jobs, Exposure, Augmentation and Substitution

## Why the task/job distinction matters

A job title is too coarse a unit for thinking about automation.

Two people with the same title can perform different mixes of:
- routine and non-routine work;
- cognitive and manual work;
- production and relationship work;
- individual and team work;
- standardised and context-specific tasks.

This is why occupation-level automation estimates can overstate replacement risk.

## Classic task-based correction

Frey and Osborne's influential 2013 study estimated that about 47% of U.S. employment was in occupations at high risk of computerisation. Its method classified whole occupations.

Arntz, Gregory and Zierahn later used a task-based approach across 21 OECD countries and estimated about 9% of jobs as automatable on average, arguing that workers within the same occupation perform heterogeneous tasks.

Sources:
- Frey & Osborne, 2013: https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/the-future-of-employment
- Arntz, Gregory & Zierahn, OECD 2016: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-risk-of-automation-for-jobs-in-oecd-countries_5jlz9h56dvq7-en.html

This contrast is valuable because it teaches the reader how methodology changes a headline.

## What AI exposure indices actually measure

Exposure indices usually map AI capabilities to occupational tasks. They answer something like:

> How much of the task content of this occupation overlaps with things current or plausible AI systems can do?

They do **not** directly answer:
- whether employers adopt the technology;
- whether it is cheaper than labour after integration costs;
- whether regulation allows its use;
- whether customers accept it;
- whether human review remains mandatory;
- whether demand expands after productivity increases;
- whether workers are laid off.

### Eloundou et al. ("GPTs are GPTs")

The study estimated:
- about 80% of U.S. workers could have at least 10% of tasks affected;
- about 19% could have at least 50% of tasks affected.

The authors explicitly did not predict adoption timelines.

Source:
https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130

### ILO refined index

2025 ILO/NASK work estimates:
- one in four workers globally in occupations with some GenAI exposure;
- 3.3% of global employment in the highest exposure category;
- exposure higher in high-income countries;
- women more represented in the highest exposure category, partly because of clerical employment patterns.

The ILO's stated interpretation is that transformation is more likely than redundancy for most occupations because tasks continue to require human input.

Source:
https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure

### Explicit 2026 caution from ILO

The ILO published a dedicated explanation in April 2026 warning that exposure indicators should not, on their own, be interpreted as predictions of job losses or labour-market outcomes.

Source:
https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/new-ilo-brief-explains-what-ai-exposure-indicators-reveal-about-jobs

This is highly suitable for a plain-English evidence box.

## Complementarity versus substitution

### Complementarity

AI raises the productivity or value of a worker.

Example:
A support agent receives suggested solutions and can resolve more customer issues while remaining responsible for the interaction.

### Substitution

AI replaces a human contribution.

Example:
A company routes a category of routine customer enquiries directly to an automated assistant and no longer needs the same volume of agents for those enquiries.

The same technology can be complementary in one workflow and substitutive in another.

## Productivity can reduce headcount without full automation

The augmentation/replacement binary misses a third mechanism:

1. a worker remains essential;
2. AI makes each worker much more productive;
3. output demand does not grow enough to absorb the productivity gain;
4. the organisation needs fewer workers.

No task needs to be "fully automated" for labour demand to fall.

Conversely, if lower prices or higher quality greatly expand demand, productivity gains can support more employment. This is why technical capability alone cannot predict employment.

## Bundled tasks

Jobs that bundle many tasks can survive even when individual components are automated.

A manager may:
- draft reports;
- schedule;
- evaluate staff;
- negotiate;
- manage conflict;
- interpret incomplete information;
- take legal or organisational responsibility.

AI can affect several components without removing the bundle.

This supports the approved chapter's structural framing but argues for replacing "AI does X / humans do judgement" with a spectrum.

## Generative AI complicates older automation categories

Earlier automation research often focused on routine work. Generative AI reaches some non-routine cognitive work:
- drafting;
- coding;
- design ideation;
- analysis;
- summarisation;
- language tasks.

That is one reason exposure is often high in better-paid, educated work.

The correct conclusion is not "professional jobs are unsafe." It is that the old shorthand "automation hits only routine low-skill work" is no longer sufficient.

## Effort-heavy versus judgement-heavy: mapping to research

The book's framework can be retained as an accessible simplification.

### Effort-heavy roughly overlaps with
- repeatable production;
- first drafts;
- transformation of information;
- summarisation;
- standard classification;
- generating options.

### Judgement-heavy roughly overlaps with
- responsibility under uncertainty;
- trust;
- contextual interpretation;
- stakes;
- tacit knowledge;
- social negotiation;
- physical action in an unstructured environment.

### Where the framework breaks

1. AI can increasingly assist with parts of judgement.
2. Some "effort" tasks are required to learn professional judgement.
3. Tasks vary within occupations.
4. Physical tasks may be AI-exposed indirectly through scheduling, diagnostics or robotics.
5. employers may redesign a job rather than preserve the existing bundle.

## Recommended beginner distinction

A useful sequence for readers:

**Could AI touch this task?** → exposure  
**Is it being used?** → adoption  
**Does it help the worker?** → augmentation/complementarity  
**Does it reduce human work needed?** → automation/substitution  
**Does that change hiring, hours, pay or positions?** → labour-market impact

This sequence would make an excellent diagram if the chapter permits one.
