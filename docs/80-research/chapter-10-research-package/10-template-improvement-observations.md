# Template Improvement Observations

This file does not rewrite Chapter 10. It identifies evidence-based changes the later writing system should consider.

---

## Observation 1 — opening is balanced but under-evidenced

**Current topic:** rejection of simple yes/no answer.  
**Weakness:** correct tone, but no empirical anchor.  
**Research suggests:** immediately distinguish task exposure, adoption and observed labour-market effect.  
**Possible evidence:** Stanford 2026 payroll data: no widespread displacement alongside young-worker divergence.  
**Impact:** depth, factual grounding.

---

## Observation 2 — copywriter example is plausible but generic

**Current topic:** copywriter shifts from drafting to directing/editing AI.  
**Weakness:** reads as hypothetical.  
**Research suggests:** freelance-market evidence can show that some content-generation demand actually declined while other planning/review tasks behaved differently.  
**Possible replacement:** Fiverr or broader freelance-platform study.  
**Impact:** evidence, usefulness.

---

## Observation 3 — "basic transcription/simple translation" needs care

**Weakness:** intuitively plausible, but it risks becoming an unsourced "doomed roles" statement.  
**Research suggests:** speak about **task categories** and observed platform/industry changes.  
**Impact:** accuracy and alignment with plan's no-doomed-professions rule.

---

## Observation 4 — manager example overstates the human/AI boundary

**Current claim:** difficult conversations, judgement and trust are not meaningfully AI-assisted.  
**Weakness:** too categorical. AI can assist preparation, scenario generation, summarisation and decision support even if the human remains responsible.  
**Research suggests:** define judgement-heavy as higher-context, higher-accountability work, not AI-free work.  
**Impact:** accuracy.

---

## Observation 5 — "new role checking AI content" is too anecdotal

**Weakness:** no evidence of prevalence; could imply a major new occupation rather than a task added to existing roles.  
**Research suggests:** Danish evidence identifies new tasks involving AI oversight and integration.  
**Impact:** precision.

---

## Observation 6 — history paragraph is far too compressed

**Current topic:** agriculture, industrial automation, computing.  
**Weakness:** risks the "it all worked out eventually" trope despite the Watch Out box.  
**Research suggests:** include one concrete positive mechanism and one concrete cost:
- new work after 1940;
- long-run displaced-worker earnings losses or robot local-labour effects.
**Impact:** balance, memorability.

---

## Observation 7 — augmentation/replacement binary is incomplete

**Weakness:** ignores "augmentation that reduces headcount."  
**Research suggests:** explain that fewer people may be needed even if humans remain in the loop.  
**Impact:** economic precision.

---

## Observation 8 — "large share of current AI use is augmentation" requires qualification

**Weakness:** provider-specific data changes by interface.  
**Evidence:** Anthropic's Claude.ai usage leaned 57% augmentation, but Claude Code and API use skewed much more toward automation.  
**Impact:** factual accuracy.

---

## Observation 9 — self-assessment is too binary

**Weakness:** effort vs judgement alone misses:
- verification difficulty;
- physical presence;
- stakes;
- trust;
- repeatability;
- apprenticeship.
**Research suggests:** retain the simple framework but add a small second layer of questions.  
**Impact:** practical usefulness.

---

## Observation 10 — adaptability paragraph risks individualising structural change

**Weakness:** "people who adapt tend to end up ahead" may imply displacement is a personal failure.  
**Research suggests:** explicitly state that adaptability expands options but employers, labour-market conditions and policy shape outcomes.  
**Impact:** fairness and historical accuracy.

---

## Observation 11 — core Key Idea is too close to a universal productivity claim

**Current:** "People using AI will often outperform people refusing to use it."  
**Weakness:** defensible only conditionally.  
**Evidence:** METR shows a case where experienced users were slower.  
**Research suggests:** surrounding text must define "often" and task fit.  
**Impact:** credibility.

---

## Observation 12 — chapter currently misses entry-level effects

**Weakness:** one of the most important 2025–26 evidence developments is absent.  
**Research suggests:** brief section or evidence box on the unresolved career-ladder question.  
**Evidence:** Stanford ADP study; 41-country working paper; novice productivity field study.  
**Impact:** relevance to students/parents/young readers.

---

## Observation 13 — chapter currently speaks mostly to desk workers

**Weakness:** examples are copywriter, transcription/translation, manager, content checking.  
**Research suggests:** add at least one care/trade example showing mixed task exposure.  
**Impact:** target-audience coverage.

---

## Observation 14 — no Australian evidence

**Weakness:** missed opportunity for initial audience.  
**Research suggests:** JSA + ABS + Fair Work evidence box.  
**Impact:** localisation and credibility.

---

## Observation 15 — no explicit exposure-vs-impact explanation

**Weakness:** readers are likely to encounter headlines such as "40% of jobs affected."  
**Research suggests:** one paragraph/table explaining exposure is not job loss.  
**Impact:** AI literacy, resilience against Chapter 11-style hype.

---

## Observation 16 — "new jobs emerge" needs a distribution caveat

**Weakness:** it can sound as though new jobs naturally compensate displaced workers.  
**Research suggests:** Autor's new-work evidence plus note that new work may go to different workers/regions.  
**Impact:** honesty.

---

## Observation 17 — chapter can use one small number set without becoming statistical

Suggested maximum:
- one task-productivity result;
- one current labour-market result;
- one Australian adoption statistic;
- one historical transition-cost result.

This is enough to ground the chapter while preserving beginner readability.

---

## Observation 18 — diagrams may actually add value

The plan says none required. Research suggests one compact vertical diagram could clarify:

**Task capability → adoption → workflow change → labour demand outcome**

This would prevent several recurring misconceptions and can fit a book page better than a wide flowchart.
