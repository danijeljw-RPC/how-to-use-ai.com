# Important note about the plan's research constraints

The Chapter 10 plan states that "no new external research [is] required" because the content is conceptual, and that any historical precedent would be "illustrative rather than requiring statistical citation."

**This research task deliberately supersedes that note.**

The plan's scope, structure, learning outcomes, and tone remain authoritative. But the plan's own risks section warns against two failures, false reassurance and false alarm, and both are easier to avoid with good evidence than without it. A chapter about the most commercially and emotionally important question in the book should be able to show the reader what the evidence actually says, what it does not yet say, and how to tell the difference.

The plan also warns against **overclaiming certainty about which specific jobs or industries will be affected**, and against naming specific "doomed" or "safe" professions. Respect that. Research should help the writer stay structural (tasks, exposure, augmentation, replacement, adaptability) while grounding those structures in real evidence, rather than producing a list of professions ranked by risk.

# Central chapter framing

Research the chapter's two central claims rather than simply accepting them.

## Claim 1: "People using AI will often outperform people refusing to use it."

Investigate:

- what empirical evidence exists that AI users outperform non-users, and on what kinds of tasks;
- whether gains are larger for less-experienced or lower-performing workers, larger for experts, or mixed;
- evidence that AI use can **reduce** performance on tasks outside what AI does well (the "jagged frontier" idea);
- evidence that experienced people sometimes believe AI is speeding them up when measurement shows otherwise;
- whether individual productivity gains translate into better pay, more job security, or different outcomes for the worker;
- whether "outperform" is about productivity, quality, speed, career outcomes, or employability;
- whether there are contexts where refusing or limiting AI use is a sound professional choice (for example, regulated work, confidential work, skill-building stages, or tasks where AI performs poorly);
- and whether the claim needs qualification to remain honest.

## Claim 2: "Some jobs change, some disappear, many evolve, new ones emerge."

Investigate:

- whether this four-part structure is well supported by labour-economics research;
- evidence for each of the four categories;
- how much evidence exists for **disappearance** so far, as opposed to predicted disappearance;
- how much evidence exists for genuinely **new** AI-related jobs so far, how many there are, and whether they are accessible to ordinary workers;
- whether the categories overlap or can be distinguished cleanly;
- and whether better language or additional categories (for example, "jobs that get harder to enter," "jobs that pay less," "jobs where expectations rise") would be more accurate.

## The effort/judgement framework

The chapter uses the book's effort-heavy versus judgement-heavy framework as its self-assessment tool.

Investigate:

- how this framework relates to established task-based frameworks in labour economics (for example, routine versus non-routine tasks, cognitive versus manual tasks, and exposure indices);
- whether generative AI breaks earlier assumptions (earlier automation mostly affected routine tasks; generative AI appears to affect some non-routine cognitive tasks);
- whether "judgement-heavy" tasks are genuinely safe, or whether AI is also affecting some judgement tasks;
- whether effort and judgement can be separated cleanly in real jobs;
- whether some effort-heavy tasks are how people **build** judgement (the apprenticeship concern);
- and whether the framework applies to physical, care, and in-person work.

Do not abandon the approved chapter framing without strong reason, but identify weaknesses or necessary nuance so the eventual writer can avoid overstating it.

# Tasks versus jobs

Research the distinction between tasks and jobs thoroughly, because it is the conceptual core of the chapter.

Investigate:

- how economists define and measure tasks within occupations;
- occupational task databases (for example, O*NET in the United States and Australian equivalents such as the Australian Skills Classification and ANZSCO occupation classifications);
- how "AI exposure" indices are constructed;
- the main published exposure measures, what they measure, and how they differ;
- the difference between **exposure** (AI could plausibly perform or assist with a task) and **impact** (AI actually changes employment, wages, or work);
- why high exposure does not automatically mean job loss;
- the concept of **complementarity** (AI makes a worker more valuable) versus **substitution** (AI replaces a worker's contribution);
- the concept of bundled tasks and why jobs that bundle many different tasks tend to be more resilient;
- the concept of task reallocation within jobs;
- and how employers, not only technology, determine whether exposure leads to replacement.

Find beginner-appropriate explanations and examples that make the distinction concrete.

# Augmentation versus replacement

The plan requires a clear explanation of augmentation versus replacement.

Research:

- how economists and researchers define augmentation and automation;
- data on how people actually use AI at work, including any published analyses of real usage that classify interactions as augmentative or automative, and the limitations of those analyses (for example, company data from a single provider);
- evidence about whether augmentation is currently more common than replacement;
- evidence about whether augmentation tends to become replacement over time (for example, when one augmented worker can do the work of several);
- the economic argument that productivity gains can reduce headcount even without full task automation;
- the counter-argument that productivity gains can increase demand and create more work (Jevons-type effects and historical examples);
- the debate about whether AI developers and companies are choosing to build automation rather than augmentation, and arguments that the direction of technology is a choice;
- and how the distinction applies differently for employees, freelancers, and business owners.

Actively research the draft's claim that "a large share of current AI use, across most professional roles, looks more like augmentation." Determine what evidence supports it, what evidence complicates it, and whether it needs qualification.

# Historical technology shifts

The plan requires honest treatment of historical precedent, including real transition costs.

Research several historical episodes in depth, choosing those most useful to a beginner. Candidates include:

- mechanisation of agriculture;
- the Industrial Revolution and the Luddites (including what the Luddites actually objected to, which is frequently misrepresented);
- the long delay before wages rose during early industrialisation (sometimes called "Engels' pause");
- electrification;
- telephone switchboard operators;
- typists and typing pools;
- ATMs and bank tellers (research carefully: this example is widely used but its details are often oversimplified, and later developments changed the picture);
- spreadsheets and bookkeepers or accounting clerks;
- computerisation of offices;
- manufacturing automation and industrial robots;
- the decline of manufacturing employment in specific regions;
- translation software and professional translators;
- e-commerce and retail employment;
- and the internet's creation of occupations that did not previously exist.

For each episode, research:

- what the technology was;
- what jobs and tasks changed;
- what happened to employment in the affected occupations;
- what happened to wages;
- how long the transition took;
- who bore the costs (specific workers, regions, age groups, genders);
- what new work emerged, for whom, and where;
- whether displaced workers themselves moved into the new work, or whether new work went to different people;
- what policies helped or failed;
- and what the episode does and does not tell us about AI.

Research the evidence on **new work**: how much of today's employment is in job types that did not exist decades ago.

Research the evidence on the long-term costs to displaced workers, such as earnings losses after mass layoffs, regional decline, and health effects, so the chapter's Watch Out callout on real transition costs is grounded in evidence rather than general statements.

Actively research whether AI differs from past technology shifts in ways that weaken historical comparisons, for example:

- speed of adoption;
- breadth across occupations;
- effects on cognitive and professional work rather than mainly manual or routine work;
- the possibility that AI affects the process of learning and entry into professions;
- whether AI is a "general-purpose technology";
- and arguments that "this time is different" and arguments that "people always say this time is different."

Research **past predictions** about automation and jobs and how accurate they turned out to be, including widely cited estimates of the share of jobs at risk of automation, and later critiques of their methodology.

Do not allow history to become either a reassurance ("it always works out") or a warning ("this time is catastrophic"). Record what it actually shows.

# What the current evidence shows

Actively search for the most recent and credible empirical evidence on generative AI and employment.

Research:

- controlled experiments measuring productivity and quality effects of AI in specific jobs or tasks (for example, customer support, professional writing, consulting, software development, legal work);
- field studies in real workplaces;
- large-sample studies using administrative or payroll data;
- studies of freelance marketplaces (for example, changes in demand for writing, translation, and graphic design work after generative AI became available);
- studies of entry-level and early-career employment in AI-exposed occupations;
- national labour-market data and whether any AI effect is visible yet in aggregate employment or unemployment;
- surveys of employers about hiring intentions;
- surveys of workers about AI use, fear, and experience;
- company announcements of AI-related job cuts, and evidence about whether those cuts were actually caused by AI or attributed to AI for other reasons;
- company reversals, where firms that cut staff for AI later rehired people;
- and evidence on wages and wage inequality.

For each important study, record:

- research question;
- authors;
- publication or institution;
- publication date;
- whether peer-reviewed or a working paper;
- sample size and population;
- country;
- occupation or task studied;
- AI tool and version where relevant;
- method (experiment, field study, administrative data, survey, modelling);
- major findings;
- effect sizes or useful numerical results;
- limitations;
- what the study genuinely supports;
- and what it does **not** support.

Actively research whether different high-quality studies **conflict**, and why. For example, some studies have found large productivity gains in specific tasks while other studies using broad national data have found little measurable effect on earnings or hours so far. Record how both findings can be true at once (different time frames, different measures, different populations, adoption lags, task-level versus economy-level effects).

Do not generalise a short laboratory task into sweeping conclusions about whole occupations or long-term careers.

# Contrasting viewpoints

Chapter 10 concerns a question where serious economists, technologists, unions, employers, and governments disagree.

Do not manufacture artificial "both sides" symmetry.

Instead:

- identify the main positions;
- explain the reasoning behind each;
- identify what evidence supports each;
- identify where evidence is weak;
- distinguish economic predictions from observed effects;
- distinguish task-level experiments from economy-wide data;
- distinguish company announcements from independent evidence;
- distinguish worker testimony from representative labour-market evidence;
- distinguish arguments about what **will** happen from arguments about what **should** happen (for example, policy choices about augmentation versus automation);
- identify where two apparently conflicting claims can both be true.

Examples:

> AI substantially improves productivity on specific tasks in controlled studies.

and:

> National data so far shows little measurable effect of AI on overall employment or earnings.

These are not necessarily contradictory.

Likewise:

> AI helps new workers perform closer to experienced workers.

and:

> AI may reduce the number of entry-level positions available.

may both be true at the same time.

Likewise:

> Past technology shifts eventually created more jobs overall.

and:

> Past technology shifts caused lasting harm to specific workers and regions.

are both well documented.

Capture this nuance.

Research perspectives from:

- labour economists;
- economic historians;
- AI researchers;
- AI-company leaders (noting commercial interest);
- unions and worker organisations;
- employer groups;
- governments;
- international organisations;
- workers in affected occupations;
- and sceptics of both the "mass unemployment" and "nothing will change" narratives.

- forecasts rather than evidence.

### Employer survey
Useful for:
- stated hiring intentions.

Possible limitation:
- intentions often differ from actual behaviour;
- respondent selection.

### Union or worker-advocacy source
Useful for:
- worker experience;
- policy arguments.

Possible limitation:
- advocacy framing;
- selective examples.

### Academic study
Useful for:
- controlled evidence;
- methodology.

Possible limitation:
- artificial task;
- small sample;
- specific AI version;
- short-term outcome;
- working paper not yet peer-reviewed.

### Government source
Useful for:
- official data;
- policy.

Possible limitation:
- data lags;
- proposals may not become policy.

Do not discard a source because it has an interested party.

Instead, describe what it can and cannot establish.

# Quotations

Use direct quotations sparingly.

Prefer summarising a source's substantive finding.

Where a quotation is unusually useful:

- preserve it exactly;
- keep it short;
- record the source;
- record the page/section where possible;

Do not collect quotations simply because they sound dramatic. Treat dramatic predictions by AI-company leaders as data about what leaders are saying, not as evidence about what will happen.

# Depth

The research can be extensive.

Do not artificially restrict it to the expected length of the eventual Chapter 10.

It is preferable for the chapter-writing AI to receive substantially more high-quality research than it ultimately uses.

Do not pad the package with repetitive information.

Prioritise:

1. accuracy;
2. primary evidence;
3. recent evidence;
4. practical usefulness to worried readers;
5. strong real-world and historical examples;
6. Australian relevance;
7. clear separation of observed effects from forecasts;
8. meaningful counterarguments;
9. perspectives from workers as well as economists and companies;
10. clear distinction between evidence and opinion;
11. material that gives the eventual writer genuine editorial choices.

# Research philosophy

Do not approach this task trying to prove that:

- AI will cause mass unemployment;
- AI will create more jobs than it destroys;
- workers should not worry;
- workers should be very afraid;
- history proves everything will be fine;
- this time is completely different;
- adaptability solves everything;
- individuals are powerless.

Approach these as questions to investigate.

The research package should contain enough evidence for the later writer to say:

> Here is what we actually know so far.

> Here is what history does and does not tell us.

> Here is why the forecasts disagree.

> Here is what remains genuinely uncertain.

> Here is how to think about your own work, task by task.

> Here is what you can actually do now.

That is more valuable than producing a predetermined narrative.
