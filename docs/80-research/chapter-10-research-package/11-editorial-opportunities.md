# Editorial Opportunities

These are editorial possibilities, not instructions to rewrite the approved structure.

## 1. Table — Four words headlines confuse

| Term | Plain English | Does it mean job loss? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exposure | AI overlaps with tasks | No |
| Augmentation | AI helps a person do work | Usually not directly |
| Automation | AI performs a task | Not necessarily |
| Displacement | Demand for human labour falls | Yes, by definition |

Why it works:
Readers can decode labour-market claims immediately.

---

## 2. Vertical diagram

**Could AI perform part of the task?**  
↓  
**Is it adopted in the workplace?**  
↓  
**Does it complement or substitute for the worker?**  
↓  
**Does demand expand, stay flat or contract?**  
↓  
**What happens to hiring, hours, wages and job design?**

Why it works:
Shows why capability does not equal unemployment.

Where it breaks:
Real workplaces have feedback loops and multiple simultaneous effects.

---

## 3. "Same job, different tasks" example

Use aged care, electrician or retail manager.

A two-column box:
- tasks AI may assist;
- tasks requiring context/physical presence/accountability.

Avoid labelling the occupation safe.

---

## 4. "Two studies, both true"

Side-by-side:

**Customer support:** +14% average productivity.  
**Denmark national/admin evidence:** no large average earnings/hours effect in first two years.

Question:
How can both be true?

Answer:
one is task/workplace productivity; the other is labour-market outcomes across workers and firms.

This would teach evidence literacy exceptionally well.

---

## 5. Career-ladder box

Title idea:
**What happens if AI automates the work beginners used to learn from?**

Present both:
- AI can help novices perform better;
- firms may require fewer juniors.

No prediction required.

## 6. Historical timeline

Potential stops:
- mechanised textiles;
- industrialisation / Engels' pause;
- office computerisation;
- industrial robots;
- generative AI.

Keep it as "mechanisms changed" rather than a march of inevitable progress.

## 7. Website interactive — task sorter

Reader enters 5–10 tasks and rates:
- repeatability;
- digital/physical;
- stakes;
- verification;
- relationship;
- accountability;
- learning value.

Output should **not** produce an automation-risk percentage.

Better outputs:
- try AI assistance;
- high verification burden;
- strong human-context component;
- apprenticeship task;
- physical/embodied task.

---

## 8. Website interactive — headline decoder

Paste:
"60% of jobs exposed to AI"

The tool asks:
- exposure or loss?
- tasks or jobs?
- forecast or observed?
- country?
- time horizon?
- source?

This bridges naturally into Chapter 11.

## 9. Possible analogy — renovation, not demolition

A job is like a house made of rooms. AI may renovate some rooms, remove one, add another, or alter how the rooms connect.

Strength:
communicates bundled tasks.

Weakness:
can make change sound painless and planned.

Use only if paired with transition-cost evidence.

---

## 10. Better analogy — menu of tasks

A job title is the restaurant name; tasks are the menu. Technology can change the menu without closing the restaurant.

Strength:
simple.

Weakness:
does not capture worker displacement well.

---

## 11. Evidence box — Australia

Potential contents:
- 12% of Australian businesses reported AI use in 2024–25;
- JSA says augmentation more likely than replacement overall;
- Fair Work consultation may apply to major technological change;
- adoption is uneven by industry.

---

## 12. Compact reader challenge

Ask readers to choose one task they already do and test:
- without AI;
- with AI;
- compare total time including checking and correction.

This directly addresses the METR lesson that perceived speed is not always real speed.

Safety:
use only a low-risk, non-confidential task.
