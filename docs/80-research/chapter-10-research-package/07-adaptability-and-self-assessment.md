# Adaptability and a Research-Backed Self-Assessment

## Adaptability — keep the idea, qualify the burden

The approved chapter says adaptability matters more than any specific skill.

This is useful if adaptability means:
- noticing change;
- testing tools;
- learning;
- changing workflow;
- retaining verification ability;
- developing domain knowledge;
- moving toward tasks where human contribution remains valuable.

It becomes misleading if it implies:
- workers control whether their employer eliminates a position;
- retraining guarantees equivalent work;
- displaced workers can relocate easily;
- age, caring responsibilities, disability, money and geography do not matter;
- every labour-market shock is an individual learning problem.

Historical displaced-worker evidence shows that even capable, experienced workers can suffer persistent earnings losses after structural change.

---

## What readers can realistically do now

These actions fit Chapter 10 without stealing Chapter 12:

1. **Inventory tasks rather than job titles.**
2. **Observe actual change** in the reader's workplace and industry.
3. **Test AI on low-risk tasks** rather than assuming it helps.
4. **Measure the result** — time, error rate, revision effort, quality.
5. **Learn employer policy** and confidentiality rules.
6. **Preserve verification skills.**
7. **Notice apprenticeship tasks** that build future judgement.
8. **Build contextual/domain knowledge** that makes AI output easier to evaluate.
9. **Maintain human relationships** important to trust, coordination and reputation.
10. **Watch hiring signals**, especially entry-level patterns, not only layoffs.

# Improving the self-assessment

The current draft asks readers to sort tasks into:
- effort-heavy;
- judgement-heavy.

Keep this because it is simple. Add dimensions rather than replacing it.

## Suggested research-backed questions

For each task ask:

### A. Repeatability
Is it performed in roughly the same way each time?

High repeatability often increases automation potential.

### B. Digital input/output
Can the task be completed using information already available digitally?

A task requiring physical manipulation is less directly exposed to current generative AI.

### C. Verification
If AI produced an answer, how easy would it be for you to tell whether it was correct?

This is critical. A task can be easy to generate and hard to verify.

### D. Stakes
What happens if the output is wrong?

High-stakes tasks may remain human-supervised even if AI is technically capable.

### E. Context
Does success depend on information not written down anywhere?

Tacit knowledge can reduce immediate automation potential.

### F. Relationship and trust
Does the task depend on a particular person trusting *you*?

Relationships can be part of the economic value, not merely decoration around the task.

### G. Accountability
Who is responsible when the decision causes harm?

Formal accountability often changes how automation is adopted.

### H. Physical presence
Does the task require being in a particular place or manipulating the physical world?

This separates GenAI exposure from robotics/automation.

### I. Learning value
Is doing this task how a junior person learns the profession?

If yes, automating it may create a training-pipeline problem even if automation is efficient.

### J. Current AI usefulness
Can AI already reduce effort here **without creating more checking work than it saves**?

This makes the exercise practical instead of predictive only.

## Better output than "safe / unsafe"

The exercise should yield four possible labels:

- **Good candidate for assistance now**
- **Possible automation candidate**
- **Human-heavy because of context/stakes/physical presence**
- **Important learning task — use AI carefully**

This avoids pretending the reader can calculate a personal automation probability.

## Example: aged-care worker

Tasks might include:
- writing shift notes;
- rostering;
- summarising records;
- communicating with families;
- noticing changes in a resident's behaviour;
- physically assisting a resident;
- deciding when something is concerning;
- building trust.

The exercise would show mixed exposure inside one job:
- documentation may be AI-assisted;
- physical care remains physical;
- judgement may be supported but remains high-stakes;
- relationship value remains central.

This is much better for the book's broad audience than using only office jobs.

---

## Example: electrician

Possible AI-affected tasks:
- drafting quotes;
- summarising standards provided by the user;
- client communication;
- job scheduling;
- inventory planning.

Less directly affected by a language model:
- installing;
- fault finding in a physical environment;
- working safely around live systems;
- taking responsibility for compliant work.

Again: not "safe profession," but mixed task exposure.

---

## Example: junior lawyer / accountant / developer

The career-ladder question is important:

If AI performs:
- first drafts;
- routine research;
- simple reconciliations;
- basic coding;
- standard document review;

then firms may gain productivity but reduce the volume of junior tasks.

However, the customer-support study suggests AI can also accelerate novice learning.

These two mechanisms can coexist:
- AI makes each novice more capable;
- employers need fewer novices.

The chapter should present this as an unresolved structural tension, not a solved prediction.

---

## Adaptability evidence and caution

The most honest formulation:

> Adaptability is one factor a worker can control. It improves the ability to respond, but it does not determine the labour market by itself.

That keeps agency without blaming workers for structural outcomes.
