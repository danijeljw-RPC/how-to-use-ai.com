# Historical Technology Shifts — What History Does and Does Not Tell Us

## Principle

History is useful for mechanisms, not prophecy.

It demonstrates that:
- technologies frequently automate tasks without eliminating occupations;
- new work appears;
- productivity can expand demand;
- transitions can take decades;
- gains and losses are uneven;
- displaced workers do not automatically become the workers who capture new opportunities.

It cannot prove that AI will follow the same path.

---

## 1. British Industrial Revolution and "Engels' pause"

Robert C. Allen documents a period in the first half of the nineteenth century when British output per worker rose while real wages stagnated. Real wages later began rising more closely with productivity.

Why it matters:
- productivity growth can precede broad worker gains;
- aggregate growth does not tell the reader who benefits or when.

Source:
Robert C. Allen, 2009, *Engels' Pause: Technical Change, Capital Accumulation, and Inequality in the British Industrial Revolution*.  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eeh.2009.04.004

Caveat:
Economic historians debate measurement and interpretation of living standards during industrialisation. Use as a documented distribution/timing example, not a simplistic morality tale.

---

## 2. Luddites

Avoid the cliché that Luddites were merely irrational opponents of technology.

Historical scholarship places the disturbances in a setting of changing textile production, regulation, skill, wages and bargaining power. Machinery threatened customary work practices and workers' livelihoods.

Useful lesson:
people can object rationally to **how** a technology is deployed and how gains are distributed without opposing technology in principle.

Source:
Adrian Randall, *Before the Luddites: Machinery, Regulation, and the State*, Oxford Academic.  
https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199259908.003.0000

Editorial caution:
Do not overcompress a complex political and economic movement into a one-paragraph "workers feared machines" analogy.

---

## 3. Industrial robots

Acemoglu and Restrepo estimate negative local labour-market effects from robot adoption in the United States between 1990 and 2007. One additional robot per thousand workers was associated with a reduction in employment-to-population ratio and wages in exposed commuting zones.

Source:
Daron Acemoglu & Pascual Restrepo, *Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets*, Journal of Political Economy 2020 / NBER WP 23285.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285

Why it matters:
- technology can produce real local displacement even without national mass unemployment;
- location matters;
- the people losing can be different from the people gaining elsewhere.

---

## 4. Firm gain versus industry loss

Research on French manufacturing provides a useful counterintuitive example. Robot-adopting firms increased productivity and expanded their own employment, yet industry employment fell because adopters gained market share at competitors' expense.

Source:
Acemoglu, LeLarge & Restrepo, *Competing with Robots: Firm-Level Evidence from France*, NBER WP 26738.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w26738

Why it matters:
"the company that adopts AI hires more" does not prove the technology raises employment across the industry.

---

## 5. Displaced workers can suffer long-term losses

Jacobson, LaLonde and Sullivan used administrative earnings records and found long-term earnings losses averaging about 25% per year for high-tenure workers displaced from distressed firms in their studied population.

Source:
*Earnings Losses of Displaced Workers*, American Economic Review 83(4), 1993.  
https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v83y1993i4p685-709.html

Why it matters:
This is one of the best empirical foundations for the chapter's planned warning that "eventual net benefit" can coexist with severe personal cost.

Important limitation:
The episode is not AI-specific and concerns workers displaced in the 1980s. It establishes a general fact about displacement costs, not an AI forecast.

## 6. New work over decades

Autor and colleagues construct a database of new U.S. job titles from 1940–2018 and find that the majority of current employment is in job specialties introduced after 1940.

Source:
Autor, Chin, Salomons & Seegmiller, *New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940–2018*.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389

Key nuance:
The locus of new work shifted:
- 1940–1980: more middle-paid production and clerical new work;
- after 1980: more high-paid professional and lower-paid service new work.

This complicates "new jobs emerge" in a useful way. New work can increase while the occupational ladder becomes less favourable for some groups.

---

## 7. The ATM/bank-teller story — use cautiously

The popular story says ATMs increased teller employment because cheaper branches caused banks to open more branches.

This example is frequently repeated as proof that automation creates jobs. It should not be used as a universal rule.

If included:
- frame it as one historical mechanism in which productivity and business expansion offset task automation;
- avoid implying that later banking digitisation followed the same relationship indefinitely;
- recheck teller employment series and time period before publication.

Recommendation:
Do not make this the chapter's central historical example. The robot/local-labour-market and displaced-worker evidence is stronger for the book's balanced purpose.

## 8. Computerisation and office work

The most useful general lesson from office computerisation is task reallocation:
- calculation;
- filing;
- document preparation;
- communication;
- scheduling.

Whole occupations rarely changed because exactly one device "replaced the job." Instead, job bundles and required skills shifted.

Editorial use:
This is a familiar bridge from typewriters/spreadsheets to generative AI, but statistical claims should be sourced if numbers are used.

---

## What history does not prove

History does **not** establish that:
- AI will create more jobs than it destroys;
- displaced workers will move into new AI jobs;
- transition will be quick;
- wages will automatically follow productivity;
- the pace and breadth of AI adoption matches earlier technologies;
- cognitive automation behaves like agricultural or factory mechanisation.

## Ways AI may differ

Potential differences worth explaining without sensationalism:

- **Breadth:** language models can touch tasks in many industries through the same general interface.
- **Cognitive scope:** some professional, creative and technical tasks are exposed.
- **Adoption speed:** cloud software can diffuse faster than physical capital, although enterprise integration can still be slow.
- **Low marginal cost:** once deployed, software can scale cheaply.
- **Learning pipeline:** automation of junior tasks may change how workers acquire expertise.
- **Agentic use:** systems increasingly execute sequences of tasks, not only answer prompts.

These are reasons to avoid assuming historical repetition, not proof that "this time is different" in any particular direction.
