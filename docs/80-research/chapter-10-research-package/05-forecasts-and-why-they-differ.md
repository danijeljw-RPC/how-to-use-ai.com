# Forecasts, Exposure Estimates, and Why They Differ

## First editorial rule

Never put all percentages about "AI and jobs" in one table without identifying what they measure.

They may measure:
- task exposure;
- occupation exposure;
- automation probability;
- employer intentions;
- jobs created/displaced under a scenario;
- productivity;
- GDP;
- observed employment.

These are not interchangeable.

---

## IMF — global exposure

IMF staff analysis estimated almost 40% of global employment as exposed to AI, with about 60% in advanced economies.

Source:
IMF, *Gen-AI: Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work*, 2024.  
https://www.imf.org/en/-/media/files/publications/sdn/2024/english/sdnea2024001.pdf

What it measures:
**exposure**, with an additional attempt to distinguish complementarity.

What it does not mean:
40% of jobs will disappear.

---

## ILO — refined GenAI exposure

2025:
- one in four workers globally in occupations with some GenAI exposure;
- 3.3% in highest exposure category;
- higher exposure in high-income economies;
- gender differences due partly to occupational composition.

Source:
https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure

The ILO explicitly treats transformation as more likely than redundancy for most occupations.

---

## World Economic Forum — employer expectations

*Future of Jobs Report 2025* projects by 2030:
- 170 million roles created;
- 92 million displaced;
- net +78 million.

Source:
https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/2-jobs-outlook/

Critical classification:
This is based substantially on **employer expectations plus modelling around broad macrotrends**, not observed AI job creation/destruction. The figures include technological change, demographics, green transition, macroeconomic and geoeconomic forces.

Do not describe this as:
"AI will create 170 million jobs."

---

## Acemoglu — deliberately modest macro estimate

Daron Acemoglu estimates AI-related total factor productivity gains of no more than roughly 0.66% over ten years under his task-based assumptions, and argues early task experiments may overrepresent relatively easy-to-learn tasks.

Source:
*The Simple Macroeconomics of AI*, NBER WP 32487, 2024 / Economic Policy 2025.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487

Why it matters:
It provides a serious counterweight to very large macroeconomic projections without asserting that AI has no effect.

## Frey & Osborne — historical lesson in forecast interpretation

2013:
about 47% of U.S. employment estimated "at risk" of computerisation.

Source:
https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/the-future-of-employment

2016 task-based OECD re-estimate:
about 9% of jobs automatable on average across 21 OECD countries.

Source:
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-risk-of-automation-for-jobs-in-oecd-countries_5jlz9h56dvq7-en.html

The lesson is not that one paper was "wrong" and the other "right." Their units and assumptions differed substantially.

This pair is ideal for teaching readers why a headline number should trigger:
> "What exactly did they count?"

## Why forecasts disagree

### 1. Different unit of analysis
occupation vs job vs task.

### 2. Different capability assumptions
current model, hypothetical future system, AI + software, agents, robotics.

### 3. Different adoption assumptions
technical possibility is not universal deployment.

### 4. Different economics
cost of integration, wages, regulation, customer preference, complementary capital.

### 5. Different demand response
higher productivity can shrink labour needed per unit while expanding total demand.

### 6. Different time horizons
two years, five years, ten years, "eventually."

### 7. Different outcome
exposure, productivity, jobs, wages, GDP.

### 8. Different data source
expert scoring, LLM scoring, employer surveys, administrative records, platform logs.

### 9. Different incentives
AI companies, consultancies, unions, governments and academics can all have different institutional incentives. This does not invalidate their work; it changes how it should be read.

## Beginner-friendly reading rule

When a headline says:

> "X% of jobs will be affected by AI"

ask:

1. Does **affected** mean exposed, automated, displaced or merely changed?
2. Is X based on observed data or a scenario?
3. Does it measure tasks or whole jobs?
4. What year and country?
5. What assumptions about future AI capability?
6. Who produced the estimate?

That rule could be reused on the companion website as an interactive "decode the AI jobs headline" tool.
