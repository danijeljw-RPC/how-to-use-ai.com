# Meal Planning and Budgeting

These sections share a strong pattern: AI can organise inputs and generate options, but the user gets better results by supplying constraints and still needs to verify consequential details.

# Meal planning and food

## Useful household applications

- meals from ingredients already available;
- leftover use;
- recipe adaptation;
- substitutions;
- quantity changes;
- weekly plans;
- combined shopping lists;
- technique explanations;
- comparing meals against budget/time/equipment.

The main value is often **removing the blank-page problem**, not replacing culinary expertise.

## Context that materially improves output

- number of people;
- ingredients available;
- ingredients that need using soon;
- foods to avoid;
- preferences;
- budget;
- time;
- equipment;
- skill level;
- whether leftovers are desirable;
- cleanup tolerance.

## Vague → better

### Weak
> Give me dinner ideas.

Likely weaknesses:
- ingredients invented;
- no serving count;
- no time/budget constraints;
- generic ideas.

### Stronger
> I need dinner for two adults. I have chicken thighs, carrots, potatoes, spinach and rice. I have about 40 minutes, don't want anything spicy, and would prefer to use what I already have. Give me three options and list any extra ingredient each would require.

Why it improves:
- provides resources, audience, time, exclusions, objective and output format.

### What still needs checking
- food safety;
- allergies;
- medical dietary restrictions;
- safe cooking temperatures/times.

## Food safety boundary

Food Standards Australia New Zealand advises consumers to follow storage/cooking instructions, check use-by dates and allergen declarations, and recognises food allergies can be life-threatening.

Chapter-ready boundary:
> AI can help plan the meal. Packaging, official food-safety guidance and medical advice remain the authority where safety or allergy risk matters.

## Shopping-list workflow

1. List what is already available.
2. Mark ingredients that should be used soon.
3. Ask for meals that overlap ingredients.
4. Ask for one combined list containing missing items only.
5. Manually check quantities and household staples.

This demonstrates how context can reduce duplicate buying and waste.

# Budgeting and understanding money

## Useful consumer tasks

Moneysmart explicitly recognises roles for general-purpose AI in:
- explaining complex information;
- answering simple financial questions;
- supporting further research.

Chapter-relevant uses:
- categorise spending supplied by the user;
- model hypothetical changes;
- explain bill terminology;
- compare recurring expenses;
- prepare questions for a financial adviser.

## Important distinction

### Understanding/organisation
- “Explain compound interest.”
- “Group these expenses into categories.”
- “Model what happens if I reduce takeaway spending by $80 per month.”

### Decision-making
- “Which shares should I buy?”
- “Which super fund is right for me?”
- “Should I refinance?”

Moneysmart warns against treating general-purpose AI as a substitute for licensed personal financial advice.

## Vague → better budget request

### Weak
> Help me save money.

### Stronger
> Here is my average monthly spending by category. I want to reduce spending by about $250 a month, but I want to keep one restaurant meal each week and my gym membership. Identify three realistic areas I could review first. Do not recommend financial products.

What improved:
- real data;
- target;
- protected priorities;
- scope.

Still verify:
- exact bills;
- contract terms;
- tax;
- debt;
- product-specific financial advice.

## Sensitive information

Moneysmart warns against unnecessarily sharing personal/financial information. OAIC guidance similarly highlights privacy risks in public generative AI tools.

A practical consumer rule:
> AI usually does not need account numbers, identity documents or full personal records to help organise a budget.
