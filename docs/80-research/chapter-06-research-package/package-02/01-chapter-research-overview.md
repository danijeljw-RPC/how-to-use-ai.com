# Chapter Research Overview

## Research questions

The expanded brief is organised around three practical questions:

1. Where can AI genuinely help in ordinary home and personal life?
2. What context should an ordinary person provide to get a useful result instead of a generic answer?
3. How can people gain convenience or capability without gradually handing over too much investigation, learning or judgement?

## AI at home is already ordinary

Current consumer products provide AI capabilities that do not require technical expertise or even an obvious “chatbot” interaction. Official documentation currently describes:

- natural-language photo search;
- photo object removal;
- visual identification and questions about images/screens;
- translation and transcription;
- drafting, rewriting and summarisation;
- conversational search;
- accessibility features that describe images and documents.

This gives the chapter a stronger opening than “here are things to ask a chatbot”. A reader may already be using AI without thinking of the activity as AI.

## A useful consumer taxonomy

The practical uses in this research cluster into five groups:

### Generate
Produce a starting point:
- meal ideas;
- itinerary skeleton;
- packing list;
- practice questions;
- draft message.

### Explain
Re-express difficult information:
- bill terminology;
- compound interest;
- a school concept;
- formal correspondence;
- a hobby technique.

### Organise
Turn unstructured information into structure:
- categorise expenses;
- group errands;
- convert a brain-dump into tasks;
- summarise decisions.

### Transform
Change form while preserving the user's purpose:
- shorten;
- change tone;
- translate;
- simplify;
- reformat;
- edit a photograph.

### Inspect
Examine supplied material:
- a photograph;
- a product;
- a letter;
- a bill;
- a screen;
- a form.

The taxonomy avoids product marketing and stays useful as brands change.

## Better input: what repeatedly matters

Across the planned chapter sections, useful context typically includes:

- **objective** — what the person is trying to achieve;
- **situation** — relevant facts;
- **constraints** — money, time, equipment, dates, mobility, allergies;
- **preferences** — what matters to the person;
- **exclusions** — what should not be suggested;
- **audience** — child, landlord, school, beginner, traveller;
- **format** — checklist, three options, day-by-day outline;
- **freshness requirement** — what needs current data rather than a generic answer.

A strong recurring chapter pattern is:

> vague request → contextual request → what improved → what still needs checking

That final step prevents the chapter from implying that better prompting turns an AI model into an authority.

## Strong Australian evidence for the chapter's boundary

### Travel

Smartraveller's July 2026 consumer guidance explicitly treats AI as a useful starting point for destinations, activities, budgets and itineraries, while warning that visa rules, schedules, prices, safety information, local laws and availability can be outdated or wrong.

This directly supports:

> AI can help construct the plan; it is not necessarily the authoritative source for every live fact inside it.

### Money

Moneysmart's August 2026 guidance similarly says general-purpose AI can explain complex financial material and support research, but should not be treated as a personal financial adviser or final decision-maker.

This supports:

> use AI to understand and organise; retain ownership of consequential decisions.

## Convenience versus capability

The expanded brief introduces a useful distinction.

### Convenience
AI makes the current task easier:
- packing list;
- routine message;
- photo cleanup;
- summary;
- shopping list.

### Capability
AI leaves the person better equipped:
- explains interest;
- quizzes vocabulary;
- teaches a technique;
- critiques reasoning;
- improves communication skills;
- helps formulate questions for an expert.

They can overlap. The question “what did I become able to do?” is a more productive over-reliance test than “did AI help me?”

## Cognitive offloading is not automatically a problem

Risko and Gilbert's established work describes cognitive offloading as using external action/resources to reduce internal cognitive demand. Humans already use notebooks, reminders, calculators, calendars, maps and search engines this way.

A 2026 meta-analysis by Burnett and Richmond found that offloading could improve performance on memory-based tasks and reduce interindividual variability.

Therefore Chapter 6 should avoid treating “less mental work” as automatically harmful.

A better distinction is:

- **productive offloading:** removes unnecessary burden while preserving understanding, checking or ownership;
- **substitutive offloading:** replaces a cognitive activity the person still needs to practise or own.

## Critical-thinking evidence is mixed and contextual

A CHI 2025 Microsoft Research study surveyed 319 knowledge workers reporting 936 examples of GenAI use. Greater confidence in AI was associated with lower reported critical-thinking effort, while greater confidence in one's own ability was associated with more. The study also found critical thinking shifted toward verification, integration and task stewardship.

This supports a narrow claim:
> trusting AI readily can reduce independent evaluation in some contexts.

It does not support:
> AI causes long-term cognitive decline.

The study is self-reported, observational and about knowledge work.

## AI can support learning

Evidence is not one-directional. A 2025 Scientific Reports randomised trial found a carefully designed AI tutor outperformed the comparison active-learning condition in a specific university physics setting. Two 2026 NBER school studies found more modest or conditional gains, highlighting that design and engagement matter.

The chapter-ready distinction is:
> asking AI to coach, quiz, explain or give a hint is not the same as asking it to complete the learning task.

## Answer-first search changes information-seeking behaviour

Pew Research Center analysed browsing by 900 U.S. adults in March 2025. In the observed Google visits:

- users clicked a traditional result in 8% of visits where an AI summary appeared;
- they clicked a result in 15% of visits without an AI summary;
- links cited inside an AI summary were clicked in 1% of visits with a summary;
- sessions were more likely to end after a page containing an AI summary.

This supports a behavioural observation, not a claim that users became worse informed.

Google reports a different aggregate picture: it says total organic click volume remained relatively stable year-over-year and “quality clicks” increased slightly. These measures are not directly comparable to Pew's next-click panel data.

The chapter should describe the shift rather than claim “AI killed research”.

## Education choice as a consequential decision

Australian evidence does not support a simple claim that free/subsidised study makes people less committed.

- Free TAFE has produced very large enrolment and completion volumes.
- Cumulative program completions cannot be divided by cumulative enrolments and treated as a mature cohort completion rate.
- NCVER's qualification completion data show large variation by learner, provider, qualification, funding and circumstances.
- Both VET and higher education show meaningful employment outcomes.

A better education example is:
> use AI to map pathways and questions; verify accreditation, content, provider, cost, time and outcomes; keep the final decision with the user.
