# Myth vs Reality and Claims We Should NOT Make Without Qualification

# Candidate Myth vs Reality items

## Myth: AI at home mostly means novelty chatting

### Reality
Current consumer AI is embedded in:
- photo search/editing;
- translation;
- transcription;
- visual assistance;
- writing tools;
- search;
- mainstream devices.

## Myth: The longer or more complicated the prompt, the better the answer

### Reality
Useful context matters more than ceremonial complexity.

A strong everyday request normally supplies:
- goal;
- relevant facts;
- constraints;
- preferences;
- desired output.

There is no need to teach consumers elaborate “prompt engineering” for these tasks.

## Myth: If AI provides sources, the answer is checked

### Reality
Sources make checking possible; their presence does not guarantee correctness. Emerging research indicates citations themselves can increase trust even when incorrect.

## Myth: Using AI for learning is the same as cheating

### Reality
Use matters. AI can:
- provide a finished answer;
- give a hint;
- quiz;
- explain;
- coach;
- provide feedback.

Designed AI tutoring has shown positive learning outcomes in some studies; effects vary by implementation and engagement.

## Myth: AI always saves time

### Reality
Verification and correction can consume time. In one 2026 school experiment, structured AI support improved aspects of correctness but students spent longer on supported questions and progressed more slowly.

If the objective is learning, slower can still be beneficial.

## Myth: AI should replace the apps I already use

### Reality
For deterministic tasks, calendars, alarms, spreadsheets, calculators and checklists may be simpler and more predictable.

AI is especially useful for unstructured language, explanation, synthesis, adaptation and multimodal interpretation.

# Claims we should NOT make without qualification

## “AI is making people stupid.”

**Status:** Unsupported broad claim.

**Narrower evidence-supported version:** Some studies suggest GenAI can reduce reported cognitive effort or encourage offloading in particular tasks, while other research shows AI can improve learning/performance when deliberately structured.

## “AI destroys critical thinking.”

**Status:** Unsupported broad claim.

**Narrower version:** In one survey of knowledge workers, higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less self-reported critical-thinking effort. The same research also found critical thinking shifted toward verification and integration.

## “People no longer research things themselves.”

**Status:** Unsupported.

**Narrower version:** Pew observed lower external click-through from Google pages containing AI summaries than pages without them in a U.S. browsing sample.

## “Google AI Overviews stop people visiting sources.”

**Status:** Too absolute.

**Narrower version:** Pew observed lower click-through in its panel. Google reports relatively stable total organic click volume and slightly higher “quality clicks” on its aggregate metrics. They measure different things.

## “Free education makes students less committed.”

**Status:** Not supported by evidence gathered.

**Narrower version:** Completion rates vary by program and learner characteristics. Early/cumulative Free TAFE program counts are not mature cohort completion rates.

## “Paid education produces better students.”

**Status:** Unsupported and misleading.

Funding status is confounded with qualification, learner group, provider, duration and other factors. NCVER aggregate data do not show a simple pay-more/complete-more relationship.

## “University qualifications are always better than vocational qualifications.”

**Status:** Unsupported.

Both sectors show meaningful employment outcomes that vary by field and circumstance.

## “AI recommendations are reducing university enrolment.”

**Status:** No strong supporting evidence found in this research.

Do not include without separate evidence.

## “Using AI makes people dependent.”

**Status:** Too broad.

**Narrower version:** Automation bias and over-reliance are documented phenomena, and GenAI interaction can reduce critical engagement under some conditions. Effects depend on task, user, interaction style and whether skills remain practised.

## “AI is better than search.”

**Status:** Undefined without a task.

Conversational AI can synthesise/contextualise rapidly; traditional search exposes sources differently. Search itself increasingly contains generative AI, so the categories are converging.

## “AI saves time.”

**Status:** Sometimes, not universally.

AI can reduce effort for drafting, organising and exploration, while correction and verification can add work.

## “Cognitive offloading is bad.”

**Status:** Contradicted by broader cognitive literature as a universal statement.

Offloading can improve task performance. The more useful question is which cognitive process is being offloaded and whether the person still needs to practise or own it.
