# Real-World Examples and Chapter-5-Style Demonstrations

The later chapter writer should independently write final prose rather than copy these research notes verbatim.

# 1. Smartraveller: AI as travel starting point

**Source:** Australian Government Smartraveller, July 2026.

**Scenario:** Australians increasingly use AI for destination planning, visa information, safety and customs.

**AI contribution:** brainstorm and organise.

**Human still does:** verifies current entry rules, safety, schedules, prices, availability and laws.

**Lesson:** planning versus live facts.

**Placement:** Travel; Over-Reliance.

**Recreatable:** Yes.

# 2. Moneysmart: learn, don't delegate the decision

**Source:** Australian Government Moneysmart, August 2026.

**Scenario:** general-purpose AI can explain complex financial ideas and support research.

**Human still does:** validates facts and retains consequential decision ownership.

**Lesson:** understanding is different from personalised advice.

**Placement:** Budgeting; Over-Reliance.

# 3. Natural-language photo search

**Sources:** Apple / Google official documentation.

**Scenario:** search a photo library using conversational descriptions.

**Lesson:** AI at home can be embedded in an ordinary app.

**Placement:** Introduction.

# 4. Remove an object from a photo

**Source:** Apple Clean Up documentation.

**Scenario:** user circles/brushes a distracting person/object for removal.

**Lesson:** AI does not have to be professional, futuristic or chat-based.

**Placement:** Introduction; Myth vs Reality.

# 5. Seeing AI

**Source:** Microsoft Accessibility.

**Scenario:** blind/low-vision users read documents, identify products, describe photos and ask questions about scanned material.

**Lesson:** convenience for one user can be independence for another.

**Placement:** Accessibility.

# 6. Be My Eyes visual assistance

**Source:** OpenAI/Be My Eyes case study.

**Scenario:** visual AI helps blind and low-vision users interpret visual information.

**Caveat:** company case study; do not reuse promotional performance claims without independent evidence.

# 7. AI summaries and click behaviour

**Source:** Pew Research Center, 2025.

**Scenario:** observed Google users clicked external result links less frequently when AI summaries appeared.

**Lesson:** answer-first interfaces can shorten investigation.

**Placement:** Over-Reliance.

**Caveat:** U.S.; observational; interface changes over time.

# 8. Citations can inflate trust

**Source:** Li & Aral, 2025 preprint.

**Scenario:** citations/reference links increased trust even when incorrect in the experiment.

**Lesson:** ask for sources **and check them**.

**Placement:** Over-Reliance / source test.

# 9. Extend reasoning rather than merely recommend

**Source:** Microsoft Research, CHI 2025.

**Scenario:** users interacted with recommendation-centric versus reasoning-support AI.

**Lesson:** interface/request style can change how much human reasoning remains in the loop.

# 10. AI tutoring can teach when designed to teach

**Source:** Kestin et al., Scientific Reports, 2025.

**Scenario:** pedagogically designed AI tutor showed strong outcomes in a specific university physics setting.

**Lesson:** “AI for learning” does not equal “AI does homework”.

# 11. Khanmigo large-scale trial

**Source:** NBER 2026.

**Scenario:** modest math gains, limited tutor engagement.

**Lesson:** access to an AI tutor does not guarantee transformative use or outcomes.

# 12. Free TAFE arithmetic trap

**Sources:** DEWR, NCVER, Parliament of Australia.

**Scenario:** More than 850,000 enrolments and 275,000 completions were reported cumulatively by mid-2026. A user (or AI) could divide the figures and call the result a “completion rate”.

**Problem:** mathematically possible, conceptually wrong:
- starts occurred at different times;
- many students remain enrolled;
- course durations differ;
- cohort completion methodology observes students over time.

**Lesson:** AI can calculate a number correctly while answering the wrong statistical question.

**Placement:** education decision example; companion site.

This is a particularly strong real-world demonstration of why judgement remains necessary.

# Chapter-5 request building blocks

## Meal planning
Weak: “Give me dinner ideas.”

Useful context:
- people;
- ingredients;
- time;
- budget;
- exclusions;
- equipment;
- output format.

Verify:
food safety/allergens.

## Budgeting
Weak: “Help me save money.”

Useful context:
- category totals;
- target;
- protected priorities;
- timeframe;
- no product recommendation.

Verify:
contracts, exact figures, product advice.

## Travel
Weak: “Plan a trip to Japan.”

Useful context:
- dates;
- duration;
- budget;
- travellers;
- interests;
- mobility;
- pace;
- bookings.

Verify:
entry, travel advice, schedules, prices, availability.

## Email
Weak: “Write a complaint.”

Useful context:
- recipient;
- facts;
- chronology;
- outcome;
- tone;
- length.

Verify:
facts and consequential claims.

## Parenting explanation
Weak: “Explain gravity to my child.”

Useful context:
- age;
- current understanding;
- desired length;
- analogy preference;
- follow-up question.

## Hobby learning
Weak: “Teach me guitar.”

Useful context:
- current level;
- available time;
- instrument;
- goal;
- music preference.

Capability-building format:
> Give me a 15-minute practice session and quiz me at the end.

## Organising
Weak: “Organise my tasks.”

Useful context:
- task list;
- deadlines;
- fixed appointments;
- dependencies;
- locations;
- protected free time.

## Accessibility
Weak: “Explain this letter.”

Useful context:
- plain language;
- action requested;
- source wording;
- uncertainty.

Verify:
original document.
