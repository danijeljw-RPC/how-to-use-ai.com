# Practical Ways to Preserve Judgement

These are **candidate reader behaviours supported by the research**, not scientifically validated “laws”.

# Define the objective before asking AI

Why:
- prevents the answer from silently defining the user's goal;
- makes assumptions easier to see.

Pattern:
> Before you compare anything, here is what I am actually trying to achieve.

Especially useful for study, career, expensive purchases, travel and money.

# Ask for options rather than “the answer”

A research-informed Microsoft guide recommends using AI to surface alternatives.

Example:
> Give me four approaches and tell me what each optimises for.

Better than:
> What's the best approach?

# Ask what assumptions were made

Useful follow-up:
> What did you have to assume because I did not give you enough information?

This reinforces Chapter 5 directly.

# Ask what could change the answer

> What information, if different, would make you change this recommendation?

This turns a verdict into a conditional analysis.

# Ask for counterarguments

Useful:
- “Make the strongest case against this.”
- “What would someone who disagrees say?”
- “What have I probably not considered?”

# Use AI to ask questions

A strong inversion:

Instead of:
> Tell me what to do.

Use:
> Ask me five questions I should answer before deciding.

This retains ownership.

# Write your view first for consequential decisions

Consistent with the ExtendAI research approach:

> Here is my current view and why. Don't tell me whether I am right yet. Identify weak assumptions, missing evidence and plausible alternatives.

In the CHI 2025 study, AI that extended participants' own reasoning integrated better into their decision process than a recommendation-centric design. Do not universalise the result from one study.

# Separate facts from recommendations

> Put factual claims in one section and suggestions in another.

Benefit:
- easier checking;
- clearer distinction between evidence and synthesis.

# Ask for sources, then open important ones

This is stronger than simply “ask for sources”.

Why:
- citations can increase trust even when wrong;
- answer-first search can reduce click-through.

Reader principle:
> Sources are useful because they let you check—not because their presence makes the AI correct.

# Scale checking with consequence

### Low consequence / reversible
Examples:
- dinner;
- birthday theme;
- packing;
- photo edit.

Scrutiny:
- quick common-sense check.

### Medium
Examples:
- itinerary;
- expensive purchase;
- important complaint.

Scrutiny:
- check current/key facts.

### High
Examples:
- money;
- legal rights;
- visa/entry;
- health;
- education/career pathway.

Scrutiny:
- authoritative sources and/or qualified human advice.

This is a practical synthesis, not a formally validated scale.

# Scale checking with reversibility

An inexpensive restaurant choice is easy to undo. A multi-year qualification, major investment or binding agreement is not.

Practical heuristic:
> the harder a decision is to undo, the less appropriate it is to accept an unverified AI recommendation.

# Use AI to teach when learning matters

Instead of:
> Do this.

Try:
> Teach me how, then give me a similar problem to try myself.

Or:
> Give me one hint at a time.

This aligns with tutoring and retrieval-practice evidence.

# Dependency reflection

A useful non-diagnostic question:
> If I face a similar situation next month, will today's AI use leave me more capable, equally capable or less capable?

# Candidate callout

## Use AI without handing over the wheel

Before accepting an important answer:
- What am I actually trying to achieve?
- What did the AI assume?
- What alternatives exist?
- Which facts need a real source?
- What would change the recommendation?
- Do I understand the answer?
- Is this still my decision?
