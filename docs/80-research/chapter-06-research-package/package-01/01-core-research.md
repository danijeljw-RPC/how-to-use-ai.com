# Chapter 6 Core Research — AI at Home

## 1. Introduction — where AI actually fits at home

### What the research supports

The most defensible framing is not “AI can run your life.” It is that generative AI is particularly useful where a person has a messy starting point and wants a usable first draft: a plan, list, explanation, message, set of options or summary.

A 2025 Pew Research Center survey of U.S. adults found that 73% would be willing to let AI assist them **at least a little** with day-to-day activities, while only 13% would let it assist “a lot.” This is useful framing because it shows public openness to limited assistance without implying broad trust or enthusiasm. The same Pew work found strong desire for more control over AI use in daily life.

**Source:** Pew Research Center, “AI in Americans’ lives: Awareness, experiences and attitudes,” 17 September 2025.  
https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2025/09/17/ai-in-americans-lives-awareness-experiences-and-attitudes/

### Useful explanation for a non-technical reader

Many ordinary home tasks have one of these shapes:

- “I know what I need, but I do not know how to start.”
- “I have too much information and want it organised.”
- “I know the facts but cannot find the right wording.”
- “I want options that fit several constraints.”
- “I need an explanation in a form that makes sense to me.”

Those are natural language tasks, and modern generative AI is strong at turning context and constraints into a draft response. The chapter should make clear that a polished draft and a verified answer are not the same thing.

### A useful three-part decision test

The existing template’s “effort versus judgement” distinction is good but too binary on its own. Research supports strengthening it with two more dimensions:

1. **Judgement:** Is the human decision itself valuable or consequential?
2. **Stakes:** What happens if the answer is wrong?
3. **Verifiability:** Can the output be easily checked against a trusted source, calculator, map, booking system, professional or personal knowledge?

Examples:

- A packing-list draft: low stakes, easy to check — good AI use.
- A travel itinerary: useful AI starting point, but opening hours and transport need live verification.
- A household budget: AI can help interpret categories and suggest possibilities; exact calculations are better handled by a spreadsheet/calculator and financial decisions need human judgement.
- A medical diet for a health condition: high stakes and difficult for a layperson to verify — not an appropriate place to rely on a general-purpose chatbot alone.

## 2. Meal planning

### Genuine capability

Meal planning is a strong consumer example because it combines preferences and constraints that are tedious to reconcile manually: household size, budget, leftovers, preparation time, available ingredients, dislikes, dietary pattern and cleanup tolerance.

For **ordinary meal inspiration**, the value is primarily organisational and generative. A well-formed request can turn preferences into a draft menu plus shopping list. This directly demonstrates the Chapter 5 context-and-specificity pattern.

### Important limitation: “meal planning” is not one risk level

Research consistently shows a distinction between general meal inspiration and nutritionally or clinically reliable diet design.

A 2024 evaluation of 108 meal plans created by ChatGPT and Bard/Gemini found that plans often met many dietary reference intakes but had recurring shortcomings, including low energy/carbohydrates and deficiencies such as vitamin D and fluoride; vegan plans had additional B12 issues. The authors concluded that these systems may be useful for general dietary inspiration but should not be relied on to produce nutritionally adequate plans, especially for restrictive dietary needs.

**Source:** Podszun et al., “Can the AI tools ChatGPT and Bard generate energy, macro- and micro-nutrient sufficient meal plans for different dietary patterns?”, 2024.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39102765/

A 2026 study of three major LLMs similarly found that 35-day meal plans systematically undershot target energy and had macronutrient imbalances, reinforcing the point that an attractive menu is not equivalent to a nutritionally validated one.

**Source:** “An Analysis of the Nutritional Value of Diets Generated by Large Language Models Under Average User Conditions—3(LM)Diet Study,” 2026.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42514431/

Other studies find useful potential but still recommend professional oversight for clinical scenarios.

**Sources:**  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38359704/  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41054023/

### Editorial distinction worth teaching

**Good fit:**

- “Give me five weeknight dinner ideas using chicken, lentils, rice and vegetables, under 30 minutes, with leftovers for lunch.”
- “Turn this list of meals into a combined shopping list grouped by supermarket section.”
- “Suggest substitutions for an ingredient I dislike.”

**Needs another source or professional:**

- treatment diets;
- allergy safety where cross-contamination matters;
- infant/child nutrition decisions;
- medical conditions;
- exact calorie/micronutrient targets.

### Better worked-example structure

Instead of showing only one polished prompt, show the transformation:

**Vague:** “Make me a meal plan.”

**Better:** Include number of people, number of days, budget, dietary dislikes, preparation time, leftovers and desired output.

**Refinement:** “Replace Tuesday with something vegetarian and reuse Wednesday’s ingredients.”

**Verification:** Check allergies, medical constraints and any exact nutrition claims separately.

This sequence teaches a reusable skill rather than merely displaying a good prompt.

## 3. Budgeting help

### Strong Australian primary guidance

ASIC’s Moneysmart now has a dedicated 2026 page on “AI and money decisions.” Its framing is unusually well aligned with this chapter: AI can be useful as a **learning tool, not a decision-maker**. It lists useful tasks such as explaining financial concepts, summarising complex information and suggesting what to research next, while warning against relying on AI for investment choices or personal financial advice.

**Source:** Australian Securities and Investments Commission (Moneysmart), “AI and money decisions,” updated 17 August 2026.  
https://moneysmart.gov.au/online-safety/ai-and-money-decisions

Moneysmart also states that users should avoid sharing unnecessary personal details such as date of birth, address, email and financial information, and should verify claims before acting.

This is directly relevant to the draft chapter because its current budgeting example asks the reader to provide monthly spending data but does not discuss data minimisation.

### When a conventional tool is better

For arithmetic, persistent records and exact totals, a spreadsheet or dedicated budget tool is usually a better primary tool than a generative model.

Moneysmart provides a conventional budget planner designed to calculate where money is going and whether income covers expenses. It can also be downloaded as a spreadsheet.

**Source:** Moneysmart, “Budget planner,” updated 19 May 2026.  
https://moneysmart.gov.au/budgeting/budget-planner

This creates a useful “AI versus conventional tool” example:

- **Spreadsheet/calculator:** exact arithmetic, repeatability, stored categories, month-to-month tracking.
- **AI:** explain categories, suggest questions to ask, brainstorm cuts that honour stated preferences, reframe a confusing financial concept in plain language.

### Practical prompt design

A safer example would use rounded or anonymised categories rather than copying raw bank statements into a public chatbot:

> “My approximate monthly spending is: rent $X, groceries $Y, transport $Z, eating out $A, subscriptions $B. I want to save about $C a month but want to keep one dinner out each week. Suggest three categories to examine and explain the trade-offs. Do not recommend investments or financial products.”

The chapter does not need to become a privacy chapter, but one sentence about **not pasting account numbers, bank statements or identifying information unless the user has deliberately assessed the tool’s privacy settings** would materially improve it.

## 4. Travel planning

### What LLMs are good at

Travel planning is a strong AI use case because people naturally express **soft constraints** in language: “quiet rather than nightlife,” “walkable,” “good food,” “two children,” “moderate budget,” “do not change hotels every night.” LLMs can combine these preferences into a coherent first draft much faster than manually assembling options.

### Where general LLMs are weak

Google Research’s 2025 work on LLM-based trip planning provides a valuable primary-source admission from a company building such systems: LLMs are good at qualitative preferences but less reliable with quantitative and real-world constraints such as budgets, schedules, travel time and opening hours. Their own example notes that an LLM-generated plan may suggest visiting a museum that will be closed by the time the traveller arrives.

Google’s production approach therefore combines the LLM with grounding and optimisation using current opening hours and travel times.

**Source:** Google Research, “Optimizing LLM-based trip planning,” 6 June 2025.  
https://research.google/blog/optimizing-llm-based-trip-planning/

This is an excellent chapter-level concept: **the impressive part of an itinerary is not the same as the dependable part.**

### Academic evidence

A study comparing ChatGPT itineraries with expert-created itineraries found that AI plans were accessible and easy to understand but less accurate and less specific. The authors described AI as a useful starting point rather than an exclusive trip-planning tool.

**Source:** “ChatGPT as a Travel Itinerary Planner,” 2024.  
https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-58839-6_38

A 2026 Journal of Consumer Behaviour study experimentally examined hallucinations in AI travel itineraries. Hallucinations reduced perceived accuracy; accuracy influenced usefulness and trust, which in turn affected willingness to follow recommendations.

**Source:** Rejón-Guardia et al., “AI Hallucinations in Tourism: How Errors Impact Consumer Trust and Recommendation Acceptance,” 2026.  
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cb.70105

### Recommendation bias is another limitation

Research on destination recommendations shows that LLMs can favour well-known, heavily represented destinations and reproduce stereotypes or hidden selection criteria rather than neutrally searching all possibilities.

**Sources:**  
https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3387/16/6/252  
https://www.mdpi.com/2673-2688/6/9/236

### A practical rule for the chapter

Use AI to:

- brainstorm destinations;
- group activities by interests;
- draft a day-by-day shape;
- make packing/check lists;
- compare trade-offs;
- turn existing bookings into a readable plan.

Verify elsewhere:

- opening hours;
- ticket availability;
- visa/entry rules;
- transport timetables;
- weather alerts;
- prices;
- safety information;
- reservations.

This is a clearer and more durable distinction than naming a specific travel-AI product.

## 5. Writing emails, messages and forms

### Evidence that AI can reduce drafting friction

Noy and Zhang’s preregistered experiment with 453 college-educated professionals found that access to ChatGPT for mid-level professional writing tasks reduced average completion time by 40% and improved evaluator-rated quality by 18%. The tasks were professional rather than household emails, so the study should not be overstated, but it provides strong evidence that generative AI can reduce the blank-page and drafting burden for writing tasks.

**Source:** Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang, “Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence,” *Science*, 2023.  
https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586

A much larger field experiment across 66 firms and more than 7,000 workers later found that treated users who actually used the tool spent about two fewer hours per week on email in the latter half of the trial, though broader work patterns changed less dramatically.

**Source:** Dillon et al., NBER Working Paper 33795, revised 2025.  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795

### The draft’s “sound like you” claim needs softening

Prompting for tone helps, but research suggests AI-assisted writing can become stylistically homogenised. A CHI 2025 controlled study found that AI suggestions shifted Indian participants’ writing toward Western stylistic norms. Other 2025–2026 research finds reduced stylistic or creative diversity in LLM-assisted/generated writing.

**Sources:**  
https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713564  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2025.100207  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42637911/

The safer statement is:

> Providing tone, facts and examples of your normal wording can move the draft closer to what you want, but you should still edit it so the final message actually sounds like you.

### High-value household uses

- declining invitations;
- complaints and follow-ups;
- notes to schools, clubs or landlords;
- converting bullet points into a coherent message;
- rewriting something shorter, clearer or less emotional;
- simplifying a form question before answering it;
- turning a rough voice note into a draft.

### Important caveat for forms

AI can explain wording, but the official form/instructions remain the authority. A useful pattern is:

> “Explain what this question is asking in plain English. Do not answer it for me. Tell me which facts I need to decide my own answer.”

That is often safer than asking AI to fill in consequential forms from assumptions.

## 6. Parenting support

### Useful role: support the parent, not replace the parent

The current draft is directionally sound in saying AI can help explain topics, suggest activities or draft a note while leaving judgement with the parent.

UNICEF’s current parenting guidance recommends age-appropriate discussion of AI and suggests that parents can explore AI with children and critically examine its answers together. It also explicitly warns about privacy and habit formation, including children becoming accustomed to turning to AI for answers.

**Source:** UNICEF Parenting, “Parenting in the AI age.”  
https://www.unicef.org/parenting/digital-parenting/how-approach-ai-children

### Australian evidence materially strengthens this section

The Australian eSafety Commissioner’s 2026 research on children’s experiences with AI assistants and companions reports that:

- 20% of children who had used an AI assistant or companion had experienced potentially inappropriate or harmful interactions;
- 32% had shared personal or potentially sensitive information;
- even among “assistant” users, 50% reported using them for at least one personal or social reason.

**Source:** eSafety Commissioner, “Talking to machines: Children’s experiences with AI assistants and companions,” 2026.  
https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/talking-to-machines-childrens-experiences-with-ai-assistants-and-companions

The eSafety Commissioner also advises that many generative AI tools are not designed with child-specific safeguards and recommends adult supervision.

**Source:** eSafety Commissioner, parental controls / generative AI guidance.  
https://www.esafety.gov.au/parents/issues-and-advice/parental-controls

### Crucial distinction the chapter can make without becoming a child-safety chapter

There is a large difference between:

- **a parent using AI as a tool** to draft an explanation or activity; and
- **a child relying on an AI companion or chatbot** for emotional or personal support.

The chapter only needs a short boundary sentence, but omitting this distinction in 2026 would make the parenting section too casual.

### Better example

Instead of merely “Explain why the sky is blue to a seven-year-old,” a stronger demonstration is:

> “My seven-year-old asked why the sky is blue. Give me a scientifically accurate explanation in three short sentences that I can explain in my own words. Then give me one simple experiment or observation we can do together. Flag anything in the activity that needs adult supervision.”

This keeps the parent in the loop and makes the AI output a starting point for a human interaction rather than a substitute for one.

## 7. Learning hobbies

### Strong fit: personalised explanation and practice

Hobbies are a natural low-to-medium-stakes use case when AI is used to:

- explain unfamiliar terminology;
- break a skill into steps;
- suggest a short practice routine;
- create exercises or quizzes;
- diagnose a likely beginner mistake from a description;
- adapt an explanation after the learner says what they did not understand.

The strength is not that AI is an expert teacher. It is that conversation makes it easy to request another explanation immediately.

### Evidence from AI tutoring is mixed, which is useful

Khan Academy reported 2026 product experiments across millions of tutoring threads in which adding structured learning-history context improved “next-item correctness.” This supports the general idea that context improves tutoring usefulness, but it is a vendor’s own product research and should be labelled accordingly.

**Source:** Khan Academy, “How Khan Academy Is Building a Better AI Tutor: Our Most Recent Learnings,” May 2026.  
https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-khan-academy-is-building-a-better-ai-tutor-our-most-recent-learnings/

An independent 2025 study comparing Khanmigo, Google Search and paper-only learning for undergraduate physics found significant learning gains in all conditions but no statistically significant difference between groups. This is a useful counterpoint: conversational AI can be a pleasant or convenient learning interface without automatically producing better learning outcomes.

**Source:** Slijepcevic and Yaylali, “Leveraging ‘Khanmigo’ Generative AI-Powered Tool for Personalized Tutoring to Learn Scientific Concepts,” 2025.  
https://jtl.uwindsor.ca/index.php/jtl/article/view/10052

UNESCO guidance also favours a human-centred, age-appropriate approach and warns that educational value depends on how generative AI is integrated rather than on access alone.

**Source:** UNESCO, “Guidance for generative AI in education and research,” updated 2026.  
https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=387

### Important boundary for hobbies

Not all hobbies are low stakes. Advice about mains electricity, power tools, climbing, diving, firearms, food safety, chemicals or other hazardous activities should not be treated like advice about knitting or guitar chords. The chapter can state the principle without creating a long safety catalogue: **the more a hobby can physically injure someone, the less appropriate it is to rely on unverified chatbot instructions.**

## 8. Organising life

### Where AI adds something beyond a normal list app

A conventional list app stores tasks. Generative AI can help with the *transformation* step:

- turn a brain dump into categories;
- identify dependencies;
- group errands by location or type;
- create a packing list from trip context;
- summarise a long planning conversation;
- convert notes into a checklist;
- propose an order for tasks based on user-supplied priorities.

This is a good place to reinforce Chapter 5: the quality of the organisation depends on context such as dates, priorities, locations, who is involved and what “done” means.

### Product examples are changing rapidly

Google’s AI Mode Canvas, for example, can maintain a planning workspace across sessions and update it through follow-up requests. This is useful as a current screenshot/example for the companion website, but the chapter should describe the task rather than depend on the product.

**Source:** Google, “Use Canvas in AI Mode to get things done and bring your ideas to life,” March 2026.  
https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/ai-mode-canvas-writing-coding/

### Privacy issue the current draft misses

The draft suggests summarising a long group-chat thread. That can be useful, but pasting messages written by other people into a public AI service may disclose their personal information. The Australian Information Commissioner recommends, as a best practice for organisations, not entering personal and especially sensitive information into publicly available generative AI tools because of complex privacy risks. While that guidance is written for regulated organisations, the underlying consumer lesson is straightforward: **minimise what personal information you upload and understand the tool’s data settings before pasting private conversations or documents.**

**Source:** Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, “Guidance on privacy and the use of commercially available AI products,” updated 17 January 2025.  
https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products

## 9. Accessibility support

### The current chapter undersells this topic

Accessibility is one of the strongest areas for concrete, non-gimmicky home examples. The topic should go beyond “simplify a letter.” Current AI-enabled accessibility includes:

- image descriptions for blind/low-vision users;
- speech-to-text and voice control;
- transcription and captioning;
- rewriting/simplifying dense text;
- tone or communication support;
- turning visual or document information into another modality;
- question-answering about an image or scene.

### Strong real-world example: TalkBack image descriptions

Google’s TalkBack screen reader can use generative AI to provide more detailed image descriptions and, on supported devices/features, allow follow-up questions about images. Google explicitly notes that generative descriptions are experimental and may be inaccurate.

**Sources:**  
https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/accessibility/android-gemini-ai-gaad-2025/  
https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/15341968?hl=en

### Research with blind users

A 2024 ASSETS study interviewing 19 blind people who used mainstream generative AI tools such as ChatGPT and Be My AI found real everyday utility alongside problems with hallucinations, inaccuracy, accessibility barriers and users’ mental models of the systems.

**Source:** “I look at it as the king of knowledge: How Blind People Use and Understand Generative AI Tools,” ASSETS 2024.  
https://doi.org/10.1145/3663548.3675631

A 2025 study evaluated AI assistance for activities of daily living among people with vision impairment, making it a potentially useful evidence source for the final chapter writer.

**Source:** Seiple et al., “Performance on Activities of Daily Living and User Experience When Using Artificial Intelligence by Individuals With Vision Impairment,” 2025.  
https://doi.org/10.1167/tvst.14.1.3

### Important framing

AI can provide an accessibility layer, but it does not make an inaccessible service or document “fixed.” Nor should an AI description be treated as infallible where a mistake could create physical, financial or legal harm.

A strong chapter example would pair a capability with its limitation:

> A blind user can ask for a description of an unlabeled image, but because generated descriptions can be wrong, a high-stakes interpretation may still need another source or a human check.

This avoids both techno-optimism and fear-based framing.

## 10. Over-reliance / appropriate reliance

### Better terminology: appropriate reliance

Microsoft Research’s 2024 synthesis defines appropriate reliance as accepting correct AI outputs and rejecting incorrect ones. This is a more precise goal than merely “use AI less.” Both over-reliance and under-reliance can reduce the value of human-AI collaboration.

**Source:** Passi, Dhanorkar and Vorvoreanu, “Appropriate reliance on Generative AI: Research synthesis,” Microsoft Research, March 2024.  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/appropriate-reliance-on-generative-ai-research-synthesis/

### Experimental evidence of over-reliance

A 2024 behavioural experiment found that participants sometimes followed AI advice even when it conflicted with contextual information and their own assessment, creating worse outcomes. This supports the chapter’s instinct that judgement can be quietly ceded rather than deliberately delegated.

**Source:** Klingbeil, Grützner and Schreck, “Trust and reliance on AI — An experimental study on the extent and costs of overreliance on AI,” *Computers in Human Behavior*, 2024.  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108352

Microsoft Research’s 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers found that higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less reported critical-thinking effort, while higher self-confidence was associated with more. The study also found that critical thinking shifts toward verification, integration and stewardship when AI is used.

**Source:** Lee et al., “The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking,” CHI 2025.  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/

### Learning and “cognitive offloading” evidence requires careful handling

The widely discussed MIT Media Lab “Your Brain on ChatGPT” work found lower EEG connectivity and lower self-reported ownership/recall in an LLM-assisted essay-writing condition. However, it was a small preprint study focused on essay writing, not proof that everyday AI use broadly “damages the brain.” If used, it should be presented as emerging evidence and a reason for further study, not a settled conclusion.

**Source:** Kosmyna et al., “Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task,” 2025 preprint.  
https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/

A 2025 randomised controlled trial of 120 undergraduates reported lower 45-day knowledge retention for students who used ChatGPT as a study aid than for a traditional-study group. Again, this is about learning design, not all AI use.

**Source:** Barcaui, “ChatGPT as a cognitive crutch: Evidence from a randomized controlled trial on knowledge retention,” 2025.  
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125010186

### Stronger practical heuristic for the chapter

Keep the plan’s “effort versus judgement” test, but add this short check:

**Before delegating a home task to AI, ask:**

- Is the annoying part the effort, or is the thinking itself valuable?
- What happens if the answer is wrong?
- Can I check the result easily?
- Is this a skill I still want to be able to do without AI?

That produces a much more useful boundary than a general warning to “not rely on AI too much.”

## 11. Myth versus reality — research-backed options

### Myth: “AI at home is mostly a novelty chatbot.”

**Reality:** AI can be genuinely useful for drafting, explaining, summarising, planning and accessibility tasks. Public-opinion research shows substantial willingness to use AI for some day-to-day assistance, though not necessarily deep dependence.

### Myth: “If AI produces a detailed answer, it has checked the details.”

**Reality:** Travel-planning research and hallucination studies show that coherent itineraries can still contain impossible logistics, stale information or fabricated details. Detail is presentation, not verification.

### Myth: “AI is either safe to trust or useless.”

**Reality:** The better goal is calibrated or appropriate reliance: use AI where it adds value, verify where the model is weak, and retain human judgement where consequences matter.

### Myth: “A more personalised answer must be more accurate.”

**Reality:** Personalisation can make an output more relevant or persuasive without making its facts more correct. This distinction matters particularly for finance, travel and parenting.

### Myth: “Using AI always weakens your skills.”

**Reality:** Evidence is task-dependent. AI can improve performance and reduce drafting time, but unrestricted use in some learning settings may reduce retention or critical engagement. The effect depends on whether AI is replacing the thinking that creates the skill or supporting it.
