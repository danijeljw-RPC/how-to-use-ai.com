# Contrasting Viewpoints and Debates — Chapter 6

## 1. “AI saves time” versus “AI can remove useful effort”

### Evidence for time/productivity gains

Noy and Zhang found a 40% reduction in completion time and an 18% improvement in rated quality for professional writing tasks with ChatGPT. A later large field experiment found material reductions in email time for active users.

Sources:  
https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586  
https://www.nber.org/papers/w33795

### Evidence for possible learning/critical-thinking costs

Research on critical thinking, cognitive offloading and knowledge retention suggests that benefits depend on what kind of effort is removed. If AI removes clerical or formulation effort, the gain can be useful. If it removes the mental work that builds the underlying skill, the trade-off can be different.

Sources:  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/  
https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/  
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125010186

### Editorial conclusion

Do not write “AI saves time, therefore it is good” or “AI removes effort, therefore it makes people less capable.” The defensible conclusion is task-dependent: **removing unwanted friction is different from removing practice you still value.**

## 2. “AI can personalise advice” versus “personalised output can still be wrong”

Generative AI is unusually good at responding to preferences expressed in ordinary language. That makes outputs feel tailored and relevant. However, personalisation changes relevance, not truth status.

Travel research shows personalised itineraries can contain hallucinations, poor logistics or biased recommendations. Moneysmart warns that general-purpose AI does not know a user’s full financial situation merely because the user tells it a few facts.

Sources:  
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cb.70105  
https://research.google/blog/optimizing-llm-based-trip-planning/  
https://moneysmart.gov.au/online-safety/ai-and-money-decisions

### Editorial conclusion

A useful sentence for the eventual chapter: **“More personal” does not mean “more verified.”**

---

## 3. “AI can sound like you” versus “AI may homogenise writing”

Prompting for tone, audience and examples can improve fit. Yet controlled studies show AI suggestions can push writing toward model-dominant norms and reduce stylistic/cultural diversity.

Sources:  
https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713564  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2025.100207  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42637911/

### Editorial conclusion

AI is well framed as a drafting partner. The user should remain the final editor, especially for emotionally meaningful or identity-bearing messages.

---

## 4. “AI is a tutor” versus “AI is an on-demand explainer”

Vendor product research shows AI tutoring systems can improve immediate interaction metrics when supplied with strong context. Independent research does not consistently show that AI tutoring outperforms search or other learning methods.

Sources:  
https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-khan-academy-is-building-a-better-ai-tutor-our-most-recent-learnings/  
https://jtl.uwindsor.ca/index.php/jtl/article/view/10052  
https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=387

### Editorial conclusion

For a beginner book, “on-demand explainer and practice partner” is more defensible than “personal tutor” unless the specific product has tutoring design and evidence behind it.

---

## 5. “AI improves accessibility” versus “AI can add a new unreliable layer”

Generative image description, voice interaction, transcription and text simplification can materially improve access. Research with blind users also documents hallucination and accessibility problems.

Sources:  
https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/accessibility/android-gemini-ai-gaad-2025/  
https://doi.org/10.1145/3663548.3675631  
https://doi.org/10.1167/tvst.14.1.3

### Editorial conclusion

The chapter should neither reduce accessibility to a “nice convenience” nor imply that AI solves accessibility. A better frame is **new access channel, new verification problem**.

---

## 6. “AI meal planning works” versus “nutrition is not just a recipe list”

LLMs can generate practical meal ideas, combine constraints and make shopping lists. Nutritional studies repeatedly find shortcomings when those plans are evaluated against energy and nutrient targets, particularly for restrictive or clinical diets.

Sources:  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39102765/  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42514431/  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38359704/

### Editorial conclusion

Keep meal planning in the chapter, but distinguish **menu inspiration** from **dietetic advice**.

## 7. “AI can help with parenting” versus “a child’s relationship with an AI is a different issue”

AI can help a parent draft explanations or activities. UNICEF encourages co-use and discussion. Australian eSafety evidence shows children also use assistants for personal/social reasons and may share sensitive data or encounter harmful interactions.

Sources:  
https://www.unicef.org/parenting/digital-parenting/how-approach-ai-children  
https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/talking-to-machines-childrens-experiences-with-ai-assistants-and-companions

### Editorial conclusion

Separate two scenarios that the current template risks blending:

- parent uses AI as a tool;
- child interacts directly with AI as an adviser/companion.

The first belongs naturally in Chapter 6. The second needs only a concise caution and can be handled more deeply in later risk material.

---

## 8. “AI can plan travel” versus “grounded systems are not the same as a standalone chatbot”

Current travel products increasingly combine LLM conversation with maps, live search, booking data and deterministic optimisation. Google Research explicitly documents this hybrid approach because the language model alone is not dependable enough for hard constraints.

Source:  
https://research.google/blog/optimizing-llm-based-trip-planning/

### Editorial conclusion

Avoid writing about “AI” as if every tool has the same access to current information. A general chatbot answering from model knowledge and a travel system grounded in live maps/flights are different technical situations even if both have a chat box.

---

## 9. “Over-reliance means using AI too often” versus “reliance is about calibration”

The research literature treats the central problem as **inappropriate reliance**, not raw usage frequency. A person can use AI frequently while checking it well, or use it rarely and blindly trust one consequential answer.

Source:  
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/appropriate-reliance-on-generative-ai-research-synthesis/

### Editorial conclusion

This improves the plan’s existing non-fear framing. The chapter does not need to warn readers away from AI; it needs to teach them when confidence should and should not transfer from the system to the user.
