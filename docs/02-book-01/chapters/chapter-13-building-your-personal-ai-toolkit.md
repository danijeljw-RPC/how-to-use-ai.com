# Chapter 13 — Building Your Personal AI Toolkit

---

## Chapter Purpose

This chapter gets practical about the tools themselves: what categories of AI tools exist, how to evaluate one before you trust or pay for it, and how to experiment safely — without turning into a list of specific products that will be outdated before you finish reading it.

> **Key Idea:** AI literacy matters more than loyalty to any specific tool.

---

## Intended Reader Outcome

By the end of this chapter, the reader should understand that:

- AI tools fall into a handful of general categories, each suited to different kinds of tasks
- there's a practical, repeatable way to evaluate a new AI tool before relying on it or paying for it
- common scam and subscription-trap patterns exist and are worth recognising
- the goal is building transferable AI literacy — the same understanding this entire book has taught — not attachment to any one product

---

# Categories, Not a Catalogue

This chapter deliberately won't recommend specific AI products by name. Not because none are worth using — plenty are genuinely excellent — but because specific products change, rebrand, get acquired, or get replaced by something better on a timeline much shorter than this book's shelf life. A tool list written today would start going stale within months.

What doesn't go stale is understanding the *categories* of tools that exist, and having a reliable method for evaluating whatever specific option you're looking at, today or five years from now. That's what this chapter actually teaches.

---

# The Six Categories

**Chat AI.** General-purpose conversational tools — the kind most people picture first, covering the writing, summarising, brainstorming, and explaining tasks from Chapter 3.

**Image AI.** Tools that generate or edit images from text descriptions — covered in Chapter 8's creative discussion, also useful for practical mockups and visual concepts.

**Research AI.** Tools oriented around finding, synthesising, and citing information, often with more emphasis on sourcing and accuracy checks than general chat tools.

**Productivity AI.** AI features built into everyday work tools — document editors, spreadsheets, email, calendars — automating specific tasks within software you may already use, connecting to Chapter 7's workplace examples.

**Coding AI.** Tools aimed at writing, explaining, or debugging code, relevant to the coding-assistance example from Chapter 3.

**Voice AI.** Tools built around spoken interaction, from voice assistants (Chapter 1's example) to more advanced voice-based AI conversation.

Most people end up using a small handful of these — often just one or two — rather than all six. That's normal. The point of knowing the categories isn't to use every one; it's to recognise what kind of tool you actually need for a given task.

---

# How to Evaluate a New AI Tool

Before trying or paying for a new AI tool, a short practical checklist:

> **Try This:** Before committing to a new AI tool, ask: What specific task do I actually need this for? Is there a free or low-cost way to try it first? What happens to the information I put into it — where is it stored, and who can see it (relevant to Chapter 7's confidentiality point, even for personal use)? Is the pricing clear, or does it require digging to find? Would I still want this tool if the free trial ended today?

That last question is a genuinely useful filter — plenty of tools are appealing mainly because of an introductory offer or novelty, not because they solve a real ongoing need.

---

# Experimenting Safely

The safest way to try a new AI tool is the same approach Chapter 3 recommended for testing AI output generally: start with a low-stakes task you already understand well enough to judge the result.

Don't hand a brand-new, untested tool your most sensitive information or your most important task on the first try. Test it on something low-consequence first — a draft email you were going to write anyway, a question you already know the answer to — and see whether the result, the experience, and the way it handles your information actually meet the bar before trusting it with anything that matters more.

---

# Avoiding Scams and Subscription Traps

Chapter 9 covered AI-powered scams — AI being used *by* scammers. This is the other direction: scams and traps built *around* selling AI products themselves.

Common red flags worth knowing:

- **Urgency-driven upsells** — "limited time" pricing pressure designed to skip the evaluation step above entirely.
- **Thin wrappers** — apps that charge for access to a free or cheap underlying AI service, with little added beyond a different interface, often not disclosed clearly.
- **Unclear pricing** — a genuine product is usually straightforward about what it costs; if pricing requires digging or a sales call to find out, that's worth noticing.
- **Excessive permissions** — an AI app requesting access to contacts, files, or data with no clear connection to what it actually does.
- **Fake reviews or inflated claims** — capability claims that don't hold up against the evaluation method earlier in this chapter, or against Chapter 11's hype-evaluation approach.

None of this means most AI products are scams — most aren't. It means the same basic consumer caution you'd already apply to any other paid app or subscription service applies here too.

# Myth vs Reality

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| You need to use every category of AI tool to be AI-literate. | Most people genuinely need one or two categories relevant to their own tasks — knowing the categories exist is what matters, not using all of them. |
| The most important thing is picking the "best" AI tool. | The most important thing is understanding what you actually need and having a method to evaluate whatever option you're considering — tools and rankings change constantly. |
| Free trials and low prices mean a tool is automatically worth using. | Pricing and trial offers don't replace the evaluation checklist — a genuinely useful tool should hold up to "would I still want this once the introductory offer ends?" |
| AI subscription scams are rare or obvious. | Thin wrappers, unclear pricing, and urgency-driven upsells are common enough to be worth actively checking for, not assuming you'd automatically notice. |

# Core Takeaway

AI literacy matters more than loyalty to any specific tool.

Understanding the six general categories, having a practical evaluation method, experimenting safely with low-stakes tasks first, and recognising scam and subscription-trap patterns will serve you long after any specific product named in a book has been replaced by something newer.

This has actually been true of every chapter in this book: the goal was never to teach you a specific tool. It was to teach you to understand AI well enough to evaluate whatever tool you encounter, today or years from now.

---

> **Recap:** AI tools generally fall into six categories — chat, image, research, productivity, coding, and voice. Before trying or paying for one, use a simple evaluation checklist, start with low-stakes experiments, and watch for scam and subscription-trap red flags. The specific tools will keep changing. The literacy to evaluate them won't need to.
