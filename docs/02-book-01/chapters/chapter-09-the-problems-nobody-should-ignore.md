# Chapter 9 — The Problems Nobody Should Ignore

Parts 1 and 2 built a grounded, practical understanding of AI: what it is, what it's good for, and how to use it well. Everything so far has been fairly optimistic and practical — AI is useful, here's how to use it well, here's where it fits in your life. That's not the whole picture, and pretending otherwise wouldn't be honest. Part 3 turns to something this book hasn't set aside so much as saved for its own proper space: the genuine risks that come with AI's growing presence in daily life.

> **Key Idea:** AI has genuine risks that require informed users and responsible regulation.

This chapter takes that other half seriously. Not with alarmist framing — the goal isn't to make you afraid of AI — but with the same grounded, specific approach this book has used throughout. Real risks deserve real attention, described plainly, so you can actually do something useful with the information instead of just feeling generally unsettled.

## Misinformation and Deepfakes

### Misinformation

Generative AI, covered back in Chapter 2, can produce convincing false text at a scale and speed that used to require a team of people — a flood of fake reviews, fabricated news-style articles, or convincing-sounding social media posts, all generated in seconds. What used to be limited by how fast a person could type is now limited only by how fast someone chooses to click "generate."

### Deepfakes

Beyond text, AI can produce convincing false images, audio, and video — including fabricated quotes, doctored photos, and "deepfake" videos that show real people appearing to say or do things they never actually said or did. This matters because it lowers the cost of producing convincing fake content dramatically. It used to take real skill and time to fake a photo or video convincingly. Now it can take minutes.

> **Watch Out:** Treat unexpected, dramatic, or emotionally charged content — a video, an audio clip, a screenshot — with a little more scepticism than you might have a few years ago, especially if it's not from a source you trust directly. If something looks or sounds surprising, check whether it's been independently reported elsewhere before assuming it's real.

## Scams

Scammers use AI the same way legitimate users do: to write more convincing messages, generate fake profile photos, or even clone a familiar voice from a short audio sample. A phishing email that used to have obvious spelling mistakes and awkward phrasing can now read as smoothly as a genuine message from your bank. A scammer might use a short public audio clip of someone's voice — pulled from a social media video, for instance — to generate a fake urgent phone call that sounds like a family member in distress, asking for money to be sent quickly.

The core defence hasn't changed, even though the scams have gotten more convincing: be wary of urgency, verify through a separate channel by calling the person back on a known number rather than trusting the caller, and don't let a convincing voice or message override normal caution. The technology has improved. The rule for protecting yourself hasn't needed to.

## Bias

AI systems learn patterns from their training data, and if that data reflects existing unfair patterns — in hiring, lending, policing, or anything else — the AI can reproduce or even amplify those patterns, often without anyone intending it to. This connects directly back to Chapter 4: AI has no common sense or moral judgement of its own. It reflects the patterns in what it learned from, including patterns nobody would defend if asked directly.

> **Watch Out:** AI output can sound neutral and objective while still reflecting biased patterns underneath. This matters most for AI used in decisions that affect people's lives — hiring, lending, insurance, and similar — where an unfair pattern in the data can quietly become an unfair pattern in real outcomes.

## Surveillance and Privacy

### Surveillance

AI makes certain kinds of surveillance dramatically cheaper and easier: automatically identifying faces in footage, transcribing and searching recorded conversations at scale, or building detailed profiles from scattered pieces of personal data that would have taken a human analyst far longer to piece together. What used to require a dedicated team of investigators can now be done automatically, at a scale that changes what's practically possible, not just what's theoretically possible.

### Privacy

This raises real privacy questions independent of whether any particular use is well-intentioned. The same capability that helps a business understand its customers can also enable much more invasive tracking than most people are aware of or have consented to — often through terms and conditions nobody reads in full, which is part of why privacy concerns here feel different from a simple, single decision to opt in or out.

## Copyright Disputes

Chapter 8 already touched on this in the creative context: many AI systems were trained on large amounts of existing creative and written work, and whether that use requires permission or compensation is a genuinely unresolved legal question, actively being contested in courts as this book is written. This isn't limited to art and writing. It also affects code, journalism, and any other content that AI models may have been trained on without the original creators' explicit involvement.

## Environmental Costs

Training and running large AI systems requires substantial computing power, which requires substantial electricity and, often, water for cooling data centres. As AI use has grown, so has scrutiny of its environmental footprint — a genuine cost that's easy to overlook because it's invisible from the user's side of a chat window. This doesn't mean every AI use is environmentally catastrophic; a single request uses a relatively small amount of energy. But at the scale AI is now used globally, the cumulative cost is real and worth being aware of, not dismissed.

## Monopolisation by Large Technology Companies

Building and running the largest AI systems requires enormous amounts of money, specialised hardware, and technical expertise, resources concentrated in a small number of large technology companies. This raises a real concern: a handful of companies increasingly shape what AI tools are available, how they work, and what values or restrictions they build in, with relatively little competition or independent oversight. This isn't unique to AI — similar concerns have followed other major technology waves — but it's a genuine, ongoing dynamic worth being aware of rather than assuming AI development is naturally open, competitive, or accountable by default.

It's worth being precise about what this chapter is and isn't saying. AI risk isn't mostly overblown media hype — several of these risks, including scams, deepfakes, bias in high-stakes decisions, and privacy erosion, have concrete, documented real-world examples behind them. Taking risk seriously doesn't mean AI is fundamentally dangerous and should be avoided — finding AI genuinely useful, as Parts 1 and 2 of this book did, and taking its risks seriously aren't contradictory positions; this book holds both at once. These aren't all abstract, distant problems that only affect institutions or governments — scams, misinformation, and privacy concerns affect ordinary individual users directly. And it isn't true that nothing can be done about any of this: individual awareness, like verifying suspicious content and being cautious with personal data, and responsible regulation both play a real role, and neither replaces the other.

## Core Takeaway

AI has genuine risks that require informed users and responsible regulation. Misinformation, deepfakes, scams, bias, surveillance, copyright disputes, privacy concerns, environmental costs, and monopolisation are all real, specific, current issues, not distant hypotheticals. None of this cancels out what Parts 1 and 2 of this book established. AI can be genuinely useful and genuinely risky at the same time, and holding both of those honestly is what being informed actually looks like.

> **Recap:** This chapter opened Part 3 by covering nine genuine AI risk categories — misinformation, deepfakes, scams, bias, surveillance, copyright disputes, privacy, environmental cost, and monopolisation — each grounded in a concrete example rather than speculation. The goal wasn't to create fear, but to replace vague unease with specific, actionable awareness.
