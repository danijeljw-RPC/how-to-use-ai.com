# Chapter 5 — Talking to AI Properly

## Chapter Purpose

Part 1 covered what AI is, why it's suddenly everywhere, what it's good at, and where it falls short. Part 2 starts putting that understanding to work. This chapter covers the single most useful practical skill in the whole book: how to talk to AI in a way that gets you noticeably better results.

> **Key Idea:** Better questions produce better answers.

## Intended Reader Outcome

By the end of this chapter, the reader should understand that:

- vague requests produce vague answers, for a specific, explainable reason
- context, specificity, and examples all measurably improve AI output

---

# Good Questions, Better Answers

If you've tried an AI tool once, gotten a flat, generic answer, and quietly decided "it's not that good," this chapter is for you.

There's no secret trick here, no technical discipline to master, and nothing that requires understanding how the technology works under the hood. Getting good results from AI is much closer to a familiar, everyday skill: giving clear instructions to another person.

Think about the difference between asking a new colleague "can you help me with this?" and asking them "can you help me shorten this email to three sentences, keep it friendly, and make sure it still mentions the delivery delay?" The second version gets a far more useful result — not because the colleague is smarter, but because they know what you actually want.

AI works the same way.

---

# Why Vague In Means Vague Out

Chapter 3 explained that AI predicts the most likely, plausible-sounding response to whatever you give it. That explanation is the entire reason this chapter works.

If your request is vague, the "most likely response" to a vague request is, unsurprisingly, a vague answer — because a vague request could reasonably lead anywhere, so the AI has to guess broadly at what would satisfy it.

If your request is specific, detailed, and gives the AI real context to work with, the range of "most likely, plausible-sounding responses" narrows dramatically toward what you actually want.

This isn't a trick. It's a direct, logical consequence of how prediction works: better input narrows the range of good outputs.

---

# Context Matters

**Context** means the background information the AI needs to give a relevant answer — because, unlike a colleague who already knows your job, your project, and your situation, AI starts each conversation with none of that.

**Vague:** "Write a description for my product."

**Better:** "Write a two-sentence product description for a reusable water bottle aimed at hikers, emphasising that it keeps drinks cold for 24 hours."

The second version gives the AI something to actually work with: audience, format, length, and the one selling point that matters most. The first version leaves all of that to guesswork.

> **Plain English:** Context isn't extra politeness — it's the information AI needs to narrow down what "a good answer" would even look like. Without it, AI is filling in the gaps with its own best guess, which is rarely the guess you had in mind.

---

# Specificity Matters

Specificity means being precise about exactly what you want, rather than leaving the shape of the answer open-ended.

**Vague:** "Help me plan a trip."

**Better:** "I'm planning a 4-day trip to Lisbon in October, travelling with one other adult, moderate budget, more interested in food and walking neighbourhoods than nightlife. Suggest a rough day-by-day outline."

Notice this isn't longer for the sake of being longer — every added detail (dates, group size, budget, interests) directly shapes what a genuinely useful answer looks like.

---

# Examples Improve Output

When you want a particular style, tone, or format, showing AI an example is often faster and more reliable than describing it in the abstract.

**Vague:** "Write this in a more casual tone."

**Better:** "Write this in a more casual tone — something like: 'Hey, quick heads up — your order's running about two days behind, sorry about that! We'll get it out as soon as we can.'"

Describing "casual" in words is subjective. Showing a concrete example removes the guesswork entirely.

---

# Iteration Improves Results

Perhaps the most underused technique of all: treating the first response as a draft, not a final answer.

If the first response is close but not quite right, say so directly — "make this shorter," "that's too formal, try again warmer," "keep the structure but cut the second paragraph" — the same way you'd give a second round of feedback to a colleague's first draft.

Most people stop after one attempt and judge the whole tool by that single response. A second, more specific follow-up request often gets a meaningfully better result than trying to write the "perfect" first request from scratch.

---

> **Try This:** Think of a real request you'd normally type quickly into an AI tool — an email, a plan, an explanation. Before sending it, rewrite it using this chapter's pattern: add relevant context, be specific about format and length, and include an example if tone or style matters. Compare how the result feels compared to your first instinct.

# Before and After: Putting It Together

A few more side-by-side examples, across different everyday tasks:

**Learning something new**

- Vague: "Explain inflation."
- Better: "Explain inflation to me like I've never studied economics, using a grocery-shopping example, in under 150 words."

**Getting advice**

- Vague: "Give me feedback on this."
- Better: "Give me feedback on this cover letter for a marketing coordinator role — focus specifically on whether the opening paragraph grabs attention, and keep suggestions to bullet points."

In every case, the underlying request didn't change. What changed was how much the AI had to guess.

> **Watch Out:** More words is not the same as more useful context. Padding a request with irrelevant detail doesn't help the way relevant detail does — a request can be long and still vague, or short and still specific. Focus on what actually shapes the answer: audience, format, length, tone, and the one thing that matters most.

# Myth vs Reality

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| Getting good AI results requires learning secret technical phrasing. | Good results mostly come from giving clear, specific instructions — the same skill as briefing a new colleague. |
| If the first answer isn't great, the tool just isn't very good. | The first answer is often a rough draft; a specific follow-up request usually improves it significantly. |
| Longer requests are always better requests. | Relevant detail helps; irrelevant padding doesn't. Specificity matters more than length. |
| Examples are only useful for creative or artistic tasks. | Examples help with almost anything where tone, format, or style matters — emails, plans, explanations, and more. |

---

# Core Takeaway

Better questions produce better answers.

AI predicts the most plausible response to whatever it's given — so a vague request gets a vague answer, and a request with real context, specificity, and examples narrows that prediction toward what you actually want.

None of this requires prompt-engineering jargon or technical skill. It's the same conversational instinct you'd already use briefing a new colleague — now aimed at a tool that responds instantly and never gets tired of a second draft.

---

> **Recap:** Vague requests produce vague answers because AI is predicting the most plausible response to whatever it's given, and a vague request could reasonably lead anywhere. Adding context, specificity, and examples narrows that prediction toward what you actually want — and treating the first response as a draft, not a final answer, usually improves results further than trying to write the perfect request from scratch.
