# Chapter 8 — AI and Creativity

## Creativity Raises Different Questions

Picture three people opening the same AI app on the same Tuesday evening.

The first has never been able to draw. Her granddaughter has asked for a bedtime story about the family's old dog, Biscuit, who once chased a pelican along the beach and lost. Twenty minutes later there is a picture of Biscuit mid-leap, ears flying, the pelican looking unimpressed. It is not a masterpiece. It is also something she could not have made last year, and the story now has a face.

The second is a freelance illustrator. She has just read an email from a regular client: “We'll handle the concept boards with AI this time and come back to you for finals.” The concept boards were a third of her income from that client. Nobody has insulted her work. The work has simply become smaller.

The third is a songwriter with a chorus she has been humming for a month. She types in her chords and asks what might happen if the chorus opened up instead of repeating the verse. One suggestion is dull, one is clever but wrong for the song, and one makes her sit up. By midnight she has rewritten the chorus herself, and it is better than anything the AI proposed.

Same technology. Same evening. Three completely different experiences of what AI means for creative work. (All three are fictional teaching examples, not people I have interviewed, but none of them is far-fetched.)

That is why this chapter is different from the two before it. Ask whether AI can shorten an email or organise a shopping list and the questions are fairly contained: did it help, is it accurate, was the time saving worth it? Ask whether AI should help write a novel, illustrate a children's book, clone a singer's voice or generate a film performance and different questions arrive. Who is the author? Where did the training material come from? Was anyone asked? Is the result original? Who lost paid work? Does the audience deserve to know how it was made?

Those questions do not disappear because the output is useful. They also do not prove that the output is useless.

Part 2 has been building towards this. Chapter 5 showed how to communicate with AI: give it context, constraints and purpose, then inspect and refine. Chapter 6 applied that at home and introduced a distinction between removing effort you do not value and surrendering judgement you do. Chapter 7 raised the stakes at work, where confidentiality, policy and verification matter because other people rely on the result. Creative work uses all of that foundation, then adds craft, identity, authorship, consent, livelihood and cultural value.

You will often hear that AI is “just another tool”. That is true, and it is incomplete. A pencil is a tool. So is a camera, a synthesiser, Photoshop and a film-editing suite. The interesting question is what the tool does inside the process. Earlier creative tools mostly helped a person carry out a decision they had already made. Generative AI can supply expressive material of its own: the plot twist, the colour palette, the melody, the face of the person in the picture. Its suggestions can widen a creator's options. They can also quietly become the options from which the creator thinks they are choosing.

> **Key Idea:** AI changes creative workflows more than it replaces creativity itself, but it does so by changing where human effort goes, not by removing the human from the picture. It can generate and transform material remarkably quickly. People still make the consequential decisions about direction, selection, meaning, quality, context and rights, and about whether the result is worth publishing at all. How that balance falls differs by task, by medium and by how you choose to work.

That idea is more useful than either of the slogans you will meet online. AI does not press a button labelled *creativity*. Nor is it limited to fetching coffee for the real artist while the important work happens elsewhere. By 2026 it can produce polished-looking prose, images, songs, voices and video. So “you can always tell” is no longer a helpful test, and “it only makes rough drafts” is no longer true. The harder tests are control, distinctiveness, consistency, truthfulness, rights, fit to the brief, and whether the work gives anyone a reason to care.

This chapter does three things. First, it walks through writing, image-making, music, video and design with worked examples you can adapt. Second, it looks honestly at what “effort versus judgement” means when the craft itself is where much of the judgement lives. Third, it takes the three arguments you have almost certainly heard, *AI is stealing*, *AI is replacing artists* and *AI is killing creativity*, and unpacks what each one actually contains. The aim is not to hand you a verdict. It is to give you enough evidence and enough distinctions that your own verdict is worth something.

## AI Across Creative Domains

### One Label, Many Workflows

Before looking at specific crafts, it helps to notice that the label *AI-made* hides an enormous range of human involvement.

At one end, a writer asks an AI for questions that help them think, then writes every sentence. An editor extends two seconds of a shot so the voice-over can finish. A designer asks for colour variations of a layout they built. At the other end, a five-word instruction produces most of a song, image or article, and software publishes thousands of such outputs with nobody looking closely at any of them.

It is useful to think of this as a ladder rather than two buckets:

![Six ways AI can take part in creative work, from most human-led to most AI-led: blank-page breaker, variation engine, production assistant, co-generator, AI-led generation and automated content. Questions about authorship, rights, disclosure and lost paid work grow as you move down.](../diagrams/creative-ai-involvement-continuum.mmd){ width=42% }

As a **blank-page breaker**, AI offers starting points for a problem you have defined: ten possible titles, five ways into a scene, three moods for a poster. As a **variation engine**, it takes a direction or piece of work you supply and produces alternatives: this melody in a minor key, this layout with more white space. As a **production assistant**, it performs a bounded task inside your plan: removing background noise, extending a shot, resizing a design for six social platforms. As a **co-generator**, it supplies substantial expressive material that you select, rearrange and rework. In **AI-led generation**, you provide a short instruction and the model decides most of the detail. And in **automated content generation**, large numbers of outputs are produced with little case-by-case human attention at all.

These are not legal categories, and real projects move up and down the ladder. A novelist might use AI as a blank-page breaker on Monday and a proofreader on Friday. But the ladder explains something the rest of this chapter depends on: most of the hard questions change as you move down it. Who is the author? Is there copyright in the result? Should the audience be told? Is this assisting a creator or replacing one? A single word, *AI-assisted*, can conceal more than it explains. Keep the ladder in mind as we go through the five domains.

### Writing: Options Without Renting Out Your Voice

The weakest description of AI in writing is “it can draft”. A capable language model can brainstorm premises, propose scene directions, test structures, interrogate a character's motivation, suggest titles, compress a synopsis, spot repeated ideas, rewrite for a different audience and imitate the surface features of many genres. That is a lot of practical power. The question is how to use it without ending up as the proofreader of somebody else's story.

Imagine a writer with a premise. During a regional blackout, a volunteer at a community radio station, running on a backup generator, takes a call from someone who seems to know what will happen next. The writer knows the story is really about trust and responsibility: what do you broadcast when you cannot verify it, and people are listening in the dark? But they are stuck at the end of the first scene.

The low-control route is to ask for the whole story, accept the first polished result and start line-editing. That may produce readable prose. The cost is that the model has quietly decided the plot, tone, pacing, imagery and much of what the story means. The writer becomes the reviewer of a statistical first instinct.

A more intentional route begins before the AI is opened. The writer jots down three directions of their own, including one strange idea they are not sure about: *what if the caller is the volunteer's own voice, recorded years ago?* Then they ask for options rather than prose:

> Here is my premise, my theme and what must stay true: the volunteer is alone, the generator has four hours of fuel, and the station is the only source of news for the town. I'm stuck at the end of scene one. Give me five structurally different directions for scene two, not finished prose. Make them genuinely different: at least one should change who the caller is, one should raise the stakes for the town rather than the volunteer, and one should be quiet rather than dramatic. For each, say what question it forces the volunteer to answer.

An illustrative response might offer: the caller is a scammer testing whether the station will broadcast anything; the caller is a child reading from a notebook; a second caller contradicts the first; the generator starts failing so the volunteer must choose which message to use their remaining airtime on; or nothing happens for an hour and the tension is whether to broadcast the warning anyway.

Suppose the writer rejects three immediately because they are the kind of twist a reader has seen before. The fourth, the failing generator, is not quite right either, but it contains a better problem than anything on their own list: *airtime is running out, so every broadcast is a choice about what matters most.* That is the moment of value. The AI did not write the story. It changed the shape of the problem the writer is now solving.

The writer drafts scene two themselves. Later, they bring AI back in a different role:

> Read this scene as a critical first reader, not a co-writer. Don't rewrite it. Tell me where the volunteer's behaviour stopped being convincing, what promise the opening makes to the reader, and whether any question is answered too early.

Treat that critique as suggestions to assess, not corrections to obey. Some of it will be generic (“consider adding more sensory detail” is the editorial equivalent of “drink more water”). Some may be sharp. The writer decides.

The difference between the two routes is not a rule that AI may generate ideas but must never generate a sentence. It is that the writer chooses its role. Asking for alternative scene mechanics can open a blocked path. Repeatedly asking the model to “make this more compelling” can gradually smooth a manuscript into competent, familiar prose that no longer sounds like the person who wrote it.

Here is what that smoothing looks like at the scale of one line. A writer's draft reads:

> *The generator coughed like it had opinions.*

Ask for it to be “more polished” and a likely response is something like:

> *The generator sputtered ominously, its uneven rhythm filling the small studio with tension.*

The second version is more conventional and arguably more correct. It is also blander, longer and less like anyone in particular. Do that to every sentence and a distinctive voice can drift towards the middle of everything the model has learned. That is a more durable risk than the old claim that AI writing is obviously bad. Modern systems write fluent sentences. They can also invent historical details in realistic fiction, lose track of continuity across a long manuscript, reach for familiar plot shapes and produce editorial advice that sounds authoritative but would apply to any story ever written.

The same principles apply well beyond fiction. Suppose you have to give a toast at your sister's wedding. AI is genuinely useful for structure: how long should it be, how do you move from the funny story to the sincere bit, how do you end without mumbling? It can suggest an order for your material and tell you when a section runs long. What it cannot do is supply the stories. Ask it to “write a heartfelt wedding speech for my sister” and you will get something warm, fluent and interchangeable with ten thousand other speeches, possibly including an anecdote that never happened. Give it your three real memories and ask for help arranging them, and you keep the only thing that makes the speech worth hearing.

Publishing adds another layer. Amazon's Kindle Direct Publishing, for example, currently distinguishes AI-*generated* material from AI-*assisted* work, and requires publishers to disclose generated text, images or translations, while not requiring disclosure of assisted work under that policy.[^ch8-kdp] That is one platform's rule, not a copyright ruling or a universal definition. It is still evidence that even platforms cannot sensibly treat every use of AI as the same kind of creative contribution. If you intend to publish, read the current rules of the place you are publishing before you start, not after.

And sometimes the right AI decision is not to use it. If you already know what you want to say, writing it directly may be faster than briefing and correcting a model. If the piece depends on facts, you still need primary sources. If the point of the work is that you lived it, as in a memoir or a eulogy, no amount of fluency substitutes for the details only you know.

### Art and Image Creation: Fast Concepts, Hidden Decisions

Image generators can now produce polished illustrations, photorealistic scenes, concept art, thumbnails, moodboards, backgrounds, textures and endless variations. Editing tools can expand an image beyond its borders, replace an object, or keep part of a composition while changing the rest. The old joke about counting fingers is not a serious guide to current capability, and it will age even faster than this sentence.

For people who have never been able to draw, this can be genuinely enabling. You can visualise a garden layout, a character for a story you are telling your children, a colour scheme for a room, or a costume idea for a school play. There is a real difference between *visualising what I mean* and *commissioning finished professional art*, and the first of those has become dramatically more accessible.

For people who do draw, the largest change is the cost of options. A concept artist can explore twenty directions before committing to the one that will take a week to paint. That changes the economics of the early stage of a project, which is also where much of the thinking happens.

Consider a worked example. A small community group is organising a neighbourhood food festival and needs a poster. Its brief is real: the event should feel welcoming rather than corporate; the poster must work as an A3 print, a social-media tile and a black-and-white handout; and it must carry exact dates, sponsor logos and a booking link. Nobody on the committee is a designer.

AI is useful here for exploration. A good request asks for genuinely different directions rather than one “best” answer:

> We're making a poster for a free neighbourhood food festival. The mood should be warm and local, not corporate or futuristic. Generate three visually distinct directions: one based on photography of people sharing food at a long table, one based on bold hand-drawn illustration, and one built mostly from big, playful shapes and colour. No text in the images; we'll add it later.

Looking at the three results side by side, the committee may discover something it could not have articulated in advance: everyone prefers the warm photographic feel, and nobody likes the illustration. That is useful even if none of the generated pictures survives into the final poster. It is often easier to react to a picture than to describe a preference.

Now look closely at what the model decided that nobody asked it to decide. The committee asked for “people sharing food at a long table”. Who are those people? How old are they, what do they look like, what are they wearing, what food is on the table, what kind of street is behind them? The model had to fill all of that in, and its defaults come from patterns in its training data. Peer-reviewed audits of generated images of professions, including an Australian study of generated images of paramedics, have found demographic imbalances and stereotyped patterns in the models and prompts tested.[^ch8-bias] That does not mean every image is biased in the same way. It means the model has quietly done some casting and art direction for you.

A useful habit is to ask one plain question of every generated image you plan to use: *what did the model decide that I never specified?* If the festival is in a neighbourhood where half the residents are over sixty and a third were born overseas, a table of identical-looking thirty-year-olds tells a different story from the one the committee wants to tell.

Rights and originality matter before public use. A generated image can resemble an existing photograph or illustration more closely than you would like, especially if you asked for something very specific or famous. A prompt that names a living illustrator's style raises different issues from copying one of their pictures, but “style is not a copyrighted work” is not a moral permission slip. We will unpack those differences later in the chapter. For now, the practical point is that the committee should treat generated images as direction-finding material unless it is confident about the tool's terms, the output's originality and the people depicted.

Training provenance also varies more than public arguments suggest. Adobe says its own Firefly models are trained on licensed content, such as Adobe Stock, and public-domain material, rather than customer content or indiscriminate web scraping, and it describes bonus payments to eligible Stock contributors.[^ch8-firefly] That is a company's account of its own system, not independent proof that every contributor is happy with it; Adobe's own contributor material says Stock contributors cannot individually opt out of that training under the relevant agreement. It is still an important counterexample to the idea that every image model has the same training-data story.

The final production of the poster is a different job, and we will come back to it in the design section. Exact typography, sponsor logos, the booking link and print requirements are better handled with ordinary design tools than by asking an image generator to paint text.

### Music and Audio: From a Second Opinion to a Complete Synthetic Song

AI music covers a wider continuum than “generate a backing track”. At the assistance end, a musician can ask for chord or arrangement ideas, audition different instrumentation, clean a noisy recording, separate a mixed track into parts, or generate sound effects. Further along, AI can produce a full demo, a synthetic vocal or a convincing imitation of a particular voice. At the far end, a short text description produces an entire song: lyrics, composition, performance and recording, in about the time it takes to read this paragraph.

Return to the songwriter from the start of the chapter. She has written a verse and a chorus, both built on the same four chords: G, E minor, C and D. The chorus feels flat because it never goes anywhere the verse has not been. A chat-based assistant can be a useful second opinion here, without touching the melody:

> My song is in G major. The verse uses G–Em–C–D and the chorus currently repeats it. I want the chorus to feel like it lifts and opens up without changing key. Suggest three chord approaches for the chorus, explain in plain language why each would feel different, and don't write any lyrics or melody.

Useful suggestions might include starting the chorus on C instead of G, so the section begins somewhere new and feels like it has opened up; borrowing an F chord, which does not strictly belong in G major but gives many pop and rock choruses a bright, anthemic lift; or holding back the home chord, G, until the last line so the chorus feels unresolved until it lands. Those are ordinary songwriting techniques, well within what a model has learned from music theory and countless songs. The songwriter tries all three on her guitar. The second one is the one that makes her sit up. The melody, lyrics and final decision remain hers.

Compare that with someone who types “upbeat summer pop song about road trips” into a music generator and accepts the result. Both people used AI to make music. One used it as a variation engine inside her own song. The other used AI-led generation in which the model decided the composition, the performance and the recording. Neither description tells you whether the result is enjoyable. It does tell you why the word *ownership* needs unpacking.

Music has unusually many layers of rights. There can be separate interests in the lyrics, the composition, the sound recording, a performance, any samples and the identity of the voice. A service may let a paid subscriber commercialise a generated track while copyright law in that subscriber's country gives uncertain or limited protection to the generated parts. Suno's current help material makes that distinction unusually explicit: songs made on its free plan are for non-commercial use, paid-plan subscribers are given ownership and commercial-use rights under Suno's terms, and the same material warns that this does not guarantee the song is eligible for copyright protection.[^ch8-suno] Permission from the tool is one question. Copyright under the law is another. We will return to that distinction because it is one of the most practically useful in the chapter.

Voice cloning adds a further question: consent. A synthetic voice that sounds like a real singer or actor may involve contracts, performer protections, personality or publicity rights, misleading-conduct rules and platform policies. Copyright in a new melody does not answer whether someone's vocal identity was appropriated. Australia, for instance, has no single general “right of publicity” of the kind recognised in some US states; complaints about the unauthorised use of someone's identity tend to be argued through other routes, such as passing off or misleading-conduct law. In covered US productions, the performers' union SAG-AFTRA has negotiated requirements for separate, reasonably specific written consent before certain digital replicas are made or used.[^ch8-replicas] That is a contractual example from one industry and one country, not a rule for the whole world, but it makes the underlying principle concrete: a voice is not just another file.

The industry's response is also more varied than “lawsuit or nothing”. Universal Music Group settled its dispute with the AI music company Udio in October 2025 and announced a licensed platform trained on authorised music. A month later, Warner Music Group and Suno announced a partnership that included licensed models and opt-in controls for artists over the use of their names, likenesses, voices and compositions.[^ch8-licensing] These are commercial announcements by interested parties, and whether the promised arrangements work as described needs checking over time. They still show that consent and payment can be built into how some systems are made.

Music also shows what happens when making something becomes extremely cheap. The streaming service Deezer has been publishing figures from its own system for detecting fully AI-generated tracks. At the launch of that system in January 2025, such tracks were about 10 per cent of daily uploads to Deezer. By April 2026 the figure was about 44 per cent. In July 2026, Deezer reported that fully AI-generated tracks had exceeded half of daily uploads at a June peak, about 90,000 tracks a day. Yet those tracks accounted for only about 1 to 3 per cent of listening on Deezer. Deezer has also reported that a large share of the streams those tracks did receive were fraudulent, generated to extract royalty payments rather than because anyone wanted to listen, and that it demonetises the streams it identifies as fraudulent.[^ch8-deezer]

![When making content gets cheap, supply can flood in without attention following it. On Deezer in June 2026, over half of daily uploads at peak were fully AI-generated, yet about 1–3 per cent of listening went to those tracks. What becomes scarce is discovery, trust, curation, identity and an audience.](../diagrams/creative-abundance-bottleneck.mmd){ width=40% }

Those are one company's figures, from its own detection system and policies. They are not a measure of the whole music industry, and they say nothing directly about Spotify, Apple Music or YouTube. Spotify, for its part, said in September 2025 that it had removed more than 75 million “spammy” tracks over the previous year and was strengthening protections against mass uploads and impersonation.[^ch8-spotify] Be careful with that number: Spotify called those tracks spam, not AI-generated. Plenty of spam predates generative AI.

The contrast is the point. Half of the uploads did not become half of the listening. Abundant production does not create equivalent attention. When it becomes easier to make another song, image, article or video, the bottleneck moves. “Can this be made?” stops being the hard question. “Why should anyone find, trust or care about this one?” becomes harder. Discovery, curation, reputation and a relationship with an audience become more valuable, and so do the platforms that decide what gets recommended.

### Video and Filmmaking: The Size of the Intervention Matters

Video AI can help with storyboards, previsualisation, concept shots, cleanup, rotoscoping, background replacement, effects, short generated inserts, captions, dubbing and translation. It can also generate synthetic presenters, performances and entire sequences. The value, and the risk, change dramatically depending on how big the intervention is. It helps to think of five different jobs:

1. **planning a shot** that will later be filmed for real;
2. **altering an existing shot** in a bounded way;
3. **generating a short insert** that was never filmed;
4. **producing an entire sequence** synthetically; and
5. **depicting a real person** doing or saying something they did not do.

These are not five points on the same scale of “more AI”. They are different kinds of act.

Consider a small bike repair shop planning a 30-second video for social media. Before anyone picks up a camera, the owner uses AI to generate a rough storyboard and a short moving previsualisation comparing two ideas: a fast montage of repairs, or a single continuous shot following a customer's bike from broken to fixed. The previsualisation reveals that the continuous shot needs a larger workshop than they have. That saving happened before a single hour of filming. AI made planning cheaper. It did not decide what the video should say about the business.

At the editing stage, the owner finds the final shot is two seconds too short for the voice-over. Tools such as Adobe Premiere's Generative Extend can create additional frames to lengthen a clip.[^ch8-premiere] Here the editor has already chosen the footage, the moment and the purpose. AI supplies a bounded transformation, closer to a very clever version of an ordinary editing tool than to a new creative author.

Now suppose the owner is tempted to generate a short clip of a happy customer saying, “Best repair I've ever had.” The generated person does not exist and never said it. That is no longer a production shortcut. A fabricated testimonial can mislead customers, whatever tool made it. The technical step is small; the ethical and legal step is large.

For a home example, think of restoring a family video. AI tools can reduce background noise in a wedding speech recorded on a phone, stabilise shaky footage, or colour-correct a faded clip from the 1990s. Those are production-assistant jobs that preserve what happened. Generating a new clip of a late grandparent saying words they never said is a different kind of act, even if it is made with love. Some families may find it moving. Others may find it deeply uncomfortable. The difference is not the software; it is what the audience believes they are seeing.

Video also exposes limitations that surface polish can hide. A single generated shot can look extraordinary. Keeping the same character, costume, lighting and geography consistent across twenty shots is much harder, and asking for one precise change often produces a newly generated alternative rather than the same shot with one thing different. A simple cut in ordinary editing software may be quicker and more repeatable than regenerating a clip until it behaves. And documentary footage, interviews, product demonstrations, safety instructions and testimony depend on showing what actually happened. For those, generation is not a shortcut; it is a different product.

Disclosure rules increasingly reflect this difference. YouTube's current policy asks creators to disclose content that is meaningfully altered or synthetically generated when it seems realistic, such as making a real person appear to say something they did not, altering footage of real events or places, or generating a realistic scene that did not happen. Its examples of content that needs disclosure include cloning someone else's voice and synthetically generating music. Using AI to help write an outline, generate captions or make minor fixes is treated differently.[^ch8-youtube] A platform rule is not copyright law, but it captures a useful principle: using AI to plan a shot is not the same audience issue as making a real person appear to do something they never did.

### Design: More Options Are Not a Solved Brief

Design platforms can now generate layout directions, presentation concepts, campaign variants, mockups, colour options, copy in context and resized versions for different formats. Canva's Magic Design, for example, generates a set of layout options from a short description or an uploaded image.[^ch8-magic-design] This is useful because much early design work is comparative. It is often faster to react to six visible directions than to describe an abstract preference.

Imagine a local bakery refreshing its look. It needs a new menu board, a printed price list and a set of templates for weekly social-media posts. The owner types “modern bakery menu” and receives several attractive options in seconds. Any of them might look good. None of them knows the things that make this particular design problem hard:

- The menu board is read from three metres away by people in a queue, so prices and item names must be legible at a distance.
- Some customers have low vision, so text and background colours need strong contrast.
- The bakery already has a logo and two brand colours, which must be used correctly rather than approximately.
- Allergen information is legally and practically important, so it cannot be squeezed into decorative small print.
- The price list is printed by a local shop that needs a particular file format with bleed, so the design must be exact rather than approximately right.
- The weekly posts will be updated by a staff member who is not a designer, so the templates need to be simple and hard to break.

A stronger workflow starts with those constraints. The owner, or a designer working with them, might ask AI for broad directions based on the brief, pick the one that feels right, and then build or refine the final versions in a tool that provides exact layout, editable type, the real logo files and predictable print output. Then they test the result at its final size: print the price list, stand three metres from the menu board, check the contrast. A beautiful preview on a laptop proves very little about a menu board in a busy shop.

This is also where the festival poster from the art section gets finished. The generated photograph might become the background, but the dates, sponsor logos and booking link are set in a layout tool, the QR code is generated by a tool that makes QR codes, and the whole thing is checked at A3 and in black and white. AI explored; conventional tools produced; people decided.

Research gives another reason not to equate more generated options with more creative exploration. In a study of visual ideation involving 60 participants, those given support from an AI image generator showed more *design fixation*, meaning they got stuck on the features of early examples, and produced fewer, less varied and less original ideas in the task studied than participants without that support.[^ch8-fixation] Seeing polished suggestions can anchor a designer before they have explored their own directions. We will come back to this when we ask whether AI kills creativity, because it is one of the most important findings in the chapter.

Expertise and workflow stage matter too. Researchers running two design experiments found that the benefits of generative AI differed between the ideation stage and the implementation stage, and between less and more experienced participants. In one implementation condition, higher-expertise participants took about 57 per cent longer with AI without any improvement in quality.[^ch8-design-workflow] That does not prove experts should avoid AI. It shows why “AI makes design faster” is a claim about a particular stage of a particular workflow, not a law of nature. An expert who knows exactly what they want can lose time coaxing a probabilistic tool towards a result a conventional tool would produce precisely.

### When a Conventional Tool or a Person Is Better

A reader who knows when *not* to use generative AI has learned something important about using it well. Generative tools shine when many different answers would be acceptable and variation is a feature. They become frustrating when the target is exact. “Make something interesting” is easy for them. “Change only this one kerning value, keep every other property, and give me the editable vector file” is not.

The table below summarises the pattern across the five domains. Treat it as a description of current workflow fit, not a permanent verdict on what AI will ever do.

| Domain | Generative AI often helps with | A conventional tool or a person is often better for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing | exploring directions, restructuring, critique, changing tone | facts needing primary sources, a distinctive final voice, lived experience |
| Images | concepts, moodboards, variations, visualising an idea | exact vector work, technical drawing, logos and trade marks, documentary photography |
| Music | chord and arrangement ideas, demos, cleanup | a specific human performance, precise mixing and mastering, a clear rights chain |
| Video | storyboards, previsualisation, bounded fixes, localisation drafts | documentary truth, testimony, exact continuity, simple edits |
| Design | fast variants, early directions, resizing | typography, accessible design systems, print production, brand identity |

Sometimes a stock image with a clear licence is safer than a generated one. Sometimes a template already solves the layout. Sometimes hiring a professional is better, because they will recognise a cultural, production or legal constraint that you did not know to mention. And sometimes human performance is the whole point: nobody buys a ticket to watch a live band press “generate”.

## Effort vs. Judgement, Applied to Creative Work

### Where the Chapter 6 Distinction Bends

Chapter 6 offered a useful distinction: some tasks are effort-heavy, and handing them to AI removes friction you may not value; other tasks are judgement-heavy, and handing those over means surrendering decisions that should stay yours. Choosing a dinner recipe is mostly effort. Choosing a university course is mostly judgement.

It is tempting to apply that neatly to creative work: AI does the execution, the human supplies the vision. Creative work shows where that split breaks down.

A writer makes judgements while choosing one word over another. An illustrator makes judgements through line weight, shape and pressure. A musician makes judgements about timing, tone and phrasing while performing, not just before. A film editor changes the meaning of a scene by moving a cut a few frames. A designer establishes hierarchy through spacing and scale. Execution is not a pile of neutral labour completed after the important thinking is done. **Craft contains judgement.** Handing over the “effort” can mean handing over thousands of small decisions that, together, are the work.

That does not make the Chapter 6 distinction useless. Plenty of creative tasks really are mostly effort: resizing a design for six platforms, removing hum from a recording, generating twenty title options to shake loose your own. But in creative work, effort and judgement are better understood as a loop than as two separate stages:

![Creative work is a loop: frame the intention, make or generate material, inspect what appeared, select or reject, revise, then test against intention, audience, rights and context. If it is worth using, publish it; if not yet, go round again; if not at all, stop. AI can join any step, and what it shows first can shape what you judge next.](../diagrams/creative-judgement-loop.mmd){ width=48% }

Judgement appears throughout that loop, not only at the end. AI can take part throughout as well. It can help frame questions, generate material, rank options, suggest revisions and check a result against criteria you supply. That makes it more powerful than an automatic brush used at the start. It also means it can influence the judgement, not merely save the effort.

### How AI Shapes the Choice Before You Make It

Here is the subtle part. If a model supplies the first six concepts, those concepts can become anchors: the reference points against which everything else is judged. If it rewrites every sentence towards the same fluent middle, it can change what the writer begins to hear as “finished”. If an image model repeatedly supplies the same visual stereotypes, it becomes an uncredited casting director and art director. The final choice may still be human, but the menu of choices did not appear from nowhere.

This is why “the human makes the final decision” is a weaker reassurance than it sounds. A final decision made from a menu someone else wrote is still a real decision, but it is not the same as one made from a menu you wrote yourself.

There is also a practical reversal worth noticing. AI can remove the cost of *making* options and increase the cost of *deciding* among them. Generating fifty logo concepts takes minutes. Reviewing fifty logo concepts properly, checking each for resemblance to existing marks, testing them at small sizes and discussing them with a client, takes much longer than reviewing five. Chapter 7's workday test applies here too: count the whole workflow, including the looking, not just the generating.

### Contribution, Accessibility and What You Want to Keep

The human contribution also differs enormously along the loop. Supplying a detailed sketch, arranging generated elements and painting over them is not the same contribution as accepting the first result from five words. Writing lyrics and melody and using AI to audition an arrangement differs from generating the whole song. These differences matter for how you describe your work, for whether copyright may protect it, and for your own sense of what you made.

Accessibility belongs in this picture as a genuine benefit, not a debating point. Generative tools can help people express an idea when drawing, typing, operating complex software, affording equipment or working in a second language would otherwise be a barrier. They can let a non-musician hear the arrangement they imagine, or give someone with a motor impairment a way into visual creation that a stylus never offered. The evidence on long-term creative participation is thinner than the evidence from short experiments, so sweeping claims about “democratising creativity” are premature. The practical possibility is real, and it does not cancel separate concerns about training consent, payment or professional displacement. Both things can be true at once.

One last question belongs here, because it cannot be answered by any study: which parts of the loop do *you* want to keep doing? Offloading effort is ordinary. We use spellcheck, calculators and autofocus without angst. But some effort is also practice. A novice writer who always asks AI to draft the scene may never find out what their own scenes sound like. A designer who always starts from generated concepts may sketch less. A musician who uses AI for backing arrangements but still writes every melody has made a deliberate choice about which skill to grow. There is no universal answer, only a question worth asking before habit answers it for you.

## Is AI Stealing?

### One Accusation, Several Different Claims

The word *stealing* compresses too many arguments into one accusation. That is part of why online arguments about it go nowhere: people are often answering different questions while believing they are disagreeing about the same one.

When a creator says AI stole their work, they may mean any of these:

1. A copy of their work was put into a training dataset without permission.
2. The dataset was built from a source they consider illegitimate, such as a pirated library.
3. Their work was used to build a commercial product and nobody paid them.
4. A licence condition or a “do not use” signal was ignored.
5. The model memorised their work and can reproduce it.
6. A particular output is substantially similar to something they made.
7. Outputs imitate their recognisable style.
8. Their voice or likeness is being imitated.
9. A system built partly from creative work now competes with the people who made that work.
10. Consent should have been required, even if the law permits the use.

These overlap, and a single complaint may involve several. But they are not the same technical fact, legal test or ethical judgement. It helps to sort them into four kinds of question:

![One accusation, four kinds of question. Technical: was the work copied into a dataset, and can the model reproduce it? Legal: did a licence or exception apply, and does an output infringe? Ethical: should consent or credit have been required? Economic: was anyone paid, and does the system compete with the creator? Each can have a different answer, and the legal answer changes by country.](../diagrams/four-questions-inside-ai-stealing.mmd){ width=62% }

The rest of this section takes those questions roughly in order. To answer the technical one, we first need to understand what training actually does.

### Training Data in Plain English

Let us clear up a few terms, because the argument depends on them.

**Training data** is the collection of examples used to build a model: text, images, audio, video, code, or a mixture. **Training** is the process of building the model from those examples. A useful way to picture it: the system is shown an example, makes a prediction about it (what word comes next, what this noisy image should look like when cleaned up), checks how wrong it was, and nudges an enormous set of internal numbers so it would be slightly less wrong next time. Then it does that again, billions of times. Those internal numbers are called **parameters** or **weights**. When training finishes, the parameters encode patterns: how sentences tend to go, what cats tend to look like, how a chorus tends to resolve.

**Generation** is what happens when you use the model. Your prompt goes in, and the model produces a new output by drawing on those learned patterns.

The important consequence is that a model is not normally a scrapbook. When you ask for a poem about the sea, it is not usually searching a hidden library for the closest poem and cutting out pieces to paste together. The patterns from millions of examples are blended into the same shared set of numbers. That is why the claim “AI just copies and pastes” is misleading.

**Retrieval** is different again. Some AI products can search the web or a document library at the moment you ask, fetch relevant material and hand it to the model to use in its answer. That is closer to looking something up. Many products combine training and retrieval, but they are different mechanisms with different questions attached: retrieval raises questions about quoting and attributing the source it fetched, while training raises questions about what was copied to build the model in the first place.

![Training adjusts a vast set of numbers using many examples; it does not build a folder of works. Generation turns your prompt into a new output from those learned patterns. Retrieval, when a product offers it, fetches a source at the moment you ask and hands it to the model. The exception: some training examples, often duplicated ones, can be memorised.](../diagrams/training-generation-retrieval.mmd){ width=62% }

Now the complication, because the opposite claim, “AI never copies anything”, is also wrong. Security researchers have shown that language models can sometimes reproduce passages of their training text word for word, and that image-generation models can sometimes reproduce images from their training data closely enough to be recognisable. Material that appears many times in the training data is more likely to be memorised; removing duplicates from training data has been shown to reduce the problem.[^ch8-memorisation] These demonstrations concern particular models and methods, and they do not give a “copying rate” for today's tools. But they establish the mechanism. Most generation is not a database lookup. Some examples can nevertheless be memorised strongly enough to reappear. Both facts matter, and anyone who tells you only one of them is selling something.

Training data also varies far more than the phrase “trained on the internet” suggests. Depending on the developer and the model, training material can include publicly available web pages, licensed collections, public-domain works whose copyright has expired, private datasets, material users have submitted where terms permit, carefully curated examples and synthetic data produced by other models. The European Union's template for summarising training data, which providers of general-purpose models must now publish, has separate categories for many of these sources.[^ch8-eu] The Firefly example from the art section shows that data sourcing can even be part of how a product is sold. When someone says “AI was trained on stolen art”, the fair follow-up question is: *which AI, trained on what, obtained how?*

### The Strongest Case on Each Side

With the mechanics clearer, we can look at the argument itself. Each side deserves its strongest version, not a caricature.

**The case for requiring permission and payment** runs like this. Creative works embody years of skill, labour and investment. Building a commercial model involves copying those works at enormous scale, usually without asking. The resulting systems can generate material that competes in the same markets as the people whose work helped build them. At internet scale, individual creators cannot realistically inspect datasets, negotiate licences or enforce their rights, so an opt-out system places the burden on the people with the least power. The argument does not need to prove that every output is a copy. It is a claim about permission to use protected work to build a commercial product, and about who shares in the value that results.

That position is widely held among Australian creators. In a 2024 survey of 419 Australian authors and illustrators, 91 per cent considered it unfair for their work to be used to train AI without compensation. In the Australian Society of Authors' 2025 survey of 928 respondents, 87 per cent said that, to their knowledge, neither they nor their publishers had given permission for their work to be used in training, and 89 per cent were concerned about AI imitating their style.[^ch8-creators] The Society uses the word “theft”. Those surveys come from a self-selecting group of respondents and an advocacy organisation, so they tell us what these creators think and feel, not what a court would decide. But they should not be softened into “some creators have concerns”. Many are angry, and they can explain precisely why.

**The case for broad room for machine analysis** runs differently. Copyright has traditionally protected particular expression, not the ideas, facts, styles and patterns that can be learned from it. People have always been free to read a thousand novels and learn how novels work. Analysing large bodies of material has real social value in research, search, accessibility, archives and science. Requiring a licence for every work in a web-scale dataset may be practically impossible, and a mandatory licensing system could entrench the few companies wealthy enough to pay, shutting out researchers, smaller developers and open-source projects. Organisations such as Creative Commons and the Electronic Frontier Foundation have made versions of these arguments, while also discussing ways for creators to signal their preferences.[^ch8-open-culture]

Notice that the two positions are not simply mirror images. The first is mostly about consent and the distribution of value. The second is mostly about the scope of copyright and the costs of restricting analysis. It is possible to find parts of both persuasive, and many people do.

The familiar comparison with human learning sits in the middle of this argument, so it is worth being precise about it. It helps with one narrow point: learning patterns from many examples is genuinely different from cutting and pasting, and a model is not a collage machine. It fails if it is asked to settle the whole debate. A person who reads a thousand novels does not make a digital copy of each one, cannot write ten thousand novels an hour, is not a commercial product owned by a company, and cannot usually be prompted to recite page 212 of a book they read once. Scale, automated copying, commercial development, memorisation and market effects are exactly the things the analogy leaves out, and they are exactly the things the law tends to care about.

Licensing is emerging as a third path between unrestricted use and prohibition. The music-industry agreements mentioned earlier, and Adobe's contributor arrangements, show that systems for consent and payment can exist.[^ch8-licensing] They do not solve everything. Identifying millions of contributors, valuing their work fairly, handling work whose rights holder cannot be found, and avoiding a market where only the largest firms can afford to train are all unresolved. But the choice is not only between “anything goes” and “nothing is allowed”.

### Legal, Ethical and Economic Answers Can Diverge

This is the distinction most public arguments skip. A use can be:

- **legally permitted but ethically contested**, for example a use that falls within an exception while the creator never consented;
- **licensed but still economically disruptive**, for example a model trained entirely on paid-for material that still undercuts the market for the same kind of work;
- **technically non-infringing at the output stage** while still raising concerns about consent and credit at the training stage; or
- **ethically objectionable to many people without being infringement**, because ethical norms can reach further than current law.

So “it's legal, therefore it's fine” is a non-sequitur. So is “it's wrong, therefore it must be illegal”. And whether something is legal depends heavily on where you are.

### Why the Answer Changes at the Border

Copyright is national law, shaped by international agreements but applied country by country. That is why global slogans such as “AI training is legal” or “AI training is illegal” are unhelpful. Here is a compact orientation for four places this book's readers are most likely to care about, with Australia first. It is not legal advice, and every line is the kind of thing that changes.

| Place | Training, in brief | AI-assisted output, in brief |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Australia | Specific fair-dealing exceptions, no broad fair use; government not considering a text-and-data-mining exception | Human authorship is central; purely generated material may not be protected |
| United States | Fact-specific “fair use”; courts are reaching different results on different facts | Human-authored contributions can be protected; prompting alone generally is not enough |
| European Union | Text-and-data-mining exceptions with conditions, including creators' ability to reserve rights | Depends on originality; AI Act adds transparency duties for model providers |
| United Kingdom | Existing exception only for non-commercial research; broader reform still unresolved | A special rule for some computer-generated works, which the government has proposed removing |

**Australia** does not have the open-ended “fair use” doctrine that the United States has. Instead, the *Copyright Act 1968* lists specific exceptions, called fair dealing, for purposes such as research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, and reporting news. In October 2025, the Australian Government said it was not considering a text-and-data-mining exception of the kind some other countries have, and asked its Copyright and Artificial Intelligence Reference Group to focus on licensing arrangements for AI, greater certainty about how copyright applies to AI-generated material, and cheaper ways to enforce rights when outputs infringe.[^ch8-australia] That is an important correction for Australian readers who have absorbed American assumptions from the internet. It is not, however, a court ruling that every training use infringes. On the output side, Australian copyright has traditionally required a human author. The Arts Law Centre's current guidance explains that material generated by AI without sufficient human authorship may not be protected, while human creative contributions to an AI-assisted work can be.[^ch8-australia] Exactly how much prompting, selecting and editing is enough has not been settled.

**The United States** decides many of these questions through fair use, a flexible test that weighs the purpose of the use, the nature of the work, how much was used, and the effect on the market for the original. The US Copyright Office's 2025 report on training does not declare all training lawful or unlawful. It says the answer depends on facts including what the model is for, where the training copies came from and whether the outputs compete with the originals, and it treats commercial use of huge bodies of expressive works to create competing expressive material as a much harder case, particularly where the sources were obtained unlawfully.[^ch8-us] The courts illustrate the point. In a case brought by authors against Anthropic, the trial judge ruled in 2025 that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use, but that building a library from pirated copies was a separate matter; the case then settled for US$1.5 billion, a settlement the court gave final approval in July 2026. And at the end of September 2026, a federal appeals court upheld Thomson Reuters' win against ROSS Intelligence, which had used Westlaw's legal summaries to build a competing legal-research tool, although the court's full reasoning had not yet been published.[^ch8-us-cases] Neither case decides the question for every generative model. Together they show why “US courts have decided AI training is fair use” and “US courts have decided it isn't” are both wrong. On outputs, the Copyright Office's position is that sufficiently human-authored contributions to an AI-assisted work can be protected, including creative selection, arrangement or modification, but that prompting alone generally does not make the user the author of what the model generated.[^ch8-us]

**The European Union** has two specific text-and-data-mining exceptions. One allows research organisations and cultural institutions to mine works for scientific research. The other allows mining of lawfully accessible works more broadly, but not where the rights holder has expressly reserved that use, which for material published online should be done in a machine-readable way. Separately, the EU's AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models to have a policy for complying with EU copyright law, respect those reservations, and publish a summary of the content used for training.[^ch8-eu] That summary is not an item-by-item list of every work, and how these rules are enforced is still developing. “Europe allows AI training” is therefore an oversimplification: Europe allows some of it, on conditions.

**The United Kingdom** currently has a text-and-data-mining exception only for non-commercial research. The government consulted on a broader exception with an opt-out for rights holders, but in its March 2026 report said this was no longer its preferred approach, citing deep disagreement and gaps in the evidence, and committed to further work. A House of Lords committee had earlier warned, in strong terms, that creators' rights were under threat.[^ch8-uk] The UK is also a useful warning against another global myth. Its law has long contained a special category for certain works “generated by a computer in circumstances such that there is no human author”, protected for 50 years. So the claim that purely AI-generated work can never be copyrighted anywhere is not accurate as a description of current UK law, although the same 2026 report proposes removing that special protection.[^ch8-uk] A proposal is not yet a change in the law.

The point of this tour is not to make you a copyright lawyer. It is to show *why* the answer differs: different countries have made different choices about exceptions, authorship and transparency, and several are actively reconsidering them. If a real decision depends on the answer, the facts, the contracts, the source material, the output and the applicable law all matter, and professional advice is worth more than any chapter.

### Style, Voice and Likeness Are Different Problems

A common prompt is “an illustration in the style of [a named living illustrator]”. Another is “recreate this illustration”. Another is “make it sound like [a famous singer]”. These feel similar. They are different problems.

A **recognisable style**, such as a way of using line, colour and composition, is generally not protected by copyright in the same way as a particular work. Copyright tends to protect specific expression, not a manner of working, partly so that artists can learn from and respond to each other. A **specific protected work**, such as one particular illustration, is a different matter: reproducing it, or reproducing substantial parts of it, can infringe. A **creator's name or brand** raises further issues if an output is presented or sold in a way that suggests the creator made or endorsed it. A **person's likeness or voice** raises questions about consent, performer rights, personality or publicity rights, and misleading conduct. And the AI provider's own **terms** may prohibit some of these uses regardless of what copyright says.

So “style is not copyrighted, therefore anything goes” is wrong, and so is “style imitation is automatically copyright infringement”. A particular output can still copy protected elements. A prompt naming an artist can be evidence of deliberate imitation. A misleading presentation can create separate legal problems. And a living artist can have a strong ethical objection to their professional identity being turned into a prompt even when copyright offers them no direct remedy. That last point is the heart of many creators' anger: the thing they spent twenty years building, a recognisable way of seeing, can now be requested by name for free.

### “I Own It” Can Mean Five Different Things

This is one of the most practically useful distinctions in the chapter, because it affects anyone who plans to use AI-generated material publicly or commercially.

When someone says they own an AI-generated image, song, video or design, they may mean any of five different things:

| Question | What it actually asks | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. What does the provider's contract say? | Rights between you and the company | Canva's terms say that, as between Canva and you and to the extent the law allows, you own your outputs |
| 2. Am I allowed to use it commercially? | The permission in your plan or licence | Suno gives commercial-use rights on paid plans, not free ones |
| 3. Does copyright law protect it? | National law and human authorship | Suno itself warns that its terms do not guarantee copyright protection |
| 4. Is it exclusive to me? | Whether others can get the same or similar output | Canva warns that outputs may not be unique |
| 5. Could it infringe someone else's rights? | Third-party copyright, trade marks, voice, likeness, contracts | No provider's ownership wording removes other people's rights |

The provider examples come from their current terms and help material.[^ch8-canva][^ch8-suno] These statements can all be true at once. “The provider says I own it” does not automatically mean “the law gives me an exclusive copyright and nobody else can object”. And “commercial use allowed” does not mean “legally risk-free”; it means the provider will not object.

For work that matters commercially, a sensible practical habit is to keep a record of your process: your own sketches, drafts or source files; the tool, version and date; your prompts where available; licences for any stock or reference material; and your editing history. It will not guarantee anything, but if questions arise later about what you contributed and where the pieces came from, you will be able to answer them.

> **Watch Out:** Before publishing or selling important AI-generated material, check five things: the provider's current terms and your plan; the rights in anything you supplied as input; whether copyright is likely to protect your contribution where you live; whether the result could resemble a protected work, trade mark or real person; and the disclosure rules of the platform you are publishing on. If the rights chain matters and remains unclear, use material with a clearer licence or get professional advice.

### Disclosure, Provenance and the Myth of the AI Detector

Closely related is a question audiences increasingly ask: *how was this made?* Several different things get muddled together here.

**Disclosure** is simply telling people. Platforms such as YouTube and Amazon KDP now require it in certain situations, as we have seen. **Provenance** is a record of where a file came from and what happened to it. **Content Credentials**, based on a technical standard called C2PA, are one way of attaching that record to a file so that tampering with the record can be detected. Adobe, for example, says it automatically attaches Content Credentials to images generated entirely with Firefly, and OpenAI says it uses both C2PA metadata and invisible watermarking on images from its image-generation systems.[^ch8-provenance-tools] **Watermarking** hides a signal inside the content itself. **AI detection** tries to guess, from the content alone, whether AI made it.

Provenance is useful, but it is not a lie detector. A Content Credential can show that a participating tool recorded certain claims about a file's origin and editing. It cannot prove that a file *without* credentials was made by a human, because most files have no credentials at all and a screenshot or re-upload can strip them. It cannot prove that an image *with* credentials shows something that really happened, because a genuine record can describe a staged scene.[^ch8-c2pa] And guessing from the content alone has become harder as systems improve. In a survey commissioned by Deezer, 97 per cent of respondents failed to identify which of the tracks they heard were fully AI-generated.[^ch8-deezer-survey] That is one company-commissioned study, not a universal law of perception, but it points the same way as the platform rules: if people could reliably tell by looking or listening, platforms would not need to ask creators to disclose.

## Is AI Replacing Artists?

### Ask About Tasks, Not Titles

“Is AI replacing artists?” is too coarse a question to answer well. Replace the word *artist* with tasks and markets and it becomes answerable.

Think about what an illustrator actually does in a year. They interpret briefs, sketch concepts, explore compositions, render finished work, handle revisions, prepare files for print, invoice, negotiate rights, maintain a portfolio, build relationships with art directors, teach workshops and sell prints at markets. A writer researches, interviews, plans, drafts, revises, negotiates, promotes and answers reader emails. A musician composes, performs, arranges, records, tours and builds an audience. Each creative occupation is a bundle of tasks, and AI affects them unevenly.

AI can substitute for one task, speed up another, create demand for a third and be useless for a fourth. It can also change the terms of work without removing it: lower prices, higher expectations, tighter deadlines. It helps to name these effects separately:

- **Task substitution:** a client stops paying a person for a specific task because AI produces an adequate substitute, as with the illustrator's concept boards at the start of this chapter.
- **Occupation replacement:** the role as a whole disappears. This is a much bigger claim, and the evidence for it across creative occupations is much weaker.
- **Productivity change:** the same person produces more in the same time, which can raise their income, reduce how many people are hired, or lower prices. The direction is not automatic.
- **Job redesign:** the role survives but shifts towards direction, selection, editing, client work, rights checking or more complex tasks.
- **Price pressure and competition:** the work remains, but clients expect to pay less because cheaper alternatives exist and more people can now enter the market.
- **Market expansion:** lower costs create projects that would never have existed.

All of these can happen at once in the same industry. Arguing about whether “artists” are being replaced hides which one you are actually talking about.

### What the Measured Evidence Shows

Substitution is not purely hypothetical. Some of the clearest evidence comes from online freelancing platforms, where researchers can see job postings, prices and earnings before and after new AI tools arrived.

One study examined postings on a large online freelancing platform. After ChatGPT's release, postings in categories judged most exposed to automation, such as writing and some coding tasks, fell by about 21 per cent relative to more manual categories used for comparison. After image generators became widely available, postings for image-creation work fell by about 17 per cent. The jobs that remained in exposed categories tended to be more complex and better paid.[^ch8-freelance] A second study analysed more than three million freelance job postings. Total demand did not simply collapse. Underneath that stable total, however, clusters of work that AI could substitute for, particularly writing and translation, declined substantially compared with their expected trend, while demand for complementary work such as machine-learning and chatbot development grew strongly.[^ch8-reallocation] A third study found short-term reductions in both employment and earnings for freelancers in more exposed occupations, and found that a strong track record did not simply protect people from the effect.[^ch8-earnings]

It is worth being precise about what those studies show. They measured online freelance markets, mostly in the first year or two after major AI releases, by comparing more exposed kinds of work with less exposed kinds. They show that some paid tasks and commissions were substituted, that surviving work shifted, and that new kinds of work grew. They do not show a 21 per cent fall in all writing jobs or a 17 per cent fall in all illustration. They say little about novelists, staff designers, session musicians or film crews. And the platforms themselves are only one corner of the creative economy.

Still, “no whole profession has vanished” is poor comfort to the person whose category of work contracted. The third study is also a direct challenge to a comfortable reassurance creators are sometimes offered: that AI only replaces mediocre work and skilled professionals are safe. In the short term, at least in that market, skill did not work as a shield. That does not mean skill no longer matters. It means exposure depends on the task and the buyer as well as on the quality of the person doing it.

### What Creators Report, and Why That Is Different Evidence

Creator surveys add a different kind of evidence: what people in these professions are experiencing and expecting. In the 2024 survey of Australian authors and illustrators mentioned earlier, 70 per cent thought it possible or likely that generative AI would replace some of their income-generating work. In a survey of 4,274 music creators across Australia and New Zealand by the collecting society APRA AMCOS, 82 per cent were concerned about their ability to make a living.[^ch8-creators] In the United Kingdom, a January 2026 report by the Society of Authors and partner organisations, drawing on more than 10,000 creator responses, reported that about a third of illustrators said they had lost commissions, or had commissions cancelled, because of generative AI.[^ch8-soa]

Surveys like these are valuable. They record real experiences and serious fears from the people most affected. They are also usually run by organisations that advocate for those people, and respondents choose to take part, so they are not random samples of the whole creative workforce. Economic forecasts need even more care. The international body for authors' societies, CISAC, commissioned a study projecting that by 2028 about a quarter of music creators' revenues could be at risk from generative AI.[^ch8-cisac] That is a modelled scenario from an interested party, not a loss that has already happened. The right way to read all of this is to take the experiences seriously and to keep them in their proper category: testimony and forecasts, not economy-wide employment statistics.

The same surveys also complicate the idea of two camps. In the Australian Society of Authors' 2025 survey, about one in five respondents said they used generative AI somewhere in their creative practice. In the APRA AMCOS survey, 54 per cent agreed that AI can assist the human creative process, even though most were worried about their livelihoods.[^ch8-creators] Creator organisations such as the Authors Guild publish guidance on using AI in writing responsibly while continuing to campaign on rights and payment.[^ch8-practitioners] Many working creators use AI for some things and refuse it for others: moodboards but never final art, arrangement ideas but never a cloned voice, licensed models only, always disclosed. “Creators versus AI users” is a false division. Plenty of people are both.

### Why Lived Experience Is Not a Force Field

It is common, and comforting, to say that human art will always be valued because it comes from lived experience and a genuine point of view that no machine has. That can be an important source of value. A memoir, a protest song or a painting of the town you grew up in can matter precisely because a particular person made it.

But it is not an economic force field. If a buyer wants a generic product description, a background image for a blog post, a thirty-second music bed for a corporate video, or a quick concept image for an internal pitch, speed and price may matter much more to them than the maker's life story. An adequate synthetic substitute can remove paid work without possessing any lived experience at all. Substitution is most likely where the need is generic rather than distinctive, the brief is easy to specify, quality is easy for a non-expert to judge, revisions are cheap, and no one cares who made it. Much of the paid work that has historically supported creative careers has exactly those features.

Lived experience, reputation, live performance, cultural knowledge, accountability and a relationship with an audience are better understood as *differentiators*: reasons some buyers and audiences will choose a person, in some markets, for some work. They are real. They are not guarantees.

### New Work, Expanded Markets and the Apprenticeship Problem

Not every generated output replaces a commission. The grandmother drawing Biscuit for a bedtime story was never going to hire an illustrator. A small community group that could not afford a designer can now make a passable poster. An independent game team can prototype environments before seeking funding, and that project may later employ people. Some AI outputs substitute for paid work; others substitute for nothing, because the alternative was that nothing would be made. Both effects are real, and the existence of one does not cancel the other.

Entry-level work deserves particular attention. Junior copywriting, stock-style illustration, rough storyboards, simple social graphics, resizing, background music and basic editing are paid work, and they are also practice. They are how many people have learned their craft while being paid for it. They are also exactly the kinds of task that are easiest to specify and easiest to substitute. If fewer people are hired to do them, the traditional apprenticeship route into creative careers could narrow, and fewer people may eventually develop into the senior practitioners that industries rely on.

That concern is plausible and serious. It is not yet proven. There is early evidence that some lower-complexity online work is more exposed. The full chain, from fewer junior tasks, to fewer people developing expertise, to a later shortage of senior talent, will take years to observe, and the evidence is not yet mature. It is fair to treat it as an important risk worth watching and planning for. It is not fair to present it as something that has already happened.

The same tools can also intensify the jobs that survive. If ten design variants become cheap, a client may ask for fifty. A smaller team may be expected to produce more on shorter deadlines, spending less time making and more time reviewing machine output. Quantitative evidence for this across creative industries is still patchy, so treat it as a recurring job-redesign concern rather than a measured universal outcome. If you commission creative work, it is worth noticing whether “AI makes it faster” has quietly turned into “so it should cost less and take no time”.

Finally, return to Deezer. Flooding a platform with cheap supply does not mean audiences transfer their attention in the same proportion. A future creative economy may contain more substitution at the low-cost production end and more value attached to curation, trusted identity, live performance, community and proof of origin. That is a possibility supported by early signals, not a promise that every human creator will receive a premium.

The honest answer, then, is neither “AI replaces artists” nor “AI can never replace real creativity”. AI already substitutes for some creative tasks and is changing some markets. It also complements creative workers, opens participation to people who were shut out, and creates new work. Which effect dominates depends on the task, the buyer, the quality threshold, the price, the workflow and what the audience values.

## Does AI Kill Creativity?

### First, Which Creativity?

Before answering, it is worth deciding what *creativity* means, because much of the argument is really a disagreement about definitions.

Researchers who study creativity often use a standard two-part definition: a creative product is both original and effective, meaning new and also useful or appropriate for its purpose.[^ch8-definition] That is sensible: random nonsense is original but not useful, and a competent, conventional answer is useful but not original. Artists and audiences often care about more than that: intention, personal voice, surprise, craft, emotional communication, lived experience, cultural meaning and the relationship between a maker and a community.

This means “Can AI be creative?” is really several questions wearing one coat. Can a model generate something new? Can people rate its output as original, useful or beautiful? Can AI help a person produce a better-rated result? And does the model itself have intention, experience or a point of view? Experiments can say a great deal about the first three. The fourth is philosophy, and this chapter does not need to settle it. A generated image can score highly with human judges without settling whether anything meant anything by it.

Keeping those questions apart also prevents a common slide. A model scoring well on a creativity test is not evidence that it has human creative intention. A person producing a highly rated story with AI help is not evidence that their long-term creative ability improved. And a group producing more similar ideas is not evidence that each individual became less creative. Each of those claims needs its own evidence.

### Better Stories, More Similar Stories

The single most useful research result for this chapter sounds contradictory until you separate what was measured.

In a study published in 2024, 293 participants each wrote a very short story. Before writing, their baseline creativity was measured with a simple task that asks people to name words as unrelated to one another as possible. Some participants wrote without AI help. Some could see one story idea generated by AI. Others could see up to five AI-generated ideas. Other people then rated the stories on qualities including novelty, usefulness, how well written they were and how enjoyable they were, and the researchers measured how similar the stories were to one another.[^ch8-stories]

Access to AI ideas improved the average ratings. The gains were largest for participants who had started with lower creativity scores: for that group, access to five AI ideas raised novelty ratings by about 11 per cent, and ratings for how well written and how enjoyable the stories were rose by more than 20 per cent. Writers who were already highly creative gained little. At the same time, the AI-assisted stories became more similar to one another.

![In one short-story experiment with 293 writers, measuring story by story showed average ratings rose, most for writers with lower starting scores. Measuring across the group showed AI-assisted stories became more similar to one another. The same study produced both results: better single stories, and a narrower collection.](../diagrams/individual-uplift-group-similarity.mmd){ width=62% }

That is not a contradiction. One measure looks at each story on its own; the other looks at the whole collection. Imagine five people all beginning their brainstorming by asking the same model for ideas. Each may get a genuinely useful push. But if they all start from similar suggestions, the collection of work they produce together can converge around related patterns. Better average output is not the same thing as broader creativity. A bookshop full of slightly better, slightly more similar books is not obviously a win.

It is important to be equally clear about what this study does not show. The participants were not professional novelists. The stories were very short. The effect was measured immediately, not over months or years. It does not show that AI makes all writing the same, that it harms anyone's creative voice permanently, or what happens when writers use AI as a critic after drafting rather than as a source of starting ideas. Researchers have also argued about how far the group-level finding generalises. One team emphasised the risk that individual gains can come with collective losses in diversity. The authors of another study, which had found that ChatGPT helped people produce more creative solutions to everyday problems, replied that diversity measured from a single submitted idea should not be stretched into a universal statement about collective creativity.[^ch8-creativity-debate] Both sides of that exchange agree that the distinction between individual and group outcomes matters. They disagree about how big the problem is in real life.

### More Pictures, Fewer Ideas

The design study mentioned earlier found a related mechanism: fixation. Participants who were given AI image support during a visual ideation task produced fewer ideas, with less variety and less originality, than participants without it, and showed more signs of being stuck on the features of early examples.[^ch8-fixation]

That result surprises people, because the AI can generate dozens of images in the time it takes to sketch one. But the number of images on the screen is not the same as the number of directions in the person's head. Picture a designer who, before looking at any AI output, sketches four genuinely different ideas: A, B, C and D. Now picture a designer who opens the tool first, sees a striking version of A, and then spends the session producing A1, A2, A3 and A4. The second designer has more polished pictures. The first explored more.

Add the finding that experienced designers were slower with AI in one implementation task, and a picture emerges that is more useful than “AI helps” or “AI hurts”. The effect depends on *where in the process* AI is used, *who* is using it and *what they are trying to do*. Early ideation, where novelty matters most, may be exactly where AI suggestions are most likely to anchor you. Later refinement, once you know what you want, may be where they help most.

### What We Do Not Know Yet

Be disciplined about the limits. These studies used bounded tasks, particular systems and short time frames. They support careful claims about immediate effects: AI can improve some ratings, especially for less experienced people; it can anchor and fixate; it can make a group's output more similar; and effects differ by stage and expertise.

They do not establish that years of AI use destroy creative ability. Long-term evidence on skill development and dependency is still thin. The reverse claim, that routine AI use trains people to become more creative over time, is also unproven. Immediate performance and durable learning are different outcomes, just as Chapter 6 showed with a student whose AI-assisted homework improved while their understanding did not. If you worry about losing a skill, treat that as a reasonable concern to manage, not a proven fact to fear.

### Using the Evidence to Work Better

You do not need to wait for the long-term evidence to use what we already know. The research on anchoring and fixation suggests some practical techniques. They are reasonable inferences from that research, not tested cures:

- **Think first.** Write down several ideas of your own before opening the AI tool, including the strange one that might not work. Once you have seen a polished suggestion, your odd idea will look worse than it is.
- **Ask for families, not variations.** Request deliberately different directions (“one quiet, one funny, one that changes who the main character is”) rather than twenty cosmetic versions of the same idea.
- **Bring your own material.** Start from your sketch, your chords, your paragraph or your constraints rather than a blank prompt.
- **Use AI to challenge, not only to originate.** Ask it to find the weakness in your idea, argue the opposite, or say what a sceptical reader would notice.
- **Reject the obvious on purpose.** If the first suggestions are what anyone would think of, say so and ask for less expected options.
- **Compare routes.** Occasionally try the same small task human-first and AI-first, and notice which gives you a wider range.
- **Know when to stop.** When more generation is producing noise rather than useful options, close the tool and make a decision.

> **Try This:** Pick a small creative task: a birthday invitation theme, a story premise, a poster idea or the mood for a short video. First, write down three genuinely different directions without AI. Then ask an AI tool for three genuinely different directions. Before combining anything, compare the two lists. Which is more varied? Did any AI idea become an anchor for the rest? What did the AI decide that you had left unspecified? Finally, decide which part of this kind of task you would still like to practise yourself.

### What People Mean by “Authentic”

Arguments about whether AI-assisted work is authentic will continue, partly because people use the word to mean different things. For one person, authentic means entirely human-made. For another, it means based on lived experience. Others mean intentionally directed by a person, performed by a real person, honestly described to the audience, not impersonating anyone, a truthful record of a real event, or made according to the norms of a particular artistic community.

Once you notice that, many disagreements become clearer. A listener may happily accept a synthetic ambient track as background music, yet feel deceived by a cloned singer presented as a real performance. A reader may not mind that a novelist used AI to organise research, but care deeply that a memoir's experiences actually happened to its named author. A viewer may enjoy AI-generated fantasy art and be alarmed by AI-generated news footage. The technology is similar in each case. What changes is what the audience reasonably expects. A more useful question than “Is AI art authentic?” is: *what would a reasonable audience expect here, and does the way this was made break that expectation?*

Historical comparisons can help, as long as they are not used to end the conversation. Photography challenged painting and changed what painters were paid to do, but a camera captures light from a scene; it is not built by processing millions of existing photographs. Synthesisers changed music and provoked arguments about real musicianship, but a synthesiser does not learn its sound from other people's recordings. Sampling led to licensing systems for reusing recordings, but a sample is an identifiable piece of a specific recording, whereas a model's learning is usually diffuse. Photoshop and computer-generated imagery made fabricated images normal, but usually with a skilled person directing each change. Generative AI belongs in that history. It also differs in its dependence on vast training collections, its ability to supply substantial expressive detail from a short instruction, the sheer scale of output it makes possible, and its potential to substitute across several media at once. “People feared earlier tools too” is true. It does not tell us which rules and norms make sense now.

### So, Does It?

The responsible answer is not just “it depends how you use it”, because we now know enough to say *how* it depends. AI can lower barriers, speed up iteration and improve immediate results in some tasks, especially for less experienced people. It can also anchor users, narrow exploration and make a group's work more similar. The stage of the work, the person's expertise, whether they bring their own material, and their willingness to reject a polished suggestion all make a difference. The long-term effects on skill and on culture remain genuinely open.

## Myth vs Reality

**“AI just copies and pastes its training data.”** Training normally adjusts a vast set of numerical parameters rather than building a searchable scrapbook, and most generation is not a lookup. But memorisation happens, especially with duplicated material, so the opposite myth is also wrong.

**“AI never copies anything.”** Researchers have extracted memorised text and images from trained models. “Rarely, in specific conditions” is not the same as “never”.

**“AI is trained on everything on the internet.”** Training data varies by developer and model, and can include scraped web material, licensed collections, public-domain works, private datasets, user material and synthetic data. Ask which model, trained on what.

**“AI learns exactly like a human artist.”** The analogy correctly explains that pattern learning is not collage. It hides automated copying, industrial scale, commercial ownership, memorisation and market effects, which are the things the law and many creators care about most.

**“Anything made with AI is copyright-free.”** Copyright depends on the country and the facts, including the human contribution. Using AI does not erase protection for the human-authored parts of a work, and at least one country has a special rule for some computer-generated works.

**“If the provider says I own it, I own the copyright.”** A provider's contract sets out rights between you and the provider. It does not decide whether copyright exists, whether the result is exclusive, or whether someone else has a claim.

**“Commercial use allowed means it's safe.”** It means the provider will not object. Third-party rights, likeness, trade marks and platform rules still apply.

**“AI is replacing all artists”, or “AI can never replace real artists.”** Measured substitution is happening in some tasks and markets, alongside new work and changed work. Neither slogan describes that accurately, and lived experience does not prevent substitution where buyers accept a cheaper generic result.

**“AI makes people more creative”, or “AI kills creativity.”** Controlled studies show immediate gains in some tasks, alongside fixation and reduced variety across groups in others. Long-term effects are not settled.

**“You can always tell when something is AI-made.”** Modern outputs can be highly polished, and platforms now require disclosure precisely because viewers cannot reliably tell. Whether you can spot AI is also a separate question from whether the work is good, original, rights-safe or honest.

**“Content Credentials can tell whether any image was made by AI.”** They record signed claims about a file's history where participating tools preserve them. Missing credentials prove little, and credentials do not prove the depicted event is true.

## Core Takeaway

AI changes creative workflows more readily than it settles what creativity means. It can lower the cost of producing, transforming and testing material. It can make creative participation possible for people who were shut out, and help experienced creators explore or finish work. It can also shape the options people consider, fill in details nobody chose, create uncertainty about rights, and substitute for paid tasks.

Human judgement has not disappeared, but it does not sit safely at the end of an automated production line. It operates before, during and after generation, and the system can influence it. The practical responsibility is to know what you are trying to make, what the AI actually contributed, which skills you still want to practise, whose rights and identities are involved, what your audience expects, and whether the result deserves to be published.

## Part 2 Recap

Part 2 began with a conversation. Chapter 5 showed that AI becomes more useful when you give it the context, constraints and purpose that change the answer, and then inspect and refine what comes back. Chapter 6 took that home and asked a question that has followed us since: is the tool removing friction you do not value, or practice you do? Chapter 7 took the same habits to work, where confidentiality, policy, evidence and other people's reliance on your output raise the stakes.

Across those chapters, AI became more valuable each time you could tell the difference between a few pairs of things that look alike: generating something and judging it, assistance and authority, speed and genuine usefulness, a plausible answer and a verified one, what a tool *can* do and what it is appropriate to use it for.

Creative work brings all of those lessons together and adds one more. A clear brief still matters. Checking still matters. Effort and judgement still matter, even though in creative work they are woven together. What Chapter 8 adds is that creative usefulness does not settle questions of authorship, consent, originality, livelihood or meaning. Polish is not fitness for purpose. Commercial permission is not copyright. A better individual result is not a more diverse culture. And a tool that helps one person make something they never could have made can, in the same week, take paid work from someone else.

> **Recap:** AI can assist writing, images, music, video and design in genuinely useful ways, from breaking a blank page to transforming finished material. The further it moves towards supplying most of the expressive work, or generating at scale, the more questions of control, rights, disclosure and substitution matter. The evidence shows real gains, real substitution and real risks of anchoring and sameness, and the law differs from country to country. You do not need to inherit either the industry's optimism or its critics' conclusions. Before you settle on your own view, try a few questions on yourself. Which parts of your own creative process are valuable precisely because they take effort? Would your view of training change if creators had been asked, or paid? Does it matter to you whether AI assisted a person or generated most of the work? When would you want to be told how something was made?
