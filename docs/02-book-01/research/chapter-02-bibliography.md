# Chapter 2 Bibliography — Why Everyone Suddenly Talks About AI

## Purpose

This is the dedicated source record for Chapter 2. It maps the chapter's research-dependent claims to the material actually used and records the limits that should survive later editing. The manuscript uses unobtrusive chapter endnotes; this file preserves fuller notes for fact-checking, print revision, website adaptation, and eventual book-wide bibliography consolidation.

The chapter's argument and wording are the author's. No source was used as a template for reproducing prose.

## Research and Drafting Acknowledgement

Chapter 2 was developed from the author's viewpoint with research and drafting assistance from ChatGPT and Codex. Codex rechecked the authoritative or primary pages supporting the revised chapter on 23 September 2026. Company announcements were used only for their own launch dates, partnership terms, and stated limitations—not as independent evidence that a product is valuable, safe, or commercially successful.

## Sources Cited in the Chapter

### ChatGPT's public launch

OpenAI. “Introducing ChatGPT.” 30 November 2022. <https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/>.

- Source type: first-party product announcement.
- Used for: launch date, free research-preview framing, dialogue interface, and limitations disclosed at launch.
- Caution: authoritative about what OpenAI announced, not an independent measure of adoption or capability.

### Language-model generation

Jurafsky, Daniel, and James H. Martin. *Speech and Language Processing*. 3rd ed. online manuscript, 2026. <https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/>.

- Source type: university textbook and academic manuscript.
- Used for: the beginner-level explanation of tokens, learned language relationships, and sequential generation.
- Caution: the chapter deliberately simplifies the mechanism and does not claim that every model has the same training data or system design.

### The Dartmouth proposal

McCarthy, John, Marvin L. Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude E. Shannon. “A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence.” Dated 31 August 1955. *AI Magazine* 27, no. 4 (2006). <https://doi.org/10.1609/aimag.v27i4.1904>.

- Source type: reproduced primary historical document.
- Used for: the origin of the term *artificial intelligence*, the 1956 project, and the organisers' ambitious stated programme.
- Caution: the chapter characterises the proposal's confidence from its own wording without claiming that every participant shared one timetable or prediction.

### The first AI winter and the Lighthill report

UK Parliament, House of Lords Artificial Intelligence Committee. “AI in the UK: Ready, Willing and Able?” 2018, section “AI through the ages.” <https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld201719/ldselect/ldai/100/10018.htm>.

- Source type: parliamentary committee report drawing on historical evidence.
- Used for: the 1973 Lighthill report, reduced UK research support, and the later “AI winter” description.
- Caution: “AI winter” is a retrospective umbrella term. The chapter explicitly avoids saying all AI research stopped.

### Expert systems and late-1980s disappointment

Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency. “Artificial Intelligence.” In *DARPA: 60 Years 1958–2018*, 96–103. <https://www.darpa.mil/sites/default/files/attachment/2025-02/magazine-darpa-60th-anniversary.pdf>.

- Source type: institutional history from a major public research funder.
- Used for: expert systems' rule-based design, maintenance and brittleness limits, the commercial boom, and late-1980s disappointment.
- Caution: DARPA is an important participant in the history it recounts, not a detached academic historian. The chapter uses it for a narrow technical and funding account.

### Deep learning and transformers

Krizhevsky, Alex, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey E. Hinton. “ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks.” *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems* 25 (2012). <https://papers.nips.cc/paper/4824-imagenet-classification-with-deep-convolutional-networks>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed conference paper.
- Used for: the 2012 image-classification milestone.
- Caution: the paper is one important milestone, not the sole cause of the deep-learning resurgence.

Vaswani, Ashish, et al. “Attention Is All You Need.” arXiv:1706.03762, 2017. <https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762>.

- Source type: influential research preprint later presented at NeurIPS.
- Used for: the introduction of the transformer architecture and its original sequence-processing context.
- Caution: the chapter does not imply that this paper alone created modern chatbots.

### Confidently wrong generated content

Autio, Chloe, Reva Schwartz, Jesse Dunietz, Shomik Jain, Martin Stanley, Elham Tabassi, Patrick Hall, Kamie Roberts, et al. *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile*. NIST AI 600-1, 2024. <https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1>.

- Source type: United States standards and government technical guidance.
- Used for: “confabulation” as confidently presented erroneous or false generated content and as a structural generative-AI risk.
- Caution: NIST guidance describes and helps manage risk; it does not measure one universal error rate for all models and tasks.

### AI investment

Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence. “Economy.” *2025 AI Index Report*. <https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/economy>.

- Source type: university-led annual evidence synthesis drawing on multiple datasets.
- Used for: 2024 generative-AI private investment and total corporate AI investment.
- Caution: private investment, corporate investment, mergers and acquisitions, public offerings, revenue, and profit are separate categories. Figures are dated and should be refreshed before final print publication.

### Strategic cloud and model-company partnerships

Microsoft. “OpenAI Forms Exclusive Computing Partnership with Microsoft to Build New Azure AI Supercomputing Technologies.” 22 July 2019. <https://news.microsoft.com/source/2019/07/22/openai-forms-exclusive-computing-partnership-with-microsoft-to-build-new-azure-ai-supercomputing-technologies/>.

Microsoft. “Microsoft and OpenAI Extend Partnership.” 23 January 2023. <https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2023/01/23/microsoftandopenaiextendpartnership/>.

Amazon. “Amazon and Anthropic Deepen Their Shared Commitment to Advancing Generative AI.” 27 March 2024. <https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-anthropic-ai-investment>.

- Source type: first-party company announcements.
- Used for: disclosed investment amounts and the accompanying cloud-computing relationships.
- Caution: these sources establish what the parties publicly announced. They do not independently establish valuation, market demand, profit, or the detailed accounting treatment. The revised chapter therefore explains incentive alignment without alleging that disclosed partnerships are fraudulent or that all associated revenue is circular.

### Public adoption

Sidoti, Olivia, and Colleen McClain. “34% of U.S. Adults Have Used ChatGPT, About Double the Share in 2023.” Pew Research Center, 25 June 2025. <https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/06/25/34-of-us-adults-have-used-chatgpt-about-double-the-share-in-2023/>.

- Source type: independent, weighted survey of 5,123 US adults.
- Used for: overall US adult use in 2023 and 2025 and the large differences by age.
- Caution: this measures whether respondents had ever used ChatGPT, not frequency, paid use, competence, or worldwide adoption.

### Productivity evidence

Calvino, Flavio, Jelmer Reijerink, and Lea Samek. “The Effects of Generative AI on Productivity, Innovation and Entrepreneurship.” OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers, no. 39 (2025). <https://doi.org/10.1787/b21df222-en>.

- Source type: intergovernmental research review of experimental evidence.
- Used for: the balanced conclusion that generative AI has produced meaningful gains in particular tasks while effects vary by task and user and long-term business outcomes remain uncertain.
- Caution: controlled task performance should not be silently converted into economy-wide productivity, profit, or job-loss claims.

### Business adoption

Bonney, Kathryn, Cory Breaux, Catherine Buffington, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia Foster, Nathan Goldschlag, John Haltiwanger, Zachary Kroff, and Keith Savage. “Tracking Firm Use of AI in Real Time: A Snapshot from the Business Trends and Outlook Survey.” US Census Bureau Center for Economic Studies Working Paper CES-24-16, 2024. <https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/adrm/CES-WP-24-16.html>.

- Source type: government working paper using a nationally representative US business survey.
- Used for: the rise from 3.7 to 5.4 per cent of businesses reporting AI use from September 2023 to February 2024, variation across firms and sectors, and few reported employment reductions among users at that stage.
- Caution: the survey measured AI use in producing goods or services under its then-current wording. Later surveys changed the question, so rates from different versions should not be joined as though they measured exactly the same thing.

## Claims Removed During the Evidence Audit

The revision deliberately removed or replaced the following claims from the earlier draft because the available sourcing was too weak, too ambiguous, or too easy to misread:

- the comparison claiming ChatGPT reached 100 million users faster than TikTok and Instagram;
- the claim that only about 3 per cent of people pay for AI products;
- a sector-wide comparison of less than $50 billion in AI revenue against more than $1 trillion in cumulative investment;
- the quoted prediction that current AI investment forms a “house of cards”;
- specific late-2025 Microsoft, Nvidia, Anthropic, and OpenAI deal figures drawn from secondary reporting;
- the implication that strategic investment-and-cloud partnerships automatically create fake or circular revenue;
- the idea that earlier internet and smartphone shifts merely delivered existing human work faster.

These removals are part of the chapter's argument: a claim does not become suitable for a beginner-facing book merely because it is vivid, widely repeated, or directionally plausible.
