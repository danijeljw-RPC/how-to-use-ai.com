# Chapter 3 Bibliography — What AI Can Actually Do

## Purpose

This is the dedicated source record for Chapter 3. It records the material consulted and actually used to support the chapter's factual claims, examples, and editorial framing. The manuscript uses unobtrusive chapter endnotes; this file preserves fuller editorial notes for later bibliography consolidation, fact-checking, website adaptation, and revision.

## Sources Cited in the Chapter

### Writing productivity

Noy, Shakked, and Whitney Zhang. “Experimental Evidence on the Productivity Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence.” *Science* 381, no. 6654 (2023): 187–192. <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed, preregistered experiment.
- Used for: evidence that AI assistance improved speed and assessed quality in a specific set of professional writing tasks.
- Caution: the reported effects apply to the experiment's population and tasks, not to writing in general.

### Creativity and idea diversity

Lee, Byung Cheol, and Jaeyeon Chung. “An Empirical Investigation of the Impact of ChatGPT on Creativity.” *Nature Human Behaviour* (2024). <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01953-1>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed multi-experiment study.
- Used for: evidence that AI assistance can improve rated creativity in some ideation tasks.

Meincke, Lennart, Gideon Nave, and Christian Terwiesch. “ChatGPT Decreases Idea Diversity in Brainstorming.” *Nature Human Behaviour* 9 (2025): 1107–1109. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02173-x>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed Matters Arising analysis.
- Used for: the counterpoint that stronger individual ideas can coincide with less diversity across a group of ideas.
- Caution: neither study defines creativity in its entirety; both examine particular tasks and measures.

### Coding productivity

Becker, Joel, Nate Rush, Beth Barnes, and David Rein. “Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity.” METR, 2025. <https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/>.

- Source type: randomised controlled trial with experienced open-source developers.
- Used for: a concrete example of AI assistance producing a slowdown in one realistic development setting despite participants perceiving a speed-up.
- Caution: the authors explicitly warn against generalising the result to all developers, tools, repositories, or programming work.

### Translation

Yan, Jianhao, et al. “GPT-4 vs. Human Translators.” arXiv, 2024. <https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03658>.

- Source type: research preprint.
- Used for: evidence that translation quality varies with language, domain, and translator expertise.

Zhu, Wenhao, et al. “Multilingual Machine Translation with Large Language Models.” *Findings of NAACL 2024*. <https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-naacl.176/>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed conference paper.
- Used for: multilingual and lower-resource-language variation.

### AI tutoring

Kestin, Greg, et al. “AI Tutoring Outperforms In-Class Active Learning: An RCT Introducing a Novel Research-Based Design in an Authentic Educational Setting.” *Scientific Reports* 15 (2025): article 17458. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed randomised controlled trial.
- Used for: evidence that a deliberately designed, pedagogy-informed AI tutor produced strong learning results in a Harvard undergraduate physics course.
- Caution: the tutor was structured around course material and teaching practice; the result does not establish that a generic chatbot is better than teachers.

### Jagged capability

Dell'Acqua, Fabrizio, et al. “Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier.” Harvard Business School AI Institute, 2023. <https://aiinstitute.hbs.edu/navigating-the-jagged-technological-frontier/>.

- Source type: field experiment overview; the research was later formally published.
- Used for: the concept of a jagged technological frontier and the contrast between gains inside the frontier and poorer outcomes outside it.
- Caution: the chapter uses the durable concept rather than presenting the experiment's percentages as universal productivity figures.

### Language-model generation

Jurafsky, Daniel, and James H. Martin. *Speech and Language Processing: An Introduction to Natural Language Processing, Computational Linguistics, and Speech Recognition with Language Models*. 3rd ed. Online manuscript released 19 August 2026. <https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/>.

- Source type: university textbook and continuously updated academic manuscript.
- Used for: the explanation of token-by-token language generation, probability distributions, and selection among plausible continuations.
- Caution: the chapter simplifies the mechanism for beginners without suggesting that a model selects among complete pre-written answers.

### Confabulation and confident error

Autio, Chloe, Reva Schwartz, Jesse Dunietz, Shomik Jain, Martin Stanley, Elham Tabassi, Patrick Hall, and Kamie Roberts. *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile*. NIST AI 600-1, 2024. <https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1>.

- Source type: United States standards and government technical guidance.
- Used for: authoritative framing of confabulation as confidently presented erroneous or false generated content and for avoiding anthropomorphic explanations.

Kalai, Adam Tauman, et al. “Why Language Models Hallucinate.” OpenAI, 2025. <https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/>.

- Source type: industry research explanation linked to a technical paper.
- Used for: the explanation that evaluation systems can reward guessing over appropriate abstention.
- Caution: corroborated against NIST framing; not treated as an independent standards source.

### Critical thinking and human judgement

Lee, Hao-Ping, Advait Sarkar, Lev Tankelevitch, Ian Drosos, Sean Rintel, Richard Banks, and Nicholas Wilson. “The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking: Self-Reported Reductions in Cognitive Effort and Confidence Effects From a Survey of Knowledge Workers.” *Proceedings of CHI 2025*. <https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778>.

- Source type: peer-reviewed survey study based on 319 knowledge workers and 936 first-hand examples.
- Used for: the finding that AI-assisted critical thinking can shift towards verification, integration, and stewardship, and that confidence in AI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking.
- Caution: the study reports associations and self-reported behaviour, not a direct measure of long-term cognitive decline.
